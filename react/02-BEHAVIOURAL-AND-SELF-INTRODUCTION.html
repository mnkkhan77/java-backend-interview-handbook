02 - BEHAVIOURAL & SELF-INTRODUCTION

Target Audience: Frontend / React Engineer (3–6 Years)
Goal: Not just recite rehearsed stories, but tell them with structure, honesty, and enough frontend-specific texture to survive follow-ups from an experienced interviewer.

The behavioural round is where offers are quietly won and lost. Two candidates with identical technical scores routinely split on this round, because the panel is answering a different question here: not "can this person code" but "do I want to sit next to this person for the next three years, and can I trust their account of what they did?" For a frontend engineer the stories have a particular flavour — you collaborate constantly with design, product, and backend, you live in the messy middle where requirements change, and your bugs are often the uniquely nasty kind (races, leaks, hydration mismatches) that only show up in the browser. This chapter is almost entirely prose. It teaches a structure, then works through the questions you will actually be asked, with adaptable sample answers.

Our persona — "Aarav": ~4 years of experience, currently a Frontend Engineer at a mid-size B2B SaaS company. Owns the customer-facing analytics dashboard (React 18 + TypeScript, Vite, TanStack Query, Redux Toolkit for a small amount of global state, Tailwind, Playwright for e2e). Previously worked on an e-commerce storefront (Next.js). Known on the team for driving a large performance clean-up, building a shared accessible component library, and mentoring two junior engineers. We reuse Aarav across the whole handbook so the stories stay consistent — adapt the specifics to your own history, but keep the structure.

SECTION 1 - THE STAR METHOD

Q1. What is the STAR method, and how do you actually use it?

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: Extremely High (the framework behind every other answer)

Short Answer

STAR is a four-part structure for answering "tell me about a time…" questions: Situation (the context), Task (what you specifically needed to do), Action (what you did — the bulk of the answer), and Result (the measurable outcome and what you learned). It stops you rambling and keeps the focus on your contribution.

Detailed Answer

Behavioural questions are all variations of "give me evidence, not adjectives." Anyone can claim to be collaborative or detail-oriented; STAR forces you to prove it with a specific past event. The four beats, and the mistakes to avoid in each:

BeatWhat to coverCommon mistake
SituationSet the scene in 1–2 sentences: the product, the stakes, the constraint.Spending two minutes on backstory before you get to the point.
TaskWhat you were responsible for — your specific goal or problem.Describing the team's job instead of your slice of it.
ActionThe concrete steps you took, the trade-offs you weighed. This is 60–70% of the answer.Saying "we" the whole time so the interviewer can't tell what you did.
ResultThe outcome with a number if possible, plus the lesson learned.Trailing off with no outcome, or claiming a win you can't quantify.

The single most important discipline is the "I" versus "we" balance. Use "we" to set the collaborative scene, then switch deliberately to "I" the moment you describe actions. Interviewers are explicitly listening for your personal contribution; if every sentence is "we," they cannot score you.

A worked example

Take the question "Tell me about a time you had to deliver under pressure." Here is Aarav's answer, annotated so you can see the machinery:

[Situation] "Last year we committed to launching a new billing dashboard for a major enterprise customer ahead of their fiscal year-end. Two weeks before the deadline, the backend team told us the usage-aggregation endpoint would slip by a week.

[Task] I owned the frontend for that dashboard, and I needed to keep us on track for the launch date even though the data I depended on wouldn't be ready until the last few days.

[Action] First, I sat down with the backend engineer and we locked the API contract — the exact response shape, pagination, and error format — even though the endpoint itself wasn't built. Then I built the entire dashboard against a typed mock that matched that contract, so my UI work wasn't blocked. I deliberately sequenced the work so the risky, integration-heavy parts were ready first and the polish was last, and I flagged to product that if we ran out of time, the CSV-export feature would be the thing we cut, not correctness. When the real endpoint landed, integration took about a day because the contract already matched.

[Result] We shipped on the original date. We did drop the CSV export to a fast-follow, exactly as I'd flagged, so there were no surprises. The bigger takeaway for me was how much a locked API contract plus mocks de-risks a deadline — I now do that on every cross-team feature by default."

Notice the properties of a good STAR answer: the Situation is two sentences, the Action is the longest part and is full of specific verbs ("locked the contract," "built against a typed mock," "sequenced the risky parts first"), and the Result has both an outcome and a durable lesson. It is also honest — it names what was cut rather than pretending everything shipped perfectly.

How to prepare

You do not need a story for every possible question. You need five or six strong stories that you can flex to answer many questions. Aarav's core kit is: the performance clean-up, the nasty race-condition bug, the disagreement with design over an animation, the deadline story above, and the component-library initiative. Between them they cover leadership, conflict, failure, technical depth, and delivery. Write each one out as four bullets (S/T/A/R), with one hard number in the Result, and rehearse it out loud until it runs 2–3 minutes.

Senior Engineer Perspective

Experienced interviewers can hear STAR being used, and that's fine — it signals preparation and clear thinking, not rehearsal. What they probe for is whether the story survives depth. If you say "I built it against a typed mock," expect "how did you keep the mock in sync with the real API so it didn't drift?" So pick stories where you genuinely did the work and can go three questions deep. The other senior signal is a real, reflective Result — the sentence "here's what I'd do differently now" turns a war story into evidence of growth. Candidates who only tell triumph stories with no self-critique read as junior; the ability to narrate your own mistakes calmly is a maturity marker.

SECTION 2 - COMMON BEHAVIOURAL QUESTIONS

Q2. Tell me about yourself.

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: Extremely High

Short Answer

Give a 90-second arc: who you are today and your core stack, one flagship thing you own with a concrete impact, and what you're looking for next — ending on a hook that maps to this role. In a behavioural round, lean slightly more on how you work with people than on the technology, because that's what the round is testing.

Detailed Answer

This question opens almost every interview, and the behavioural-round version differs subtly from the recruiter-screen version. In the recruiter screen you're setting the technical agenda; here you're planting behavioural hooks — ownership, collaboration, mentorship — that invite the stories the panel wants to dig into. The structure is still four beats: positioning, core stack, flagship ownership, forward-looking hook. Aarav's behavioural-flavoured version:

"I'm a frontend engineer with about four years of experience, focused on React and TypeScript. For the last two years I've owned our customer-facing analytics dashboard — it's the product our enterprise customers open every morning, so it has to stay fast and reliable under heavy, data-dense screens.

Two things I'm known for on the team beyond shipping features. First, I led a performance clean-up that took our worst view from visibly janky to smooth. Second, and honestly the part I care about more now, I built our shared accessible component library and I mentor two junior engineers — I've come to enjoy the leverage of making the whole team faster, not just my own output. I work closely with design and backend; I like getting into a feature early so I can flag edge cases before they become rework.

I'm looking for a role with bigger frontend scope and a strong engineering culture, ideally somewhere I can keep growing into technical leadership — which is what stood out to me about this position."

The hooks planted here are deliberately behavioural: "mentor two junior engineers," "work closely with design," "flag edge cases early," "growing into technical leadership." Each is an invitation for a story you've already prepared.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The panel is checking whether you can communicate crisply and whether your self-narrative matches the level they're hiring for. A junior recites tasks; a mid-level engineer talks about ownership; a senior talks about leverage and impact on others. Keep it under two minutes — a rambling five-minute life story is the single most common failure. And make sure the hooks you plant are ones you actually want to be asked about; every noun in this answer is a thread the interviewer may pull, so don't advertise a story you can't tell well.

Q3. What is your greatest strength, and your biggest weakness?

ROI: ★★★★☆ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Pick a strength relevant to the role and back it with a one-line proof. For the weakness, name a real one that isn't a core-competency killer, then show the concrete corrective action you're taking. Avoid the humblebrag ("I care too much") and avoid the disqualifier ("I don't write tests").

Detailed Answer

Strength. Tie it to what makes you valuable on a frontend team, and prove it with a single concrete example rather than an adjective:

"My strongest area is rendering performance — I genuinely enjoy profiling and I understand why React re-renders, which is how I led the dashboard clean-up. The related strength is that I care about the invisible states, empty, loading, error, that most people skip; it's saved us a lot of production embarrassment."

Weakness. The formula that works is: a genuine gap, framed in past-improving-to-present tense, with a specific corrective action and ideally a positive side-effect. Aarav:

"My weakness has been delegation. Because I know the dashboard so well, my instinct when something's urgent is to just fix it myself, which doesn't scale and doesn't grow the juniors I mentor. I've been deliberately working on it — I now hand off tasks I could do faster myself, pair on them instead of taking over, and I write up the context so someone else can own it. It's slower in the moment but the team's gotten noticeably more capable, and honestly it's made me a better mentor."

That weakness works because it's real, it's the kind of problem good engineers actually have, and the fix demonstrates exactly the leadership maturity a senior role wants.

Senior Engineer Perspective

Everyone knows the weakness question is a game; what's scored is self-awareness and honesty. A canned "I'm a perfectionist" signals you're either not introspective or not candid. A disqualifying weakness for the specific role ("I struggle with CSS" for a UI-heavy job) is a fast no. The sweet spot is a true growth edge adjacent to, but not central to, the job, plus evidence you're closing the gap without a manager forcing you. Beware the follow-up "and what are you still bad at?" — have a second, smaller genuine answer so you don't look like you've only manufactured one safe response.

Q4. Tell me about the most challenging bug you've fixed.

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Pick a genuinely hard, frontend-specific bug — a memory leak, a race condition, a hydration mismatch, a stale-closure state bug — and narrate it as a detective story: symptom, how you reproduced it, how you isolated the root cause, the fix, and the safeguard you added so it can't recur. The debugging method matters as much as the bug.

Detailed Answer

This question is a technical-depth probe wearing a behavioural costume. The interviewer wants to see how you think when something is genuinely broken and non-obvious. Choose a bug that is unmistakably an engineering problem, not "a tricky CSS thing," and one you can defend three follow-ups deep. Aarav's is a classic frontend race condition combined with a memory leak:

[Situation] "On the dashboard, users would sometimes see the wrong customer's data flash into a panel for a moment when they switched accounts quickly. It was intermittent, only happened on slower connections, and QA couldn't reliably reproduce it, so it had been sitting in the backlog as 'can't reproduce.'

[Task] I picked it up because it was a correctness bug — showing account A's numbers under account B's header is a trust and potentially a compliance problem, not a cosmetic one.

[Action] First I made it reproducible by throttling the network in DevTools and rapidly switching accounts, which turned an intermittent bug into a reliable one — that's usually the hardest and most important step. Then I traced it. The panel fired a fetch in a useEffect keyed on the account id, but if you switched again before the first request resolved, the older, slower response could arrive last and overwrite the newer one — a classic out-of-order race. On top of that, the effect wasn't cleaning up, so unmounted panels were still holding subscriptions, which was slowly leaking memory over a long session.

The fix was two-fold. I added an AbortController and an 'ignore stale response' guard in the effect cleanup so a superseded request could never commit its result, and I made sure every subscription was torn down in the cleanup function. Longer term I migrated that panel onto TanStack Query, which handles request cancellation and caching correctly by design, so we couldn't reintroduce the class of bug by hand.

[Result] The wrong-data flash disappeared, and a heap snapshot over a long session that used to climb steadily went flat. I added a Playwright test that rapidly toggles accounts to lock the regression. The lesson that stuck with me is how many 'flaky' frontend bugs are actually races hiding behind async effects, and that the first job is always to make the bug deterministic before touching any code."

The illustrative shape of the fix — cancel the stale request, and never commit a superseded response — is worth being able to sketch if asked:

useEffect(() => {
  const controller = new AbortController();
  let ignore = false;

  fetch(`/api/accounts/${accountId}/metrics`, { signal: controller.signal })
    .then((res) => res.json())
    .then((data) => {
      // Guard: a newer effect run may have superseded this one.
      if (!ignore) setMetrics(data);
    })
    .catch((err) => {
      if (err.name !== "AbortError") setError(err);
    });

  // Cleanup runs before the next effect and on unmount:
  // abort the in-flight request and block any late resolution.
  return () => {
    ignore = true;
    controller.abort();
  };
}, [accountId]);

Senior Engineer Perspective

The bug you pick is the follow-up territory, so choose one you fully own. If you mention a race condition, expect "why does the older response win?" and "why doesn't React protect you here?" — you must be able to explain that effects don't cancel their async work automatically and that closures capture the value at the time they ran. The strongest signal in this whole answer is "I made it reproducible first": junior engineers thrash by changing code and re-testing, while seniors invest in a deterministic repro before touching anything. The second strongest is the safeguard — a test or a structural change (moving to a library that makes the bug impossible) shows you fix classes of bugs, not just instances.

Q5. Tell me about a time you improved performance or UX.

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Use the structure Metric → Diagnosis → Fix → Result. State the symptom, how you measured it, the root cause, the specific fix, and the measured improvement. Never say "I made it faster" without a number or a method — vagueness here reads as "I wasn't really hands-on."

Detailed Answer

Performance and UX wins are interviewer favourites because they're tangible and they expose whether you understand rendering and the browser, not just JSX. Aarav's version, told in STAR but driven by the metric structure:

[Situation/Task] "Our biggest dashboard view had become janky — typing in the filter box would freeze the UI for a beat, and we were getting support tickets that the dashboard 'hangs.' I took it on as a focused performance project.

[Action] I refused to guess, so I profiled with the React DevTools Profiler and the browser performance panel first. The trace showed every keystroke re-rendering the entire table of several thousand rows, because the filtered data was recomputed inline and the rows weren't memoised. I fixed it in layers, verifying each on the profiler: memoised the derived data with useMemo, wrapped the row in React.memo so unchanged rows skipped re-render, debounced the filter input, and then virtualised the list so only the visible window was in the DOM. As a separate win I code-split the charting library out of the initial bundle.

[Result] Input latency in that view went from clearly perceptible jank to smooth, first-load JavaScript shrank substantially, and the 'hangs' tickets stopped. I saved the before/after profiler traces so the improvement was apples-to-apples, not a vibe."

Every noun here is a defensible follow-up hook: useMemo, React.memo, debounce, virtualisation, code-splitting, profiling. If your best story is a UX win rather than raw speed — say, cutting a six-step form to three and watching completion rate climb — use the identical structure: the metric you moved, how you knew (analytics funnel), what you changed, the result.

Senior Engineer Perspective

This quietly checks whether you understand why React re-renders and how the browser paints. The senior nuance is restraint: memoisation isn't free, premature memoisation is an anti-pattern, so "profile first, then memoise the proven hotspot" is the mature line. Expect "when would adding React.memo make things worse?" and "what did you measure it with?" The candidates who stand out treat performance as a measured engineering discipline, not a folklore of tips — the before/after trace is what converts a claim into evidence.

Q6. Tell me about a project that failed, and what you learned.

ROI: ★★★★☆ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Pick a real failure with genuine stakes, own your specific part in it without either scapegoating others or falling on your sword for everything, and spend most of the answer on the concrete change you made afterwards. The failure is the setup; the lesson-applied is the payload.

Detailed Answer

Interviewers ask this to see whether you can be honest about mistakes and, crucially, whether you convert them into changed behaviour. The trap is picking a fake failure ("we shipped a week late but it was great") — it reads as evasive. Pick something that actually stung. Aarav:

[Situation/Task] "We rebuilt the reporting section as a big-bang rewrite — new component architecture, new state approach, everything at once, behind a flag we flipped for all customers on one day. I was one of the two engineers driving it and I was a strong advocate for doing it all in one go to 'avoid maintaining two versions.'

[Action/what went wrong] Launch day surfaced a wave of edge cases we hadn't hit internally — unusual data shapes from long-tenured accounts, a couple of accessibility regressions, a printing layout that broke. Because everything shipped at once, we couldn't tell which change caused what, and we ended up rolling the whole thing back, which was demoralising for the team and eroded some trust with support.

[Result/lesson] The honest lesson was that my instinct to avoid dual-maintenance cost us far more than incremental delivery would have. When we did it again, I pushed for the opposite approach: ship the rewrite screen by screen behind per-view flags, dogfood each with a few friendly accounts first, and keep the old path until the new one was proven. That second attempt shipped without a rollback. What I took from it is a real bias now toward incremental, reversible delivery and toward testing against real, weird production data early — not just clean fixtures."

This works because Aarav names his own decision as the root cause ("I was a strong advocate for one go"), doesn't blame teammates, and the payload is a specific, durable change in how he ships.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The failure question is really a growth-and-accountability test. Two failure modes: the candidate who can't name a real failure (reads as no self-awareness), and the one who blames everyone else (reads as someone who'll never own an incident). The strongest answers take proportionate ownership — you don't have to claim the whole disaster, just your honest share — and demonstrate the lesson was actually applied later, not just noted. A great follow-up is "how do you know you really changed?" so having the second, successful attempt in the story is what seals it.

Q7. How do you prioritise when you're under a hard deadline?

ROI: ★★★★☆ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Show a system, not heroics. Separate must-haves from nice-to-haves with product early, protect correctness and the critical path, sequence the riskiest integration work first, and communicate trade-offs before they become surprises. Overtime is the last lever, not the first.

Detailed Answer

Interviewers want to know you make deliberate trade-offs rather than either panicking or quietly cutting corners on quality. The framework to articulate:

  • Clarify the real deadline and the real scope. Which parts are genuinely immovable, and what's the actual minimum that delivers the value?
  • Cut scope, not quality. It's almost always safer to ship fewer features that work than all features half-broken. Agree with product up front what gets dropped if time runs out.
  • Sequence by risk. Do the uncertain, integration-heavy work first so surprises surface early; leave polish for last because it's the safest thing to cut.
  • Communicate early and often. A deadline miss you flag a week out is a planning conversation; the same miss revealed on launch day is a crisis.

Aarav's short illustrative answer reuses the billing-dashboard deadline from Q1 — the point being that one well-prepared story can serve multiple questions:

"I start by pinning down what 'done' really means with product, and I explicitly agree what we'd cut first if we run short — for that billing launch it was the CSV export. Then I front-load the risky work; I built against a locked API mock so backend slippage couldn't block me. I only reach for extra hours as a last resort, because tired late-night frontend code is exactly how you ship the race conditions we talked about. The habit that matters most is telling stakeholders about a risk the moment I see it, not the day it lands."

Senior Engineer Perspective

This is a signal for judgement and communication under stress. The red flag interviewers listen for is the hero who "just pulls all-nighters" — it doesn't scale, it burns people out, and it hides planning problems. The green flag is proactive scope negotiation and early communication. A sharp follow-up is "what if product refuses to cut anything?" — the mature answer is that you present the trade-off honestly with options and let them make the call with full information, rather than silently absorbing the risk and shipping something broken.

SECTION 3 - FRONTEND TEAM SCENARIOS

Q8. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a designer, PM, or backend engineer.

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: Very High

Short Answer

Pick a disagreement you handled well: you brought data or a concrete alternative rather than an opinion, you sought to understand their goal first, and you reached an outcome you both accepted — ideally one where you were willing to be convinced too. Show spine and collaboration.

Detailed Answer

Frontend engineers live at the intersection of design, product, and backend, so this question is almost guaranteed. The interviewer is checking that you can hold a position without being difficult, and change your mind without being a pushover. The key is to frame the disagreement around a shared goal (the user, performance, the deadline) rather than personal preference. Aarav's design example:

[Situation/Task] "A designer wanted an elaborate animated transition on the main dashboard load — cards flying in and staggering. I was worried it would hurt the perceived performance of the most-used screen, which is exactly the surface I'd spent months making feel fast.

[Action] Instead of just saying 'that'll be slow,' I first made sure I understood what they were going for — they wanted the dashboard to feel polished and premium, not slow. So we were actually aligned on the goal. I prototyped both: the full animation and a much subtler fade-and-settle. I measured them and showed that the heavy version delayed time-to-interactive noticeably on mid-range laptops, and I put both in front of a couple of users. The subtle version tested as feeling more premium, because 'premium' to users meant 'instant,' not 'flashy.'

[Result] We went with the subtle transition. The designer was genuinely on board because it served their actual goal, and it became a bit of a principle on the team that we prototype-and-measure interaction decisions instead of arguing about them. I've also lost these — I've had a designer show me an accessibility detail I was wrong about — and I try to disagree the same way whichever side I'm on."

The two things that make this land: Aarav sought the other person's goal first, and he brought evidence (a prototype and a measurement) rather than an assertion. The closing admission that he's also been wrong is what makes it read as collaboration rather than "I won."

Senior Engineer Perspective

This is one of the highest-signal behavioural questions for a frontend role. The disqualifying answers are the two extremes: the engineer who steamrolls partners ("I told them it was a bad idea and refused to build it") and the doormat who never pushes back ("I just build whatever design hands me"). Both are liabilities. What's wanted is disagree and commit with evidence. The detail that impresses is reframing the disagreement around a shared objective — once it's "how do we both get the user a premium, fast dashboard" rather than "my taste vs yours," the conflict dissolves. And always include a case where you were the one who updated your view; it proves you argue to find the right answer, not to win.

Q9. How do you handle critical code-review feedback?

ROI: ★★★★☆ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Treat review as being about the code, not about you. Assume good intent, ask for the reasoning behind comments you don't understand, act on the valid ones gracefully, and discuss (don't silently ignore) the ones you disagree with. Also show the flip side: how you give kind, useful reviews.

Detailed Answer

How someone reacts to review feedback tells an interviewer whether they'll be a joy or a friction point on the team. The mature posture has a few parts:

  • Depersonalise it. A comment on my PR is feedback on the code, not a verdict on me. Separating ego from output is the whole skill.
  • Seek the reasoning. If I don't understand a comment, I ask "what problem are you seeing?" rather than getting defensive — often I learn something, occasionally I surface a misunderstanding on their side.
  • Disagree openly, then commit. If I disagree, I say so with a reason; if we can't converge in the thread, we hop on a quick call, and if it's genuinely a matter of taste I defer to conventions and move on. What I never do is silently ignore comments.
  • Give the review I'd want. When I review others, I'm explicit about what's a blocker vs a nit, I explain why, and I praise good work, especially for the juniors I mentor.

A brief real anecdote beats platitudes:

"Early on I took review comments personally and got quietly defensive. What changed me was a senior who reviewed my code by asking questions instead of issuing orders — 'what happens here if the array is empty?' — and I realised the comments were making my code better and teaching me. Now I ask for the tough review, and I try to review others the same way: questions and reasons, not commands."

Senior Engineer Perspective

The interviewer is screening for defensiveness, which poisons teams. Red flags are "I usually know best" or any hint that feedback is an attack. The strongest candidates naturally talk about both sides — receiving and giving — because that shows they think about review as a team health practice, not a gate to survive. A good follow-up is "tell me about a review comment you disagreed with and how it resolved," so have one where you pushed back respectfully and either changed a mind or committed gracefully after losing the argument.

Q10. How do you deal with tech debt?

ROI: ★★★★☆ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Treat tech debt as an engineering trade-off, not a moral failing: make it visible, quantify its cost in terms the business understands (velocity, bugs, risk), and pay it down incrementally — often opportunistically alongside feature work — rather than lobbying for a big stop-the-world refactor. Know the difference between deliberate, prudent debt and just-messy code.

Detailed Answer

This question separates engineers who whine about the codebase from those who manage it like adults. The framing that impresses:

  • Make it visible and measurable. "The old form components have no types and cause about a bug a sprint" is a business case; "the code is ugly" is a complaint. Tie debt to velocity, defect rate, onboarding time, or incident risk.
  • Prioritise by pain, not by aesthetics. Pay down the debt that's actively hurting the team's most-touched areas; leave the ugly-but-stable corner alone.
  • Pay it incrementally. The "boy-scout rule" — leave each file a little better than you found it — plus budgeting a slice of each sprint beats begging for a two-month refactor that never gets approved.
  • Prevent new debt. Types, lint rules, and a shared component library stop whole categories of debt from re-accumulating.

"On the dashboard we had a pile of untyped legacy components that generated a steady trickle of avoidable bugs. Rather than pitch a big rewrite, which never gets funded, I tracked how many of our bugs traced back to those components, showed that number to my lead, and we agreed to convert them opportunistically — whenever anyone touched one for a feature, they'd migrate it to the typed shared library first. Within a couple of quarters most of the hot ones were gone and that class of bug basically stopped, without ever taking a dedicated 'refactor sprint.'"

Senior Engineer Perspective

Interviewers use this to gauge pragmatism and business awareness. The junior answer is "we should stop everything and rewrite it"; the senior answer treats debt as a managed, prioritised backlog with a cost attached. The concept to name is that not all debt is bad — sometimes shipping quick and dirty to hit a market window is the right call, as long as it's a conscious, tracked decision rather than an accident. Bonus points for talking about preventing debt with types, lint, and shared components, which shows you think upstream.

Q11. Tell me about a time you mentored or led others.

ROI: ★★★★☆ Frequency: High (and rising with seniority)

Short Answer

Show leverage, not just help: describe how you grew someone's capability or unblocked a team outcome, with a concrete before/after. Leadership without a title — driving an initiative, setting a standard, mentoring a junior to independence — is exactly what earns a senior title.

Detailed Answer

Even for non-lead roles, interviewers want to see influence beyond your own keyboard, because that's what distinguishes a senior IC. Frame mentorship around the other person's growth (their outcome, not your generosity) and lead-by-initiative around a standard you raised for the team. Aarav:

[Situation/Task] "A junior joined our team straight out of a bootcamp, strong on building screens but shaky on the harder React concepts — effects, memoisation, why things re-render. My job as their informal mentor was to get them to the point of shipping non-trivial features independently.

[Action] I did a few deliberate things rather than just answering questions ad hoc. We paired weekly on a real ticket, and I made a point of asking questions instead of taking the keyboard — 'what do you think triggers this re-render?' — so they built the mental model themselves. I steered them toward progressively harder tasks, and I reviewed their PRs with reasons and praise, not just fixes. When they hit the classic stale-closure bug, I let them debug it with hints rather than handing them the answer.

[Result] Within a couple of quarters they were owning features end to end, including a chunk of the component library, and they've since started mentoring the next new hire. That's the outcome I actually care about — the mentorship compounding. It also taught me that leading is mostly about creating the conditions for someone to figure it out, not demonstrating how much I know."

If you don't have direct-mentorship examples, lead-by-initiative works just as well: driving the component library, introducing the e2e test suite, or establishing the accessibility review conventions are all leadership without a title.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The signal here is whether you multiply the people around you or just produce your own output. Q12. How do you handle conflicting requirements?

ROI: ★★★★☆ Frequency: Medium-High

Short Answer

Surface the conflict explicitly rather than silently guessing, trace each requirement back to its underlying goal (they often reconcile at the goal level), get the right people in one conversation to decide, and document what was agreed. Don't be the engineer who quietly picks one side and hopes.

Detailed Answer

Conflicting requirements are a daily frontend reality — design wants richness, product wants speed-to-ship, and an accessibility or performance constraint wants simplicity, all on the same screen. The interviewer is checking whether you resolve conflicts openly or paper over them. The approach:

  • Name the conflict out loud. The worst move is to silently implement one interpretation and discover at review that you guessed wrong.
  • Get to the "why" behind each ask. Requirements that look contradictory on the surface often share a goal; once you find it, a third option frequently satisfies both.
  • Bring the stakeholders together to decide. If it truly can't be reconciled, it's a prioritisation call for product/design to make with full information — my job is to lay out the trade-offs and options clearly, not to make the business decision unilaterally.
  • Write down the decision. A one-line note in the ticket prevents the same argument re-litigating itself in a month.

"On a signup flow, product wanted the fewest possible fields to maximise conversion, while a compliance requirement demanded several disclosures, and design wanted it all on one clean screen. Rather than pick one, I laid out the tension in a short thread and got product, design, and our compliance contact into a 20-minute call. Once we dug into the goals, the answer was progressive disclosure — a minimal first step for conversion, with the required disclosures surfaced at the right, legally-valid moment. Everyone's actual goal was met; I just had to make the conflict visible so the right people could resolve it together."

Senior Engineer Perspective

This tests communication and whether you understand your lane. The failure mode is an engineer who makes product decisions by fiat inside the code because it was faster than having a conversation — that erodes trust and produces the wrong product. The other failure mode is escalating trivial conflicts you could have resolved yourself. The balance interviewers want: resolve what you can with the goal-behind-the-ask technique, escalate genuine business trade-offs with clear options, and always document the outcome. Naming "progressive disclosure" or a similar concrete resolution shows you don't just facilitate, you propose solutions.

Q15. Salary negotiation basics.

ROI: ★★★★☆ Frequency: Extremely High (at the offer stage)

Short Answer

Research the market first, avoid naming a number before you have to, give a researched range anchored to your value when pushed, negotiate on the total package rather than base alone, and stay collaborative — never combative or dishonest. The offer stage is a negotiation, not a favour, and politely negotiating is expected.

Detailed Answer

Negotiation makes many engineers anxious, but a few principles cover most situations:

  • Do the research before you talk numbers. Use levelling/market data, local benchmarks, and your current comp plus a reasonable step. You negotiate from knowledge, not hope.
  • Let them go first if you can. When asked early for a number, it's fine to deflect: "I'd like to understand the role and level first, but I'm targeting a range in line with the market for a senior React engineer here." If pushed, give a range whose bottom you'd genuinely accept — interviewers anchor low.
  • Anchor to value, and get the offer in hand first. The most leverage you'll ever have is after they've decided they want you and before you've said yes. That's the moment to negotiate, calmly.
  • Negotiate the whole package. Base, equity, bonus, sign-on, level, remote flexibility, learning budget — if base is capped, other levers often aren't.
  • Never lie. Don't invent a competing offer or misstate your current salary; it's easy to get caught and it destroys the trust you just built.

"Based on my research for a frontend engineer at my level in this market, I'm targeting roughly X to Y. I'm flexible depending on the overall package — level, equity, and growth matter to me as much as base — and I'd want to understand the full compensation structure before landing on a number. I'm excited about the role, so I'm confident we can find something that works for both of us."

A useful closing tactic when you get an offer: thank them, express genuine enthusiasm, then ask "is there any flexibility on the base / the level?" A polite, specific ask paired with real enthusiasm almost never backfires, and companies routinely leave room for exactly this.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The single biggest mistake engineers make is not negotiating at all — accepting the first number out of relief or fear. Recruiters expect a counter, and a calm, informed one rarely costs you the offer (if a company rescinds because you politely negotiated, that's a red flag about them). The second mistake is giving a hard single number too early, which either leaves money on the table or prices you out before they know your value. The winning posture is calm, researched, and collaborative: you know your worth, you've done the homework, and you're solving for a package that works for both sides — not fighting for scraps.

Q21. Describe how you handle a handoff from design when the mockups don't cover every state.

Detailed Answer

"Mockups almost always show the happy path with realistic-looking data.
