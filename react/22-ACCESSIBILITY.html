SECTION 1 - FOUNDATIONS

Q1. Why does accessibility matter — legally, for UX, and for SEO?

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Accessibility (a11y) means people with disabilities can perceive, understand, and operate your app. It matters for three concrete reasons: legal — laws like the ADA, Section 508, the EU's EN 301 549 / European Accessibility Act, and the UK Equality Act expose companies to real lawsuits; UX — roughly 15–20% of people have a disability, and accessible design (keyboard support, clear focus, good contrast) helps everyone, including temporary and situational impairments; and SEO — the same semantic structure that screen readers rely on (headings, landmarks, alt text, labels) is exactly what search-engine crawlers parse.

Detailed Answer

Disability is broader than most engineers assume. It spans visual (blindness, low vision, color blindness), motor (can't use a mouse, relies on keyboard or switch devices), auditory (needs captions), and cognitive (needs clear language, predictable layouts). It's also often temporary (a broken arm) or situational (bright sunlight, a noisy train, holding a baby). Building for the permanent case improves the product for the whole spectrum.

On the legal side, high-profile cases (e.g. Domino's in the US) confirmed that websites and apps count as "places of public accommodation." Most demand letters cite WCAG 2.1 AA as the objective standard. Accessibility is therefore a compliance requirement, not a nice-to-have, for anything customer-facing.

On UX and SEO, accessible markup is machine-readable markup. A crawler and a screen reader both need to know "this is the main content, this is a navigation region, this image shows X, this button does Y." A well-structured accessible page tends to rank and convert better because it's genuinely easier to use.

Senior Engineer Perspective

Interviewers want to hear that a11y is a requirement, not a phase-two polish task, and that it's cheapest when designed in rather than retrofitted. The line that lands: "the same semantics that make a screen reader work make Google's crawler work — accessibility and SEO are the same investment." Bonus: mention the "curb-cut effect" — features built for disability (curb cuts, captions, keyboard support) end up benefiting everyone.

Q2. What are WCAG and the POUR principles, and what do the A/AA/AAA levels mean?

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) is the W3C standard that defines what "accessible" means, organised under four principles remembered by the acronym POUR: Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust. Each guideline has testable success criteria graded at three conformance levels: A (minimum), AA (the practical/legal target), and AAA (enhanced, rarely required site-wide).

Detailed Answer

The four POUR principles in plain terms:

  • Perceivable — users can perceive the content: text alternatives for images, captions for media, sufficient color contrast, content not conveyed by color alone.
  • Operable — users can operate the UI: everything works by keyboard, enough time to read/act, no seizure-inducing flashing, clear focus indicators and navigation.
  • Understandable — content and operation are predictable: readable language, consistent navigation, clear labels, helpful error identification and recovery.
  • Robust — content works across current and future user agents and assistive tech: valid markup, correct name/role/value for all UI components.
LevelMeaningTypical target
ABare minimum; blocking barriers removedNever sufficient alone
AAAddresses the biggest, most common barriersThe standard almost everyone must meet (laws, contracts)
AAAHighest bar (e.g. 7:1 contrast, sign language for video)Aspirational; not required across a whole site

Versions: WCAG 2.0 (2008), 2.1 (2018, added mobile/low-vision/cognitive criteria), 2.2 (2023, added focus-appearance and dragging alternatives). WCAG 3.0 is in draft. AA of 2.1/2.2 is the current practical benchmark.

Senior Engineer Perspective

You don't need to memorise every criterion, but you must be able to name POUR, know that AA is the target, and cite a couple of concrete AA criteria (4.5:1 text contrast, keyboard operability, visible focus, labels/instructions). Interviewers use this to separate people who've actually shipped compliant work from people who've only heard the buzzword. A good tell: knowing WCAG describes outcomes, while ARIA and semantic HTML are the techniques you use to satisfy them.

Q3. Why should you reach for semantic HTML first, and why does it beat "div soup"?

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: Extremely High

Short Answer

Semantic HTML elements (<button>, <a>, <nav>, <main>, <label>, <h1>–<h6>) come with built-in role, keyboard behaviour, focus management, and states — for free. "Div soup" (<div onClick> everywhere) has none of that: a div is announced as nothing, isn't focusable, doesn't respond to Enter/Space, and forces you to reimplement everything the browser already gave you. Native first is faster, more robust, and less bug-prone.

Detailed Answer

Every native interactive element ships an entire accessibility contract. A real <button> is in the tab order, exposes the "button" role to screen readers, fires on both Enter and Space, shows a focus ring, and works with form submission. To make a <div> equivalent you'd have to add role="button", tabIndex={0}, a keydown handler for Enter and Space, and a focus style — and you'll still miss edge cases (right-click, high-contrast mode, forced-colors).

Semantic elements also give you landmarks (<header>, <nav>, <main>, <footer>, <aside>) that let screen-reader users jump directly to regions, and a heading outline (h1–h6) that acts like a table of contents. Div soup produces a flat, unnavigable page.

Code Example

// BAD: div soup — reinvents everything, still incomplete
<div className="btn" onClick={save}>Save</div>

// To make that div even roughly correct you must add ALL of this:
<div
  role="button"
  tabIndex={0}
  onClick={save}
  onKeyDown={(e) => {
    if (e.key === "Enter" || e.key === " ") { e.preventDefault(); save(); }
  }}
>
  Save
</div>

// GOOD: the native element gives role, focus, Enter/Space, and focus ring for free
<button type="button" onClick={save}>Save</button>

// Landmarks + headings give screen-reader users structure to navigate
<header>...</header>
<nav aria-label="Primary">...</nav>
<main>
  <h1>Dashboard</h1>
  <section><h2>Reports</h2>...</section>
</main>
<footer>...</footer>

Project Example

A common cleanup on a React codebase: replacing clickable <div> cards and icon "buttons" with real <button>/<a> elements. It usually deletes dozens of hand-rolled keydown handlers and tabIndex props, fixes a batch of axe violations at once, and makes the whole app keyboard-operable with far less code.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The one-liner interviewers want: "use the platform." The strongest signal is knowing exactly what you give up with a div — role, focusability, Enter/Space, focus ring — and that <a> is for navigation (has an href) while <button> is for actions. Watch for the trap of <a href="#"> or <a> with an onClick but no href used as a fake button — that's the same anti-pattern wearing a different tag.

SECTION 2 - SEMANTIC HTML & ARIA

Q4. When should you use ARIA, and what is the first rule of ARIA?

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: Extremely High

Short Answer

ARIA (Accessible Rich Internet Applications) is a set of attributes that add roles, states, and properties to markup so assistive tech can understand custom widgets. The first rule of ARIA is: don't use ARIA if a native HTML element or attribute already gives you the semantics and behaviour you need. ARIA changes only how something is announced — it adds no behaviour, no keyboard handling, no focus. Use it only for gaps native HTML can't fill.

Detailed Answer

The five rules of ARIA, condensed:

  • 1. Prefer native HTML; only use ARIA when no native element fits.
  • 2. Don't change native semantics unless you must (don't put role="heading" on a <button>).
  • 3. All interactive ARIA widgets must be keyboard-operable.
  • 4. Don't put role="presentation" or aria-hidden="true" on a focusable element — you'll create an element that's reachable but invisible to screen readers.
  • 5. Every interactive element needs an accessible name.

The crucial mental model: ARIA is a promise to the screen reader, not an implementation. Writing role="button" makes the element announced as a button but does not make Enter/Space work or make it focusable — you must add that yourself. This is why "no ARIA is better than bad ARIA": a wrong or unfulfilled promise actively misleads users.

Code Example

// WRONG: ARIA that duplicates native semantics (rule 1 violation)
<button role="button">Save</button>         // redundant
<input type="checkbox" role="checkbox" />    // redundant

// WRONG: role without the behaviour ARIA promises (rule 3 violation)
<div role="button">Save</div>
// announced as a button, but not focusable and Enter/Space do nothing

// RIGHT: use ARIA to fill a real gap native HTML can't express
<nav aria-label="Pagination">...</nav>          // distinguish multiple navs
<button aria-expanded={open} aria-controls="menu">Menu</button>
<ul id="menu" hidden={!open}>...</ul>

// RIGHT: hide decorative content from the accessibility tree
<span aria-hidden="true">🎉</span>

Senior Engineer Perspective

Say the first rule verbatim and then prove you understand why: ARIA adds semantics but zero behaviour, so misapplied ARIA is worse than none. Interviewers love the follow-up "what does role='button' on a div actually get you?" — the answer is "the announcement, but you still owe tabIndex, keyboard handlers, and focus styles." Mention that aria-hidden="true" on a focusable element is a classic footgun (rule 4).

Q5. What are ARIA roles, states, and properties?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

ARIA describes UI to assistive tech in three parts. A role says what an element is (role="dialog", role="tablist"). States describe the current, changeable condition (aria-expanded, aria-checked, aria-disabled, aria-selected). Properties describe more static relationships and features (aria-label, aria-labelledby, aria-controls, aria-required). Together they form the "name, role, value" that WCAG 4.1.2 requires for every component.

Detailed Answer

The practical difference between a state and a property is how often it changes. States flip in response to user interaction and must be kept in sync in React — e.g. toggling aria-expanded when a disclosure opens. Properties tend to be set once and describe structure or naming.

CategoryExamplesNote
Roledialog, tab, tablist, tabpanel, alert, menu, switchSet once; defines the widget type
Statearia-expanded, aria-checked, aria-selected, aria-disabled, aria-pressed, aria-currentDynamic; update on interaction
Propertyaria-label, aria-labelledby, aria-describedby, aria-controls, aria-haspopup, aria-requiredMostly static; naming/relationships

In React, the key discipline is that ARIA state attributes are driven by your component state and re-render like any other prop — never let the DOM's ARIA and your state drift apart.

Code Example

function Disclosure({ title, children }) {
  const [open, setOpen] = React.useState(false);
  return (
    <>
      <button
        aria-expanded={open}          {/* STATE, synced to React state */}
        aria-controls="panel"         {/* PROPERTY, relationship */}
        onClick={() => setOpen(o => !o)}
      >
        {title}
      </button>
      <div id="panel" role="region" hidden={!open}>   {/* ROLE */}
        {children}
      </div>
    </>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

Frame it as "name, role, value" from WCAG 4.1.2 — that vocabulary signals depth. The senior insight is the React-specific one: ARIA states are just another piece of UI that must be derived from your state, so they belong in the render output, not set imperatively via a ref where they can desync. Note booleans are strings in the a11y tree but React lets you pass real booleans (aria-expanded={open}) — except quirks like aria-hidden which you often gate so it's absent rather than "false".

Q6. How does an element get its accessible name (label / aria-label / aria-labelledby)?

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: Extremely High

Short Answer

The accessible name is the string a screen reader announces for an element (e.g. "Search, edit text" or "Close, button"). It's computed from a priority order: aria-labelledby wins, then aria-label, then a native association like a <label> tied to an input, then the element's own text content, and finally title. For form fields, prefer a real <label>; use aria-label only when there's no visible text (e.g. an icon-only button).

Detailed Answer

The accessible-name computation (simplified, highest priority first):

  1. aria-labelledby — references the id(s) of other elements; their text becomes the name. Best when a visible element already holds the label text.
  2. aria-label — a literal string you supply. Use for icon buttons and cases with no visible label. Downside: it's invisible, so it can drift from what's on screen and isn't translated by the page.
  3. Native labelling — a <label for="id"> (or wrapping label) for form controls, alt for images, <caption>/<legend>.
  4. Content — the element's own visible text (a <button>Save</button> is named "Save").

Two related but distinct concepts: the name (what it is) and the description (extra detail via aria-describedby, e.g. a hint or an error message). Don't stuff hints into the label — use a description.

Code Example

// Best for forms: visible, clickable, translatable native label
<label htmlFor="email">Email</label>
<input id="email" type="email" />

// Icon-only button: no visible text, so supply aria-label
<button aria-label="Close dialog">
  <XIcon aria-hidden="true" />   {/* hide the decorative icon */}
</button>

// aria-labelledby: reuse a visible heading as the name (great for dialogs)
<div role="dialog" aria-labelledby="dlg-title" aria-describedby="dlg-desc">
  <h2 id="dlg-title">Delete account</h2>
  <p id="dlg-desc">This action cannot be undone.</p>
</div>

// aria-labelledby can concatenate multiple ids in order
<span id="unit">Price in USD</span>
<input aria-labelledby="unit amount-label" />
<span id="amount-label">amount</span>

Senior Engineer Perspective

Interviewers probe the priority order and, critically, that aria-label is a last resort, not a default — a visible <label> helps sighted, cognitive, and voice-control users too (voice users say the visible label). Common bugs to name: placeholder-as-label (disappears on input, poor contrast, not a real name), and icon buttons with no name announced as just "button." Distinguish name vs description (aria-describedby) — a frequent follow-up.

Q7. How do you build accessible forms with proper labels and error messaging?

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: Extremely High

Short Answer

Every control needs a programmatically associated <label>. Group related controls with <fieldset>/<legend>. Mark invalid fields with aria-invalid, link the error text with aria-describedby, indicate required with the native required attribute (and/or aria-required), and announce errors so screen-reader users hear them — either by moving focus to the first error or exposing the message in a live region. Don't rely on color alone to signal errors.

Detailed Answer

Accessible-form checklist:

  • Labels — visible <label htmlFor> for each input; never placeholder as the only label.
  • Grouping — radio groups and checkbox groups belong in a <fieldset> with a <legend> describing the group.
  • Required & format — use native required; describe format rules with aria-describedby hint text, not just placeholder.
  • Errors — set aria-invalid="true", connect the message via aria-describedby, and make the error text itself perceivable (icon + text, not color only).
  • Announcing — on submit, either focus the first invalid field (moves the SR there and reads its error) or render an error summary at the top in a live region / focus it.

In React with react-hook-form or Formik, wire aria-invalid and aria-describedby from the form state so they update on validation.

Code Example

function EmailField({ error }) {
  return (
    <div>
      <label htmlFor="email">Email</label>
      <input
        id="email"
        type="email"
        required
        aria-invalid={error ? true : undefined}
        aria-describedby={error ? "email-error email-hint" : "email-hint"}
      />
      <p id="email-hint">We'll never share your email.</p>
      {error && (
        <p id="email-error" role="alert">   {/* announced immediately */}
          {error}
        </p>
      )}
    </div>
  );
}

// Grouping radios correctly
<fieldset>
  <legend>Notification preference</legend>
  <label><input type="radio" name="notify" value="email" /> Email</label>
  <label><input type="radio" name="notify" value="sms" /> SMS</label>
</fieldset>

Senior Engineer Perspective

The senior differentiators: (1) errors must be announced, not just shown — role="alert" on the message or focusing the first bad field is the mechanism; (2) never signal validity with color alone (WCAG 1.4.1) — pair it with text/icons; (3) associate the error with the input via aria-describedby so it's read when focus lands there. A great detail: an error summary at the top that you focus on submit scales better than per-field focus for long forms, and it doubles as a set of skip-links to each error.

SECTION 3 - INTERACTION & KEYBOARD

Q8. How do you handle keyboard navigation, tab order, and focus management?

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: Extremely High

Short Answer

Everything a mouse can do must be doable by keyboard (WCAG 2.1.1). Tab order should follow the DOM order — so structure your markup in reading order and avoid positive tabIndex. Use tabIndex={0} to make a custom control focusable, tabIndex={-1} to make an element focusable only programmatically (via .focus()), and never positive values, which hijack the natural order. Always keep a visible focus indicator.

Detailed Answer

Key rules:

  • Follow DOM order. The tab sequence is the DOM order of focusable elements. Reordering visually with CSS (flex/grid order, absolute positioning) can desync visual and tab order — a real trap.
  • tabIndex values: 0 = in natural order; -1 = focusable by script only (for roving focus, error fields, headings you jump to); positive numbers = an anti-pattern that overrides natural order and creates a maintenance nightmare.
  • Expected keys. Widgets follow the ARIA Authoring Practices: Enter/Space activate buttons, arrow keys move within composite widgets (menus, tabs, radios), Escape closes overlays.
  • Visible focus. Never do outline: none without an equally clear replacement. Use :focus-visible to show rings for keyboard users without cluttering mouse clicks.
  • Roving tabindex for composite widgets: only one item is tabIndex={0} at a time, the rest are -1, and arrow keys move the "0".

Code Example

/* Keep focus visible for keyboard users only */
button:focus-visible {
  outline: 3px solid #1f4e79;
  outline-offset: 2px;
}

// Roving tabindex: arrow keys move focus among toolbar buttons
function Toolbar({ items }) {
  const [active, setActive] = React.useState(0);
  const refs = React.useRef([]);

  function onKeyDown(e) {
    if (e.key === "ArrowRight") {
      const next = (active + 1) % items.length;
      setActive(next);
      refs.current[next].focus();
    }
    if (e.key === "ArrowLeft") {
      const prev = (active - 1 + items.length) % items.length;
      setActive(prev);
      refs.current[prev].focus();
    }
  }

  return (
    <div role="toolbar" onKeyDown={onKeyDown}>
      {items.map((label, i) => (
        <button
          key={label}
          ref={(el) => (refs.current[i] = el)}
          tabIndex={i === active ? 0 : -1}   {/* only one in tab order */}
        >
          {label}
        </button>
      ))}
    </div>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

Two lines that mark a senior: "tab order should equal DOM order — never use positive tabIndex," and "removing the outline without a replacement is a WCAG failure; use :focus-visible." Expect follow-ups on the visual-vs-DOM-order desync caused by CSS reordering, and on the roving-tabindex pattern for composite widgets (only one stop, arrows move within). Bonus: mention that you test by unplugging the mouse and driving the whole flow with Tab, Shift+Tab, arrows, Enter, Space, Escape.

Q9. How do you trap focus in a modal and restore it when the modal closes?

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: Extremely High

Short Answer

When a modal opens: move focus into it (to the first focusable element or the dialog itself), trap Tab/Shift+Tab so focus can't escape to the page behind it, mark the rest of the page inert (aria-hidden or the inert attribute) so screen readers can't wander out, and close on Escape. When it closes: restore focus to the element that opened it (the trigger). The native <dialog> element with showModal() does most of this for you.

Detailed Answer

A correct accessible modal handles all of:

  • Role + name — role="dialog" (or alertdialog), aria-modal="true", and an accessible name via aria-labelledby.
  • Initial focus — send focus inside on open (first field, or the dialog container with tabIndex={-1}).
  • Focus trap — wrap Tab from the last focusable element back to the first and Shift+Tab from the first back to the last.
  • Background inert — the content behind must not be focusable or read; the inert attribute (or aria-hidden) on the app root achieves this.
  • Escape to close, and clicking the backdrop where appropriate.
  • Restore focus — remember document.activeElement before opening and .focus() it on close, or nothing has focus and the SR user is stranded.

In practice, use a battle-tested primitive — the native <dialog>, Radix UI Dialog, React Aria, or Headless UI — rather than reimplementing the trap, which is easy to get subtly wrong.

Code Example

function Modal({ open, onClose, labelledBy, children }) {
  const dialogRef = React.useRef(null);
  const lastFocused = React.useRef(null);

  React.useEffect(() => {
    if (!open) return;
    lastFocused.current = document.activeElement;   // remember trigger
    const dialog = dialogRef.current;
    dialog.focus();                                 // move focus in

    function onKeyDown(e) {
      if (e.key === "Escape") onClose();
      if (e.key !== "Tab") return;
      const focusable = dialog.querySelectorAll(
        'a[href], button:not([disabled]), input, textarea, select, [tabindex]:not([tabindex="-1"])'
      );
      const first = focusable[0];
      const last = focusable[focusable.length - 1];
      if (e.shiftKey && document.activeElement === first) {
        e.preventDefault(); last.focus();           // wrap to end
      } else if (!e.shiftKey && document.activeElement === last) {
        e.preventDefault(); first.focus();          // wrap to start
      }
    }
    dialog.addEventListener("keydown", onKeyDown);
    return () => {
      dialog.removeEventListener("keydown", onKeyDown);
      lastFocused.current?.focus();                 // restore focus on close
    };
  }, [open, onClose]);

  if (!open) return null;
  return (
    <div className="backdrop" onClick={onClose}>
      <div
        ref={dialogRef}
        role="dialog"
        aria-modal="true"
        aria-labelledby={labelledBy}
        tabIndex={-1}
        onClick={(e) => e.stopPropagation()}
      >
        {children}
      </div>
    </div>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The single most-forgotten step is restoring focus to the trigger on close — without it the keyboard/SR user is dumped at the top of the document. Interviewers also want aria-modal plus making the background inert (otherwise a screen reader can Tab or swipe into the hidden page). The mature answer: "I wouldn't hand-roll this in production — I'd use the native <dialog> or Radix/React Aria, which handle the trap, inert, and restore correctly." Knowing it well enough to explain the trap, but choosing a library, is the senior move.

Q10. How do you manage focus on route change in a single-page app?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

In an MPA, a full page load resets focus to the top and the screen reader announces the new page. In an SPA, navigation only swaps components — focus stays put and nothing is announced, so keyboard/SR users don't realise the page changed. The fix: on each route change, move focus to a sensible target (usually the new page's <h1> or a focusable main container) and/or announce the new page title in a live region.

Detailed Answer

Two complementary techniques:

  • Focus management — after the route renders, call .focus() on the page's heading or main region. Give it tabIndex={-1} so it's programmatically focusable without entering the tab order. This mirrors the native "focus resets on load" behaviour and gives SR users the page heading.
  • Route announcer — an aria-live region that you update with the new page's title/name so a change is spoken even if you don't move focus. Frameworks like Next.js and Gatsby ship a route announcer; with React Router you add one yourself.

Also update document.title per route — assistive tech and browser history rely on it, and it's part of the announcement.

Code Example

import { useLocation } from "react-router-dom";

// Focus the page heading after each navigation
function useFocusOnRouteChange(headingRef) {
  const { pathname } = useLocation();
  React.useEffect(() => {
    headingRef.current?.focus();
  }, [pathname, headingRef]);
}

function Page({ title, children }) {
  const headingRef = React.useRef(null);
  useFocusOnRouteChange(headingRef);
  React.useEffect(() => { document.title = title + " — MyApp"; }, [title]);

  return (
    <main>
      <h1 ref={headingRef} tabIndex={-1}>{title}</h1>   {/* focusable target */}
      {children}
    </main>
  );
}

// Optional: a global route announcer (visually hidden live region)
function RouteAnnouncer({ message }) {
  return (
    <div
      aria-live="assertive"
      style={{ position: "absolute", width: 1, height: 1, overflow: "hidden",
               clip: "rect(0 0 0 0)", whiteSpace: "nowrap" }}
    >
      {message}
    </div>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The core insight interviewers reward: "client-side routing breaks the browser's built-in focus reset and page announcement, so I have to recreate both." Focusing the <h1> (with tabIndex={-1}) is the standard pattern; a live-region route announcer is the belt-and-braces addition. Note Next.js App Router does this automatically, but a plain React Router SPA does not — a great "gotcha" to raise. Avoid tabIndex={0} on the heading, which would wrongly add it to the tab order.

Q11. What are skip links and why do they matter?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

A skip link is a "Skip to main content" link that's the first focusable element on the page. It lets keyboard and screen-reader users jump past the repeated header/nav (which can be dozens of Tab stops) straight to the main content. It's commonly visually hidden until it receives focus, then appears. It satisfies WCAG 2.4.1 "Bypass Blocks."

Detailed Answer

Without a skip link, a keyboard user must tab through the entire masthead and navigation on every page before reaching content — exhausting on a site with a big nav. The skip link targets the main region's id; to make focus actually land there (not just scroll), the target should be focusable (tabIndex={-1}) or be a natural landmark like <main id="main">. It's typically hidden off-screen and revealed with :focus so it doesn't clutter the visual design but is available to keyboard users.

Landmarks (<main>, <nav>) provide a parallel bypass mechanism for screen-reader users (they can jump by region), so skip links most directly help sighted keyboard-only users.

Code Example

// First element inside <body>
<a className="skip-link" href="#main">Skip to main content</a>
...
<main id="main" tabIndex={-1}>
  <h1>Dashboard</h1>
</main>

/* Hidden until focused, then visible */
.skip-link {

Q13. How do live regions (aria-live) announce dynamic updates to screen readers?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

When content updates without a page reload (a toast, a search-results count, a form-submit result), a screen reader won't notice unless the region is a live region. Marking a container with aria-live="polite" (announce when idle) or aria-live="assertive" (interrupt immediately) makes the SR read out changes to that region. role="status" ≈ polite and role="alert" ≈ assertive are convenient shortcuts.

Detailed Answer

  • polite — waits until the user is idle; use for non-urgent updates (results loaded, "saved," a count changing).
  • assertive — interrupts whatever is being read; reserve for urgent messages (errors, session-expiry warnings). Overusing assertive is hostile.
  • The region must exist in the DOM before the text changes. Screen readers announce the diff when the region's contents mutate. If you mount the region and its text in the same render, some SRs miss it — render an empty live region up front and then populate it.
  • aria-atomic="true" makes the SR read the entire region on any change (useful for "3 results" → "5 results"); default reads only what changed.

In React, this "empty region exists, then text appears" pattern is the tricky part, because a conditional render mounts region + text together. Keep the region permanently mounted and toggle its text via state.

Code Example

// Permanently-mounted live region; only the text changes
function ToastRegion({ message }) {
  return (
    <div aria-live="polite" aria-atomic="true" className="sr-region">
      {message}   {/* update this via state to trigger an announcement */}
    </div>
  );
}

// Urgent, interrupting message
<div role="alert">{errorMessage}</div>   {/* assertive by default */}

// Non-urgent status, e.g. results count
<div role="status">{results.length} results found</div>

/* Visually-hidden but SR-available (do NOT use display:none) */
.sr-region {
  position: absolute; width: 1px; height: 1px;
  padding: 0; margin: -1px; overflow: hidden;
  clip: rect(0, 0, 0, 0); white-space: nowrap; border: 0;
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The gotcha interviewers reward: the live region must be present in the DOM before it gets its text — a common React bug is conditionally rendering the whole region, which mounts container + message together and gets silently dropped. Also know polite vs assertive and that role="alert"/role="status" are the implicit-live shortcuts. And never hide a live region with display:none or visibility:hidden — those remove it from the a11y tree; use the visually-hidden clip pattern.

Q14. How do you build accessible custom components (dropdown, modal, tabs) in React?

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Follow the ARIA Authoring Practices Guide (APG) pattern for the specific widget — it specifies the exact roles, states, and keyboard interactions. Provide the roles (tablist/tab/tabpanel, menu/menuitem, dialog), sync states (aria-selected, aria-expanded), and wire the expected keys (arrows to move, Enter/Space to activate, Escape to close). For production, prefer a headless library (Radix UI, React Aria, Headless UI) that implements the APG correctly.

Detailed Answer

Each pattern has a defined keyboard contract:

WidgetRolesKeys
Tabstablist / tab / tabpanelLeft/Right (or Up/Down) move between tabs; Enter/Space or auto-activate; roving tabindex
Menu / dropdownbutton + menu / menuitemEnter/Space/Down to open; Up/Down to move; Esc to close; focus returns to trigger
Modal dialogdialog + aria-modalFocus trapped; Esc closes; focus restored on close
Combobox / selectcombobox + listbox / optionType to filter; arrows to move; Enter to select; aria-activedescendant

Common state wiring: the tab uses aria-selected and points at its panel via aria-controls; the panel points back with aria-labelledby. A menu button uses aria-haspopup and aria-expanded. Getting arrow-key handling and focus management right is where hand-rolled widgets usually fail — hence the strong recommendation to lean on a headless primitive.

Code Example

// Accessible Tabs following the APG (roving tabindex + arrow keys)
function Tabs({ tabs }) {
  const [active, setActive] = React.useState(0);
  const refs = React.useRef([]);

  function onKeyDown(e) {
    let next = active;
    if (e.key === "ArrowRight") next = (active + 1) % tabs.length;
    if (e.key === "ArrowLeft") next = (active - 1 + tabs.length) % tabs.length;
    if (next !== active) { setActive(next); refs.current[next].focus(); }
  }

  return (
    <div>
      <div role="tablist" aria-label="Settings" onKeyDown={onKeyDown}>
        {tabs.map((t, i) => (
          <button
            key={t.id}
            ref={(el) => (refs.current[i] = el)}
            role="tab"
            id={"tab-" + t.id}
            aria-selected={i === active}
            aria-controls={"panel-" + t.id}
            tabIndex={i === active ? 0 : -1}   {/* roving tabindex */}
            onClick={() => setActive(i)}
          >
            {t.label}
          </button>
        ))}
      </div>
      {tabs.map((t, i) => (
        <div
          key={t.id}
          role="tabpanel"
          id={"panel-" + t.id}
          aria-labelledby={"tab-" + t.id}
          hidden={i !== active}
        >
          {t.content}
        </div>
      ))}
    </div>
  );
}

// import * as Tabs from "@radix-ui/react-tabs";

SECTION 5 - ARIA DEEP DIVE

Q16. What's the difference between aria-hidden, role="presentation"/"none", and the inert attribute?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

aria-hidden="true" removes an element (and its subtree) from the accessibility tree — visually still there, but invisible to screen readers. role="presentation" (or its synonym "none") strips only the element's semantics (its role), turning a table into an unstyled block of generic text, while its children can still be exposed. inert goes further than aria-hidden: it removes the subtree from the accessibility tree and makes it unfocusable and unselectable, in one native attribute.

Detailed Answer

These three solve different, easily-confused problems:

  • aria-hidden="true" — hides purely decorative or duplicated content (an icon next to a text label, an off-screen duplicate for a carousel). It does not affect focusability or visual rendering — a focusable element inside an aria-hidden subtree becomes a serious bug: reachable by Tab but silent to the screen reader.
  • role="presentation"/"none" — used when you must use a semantic tag for layout/styling reasons but don't want its implicit role announced (e.g. a <table> used purely for CSS grid-like layout before flexbox existed). Rare in modern code, since CSS Grid/Flexbox removed most legitimate layout-table use cases.
  • inert — a boolean HTML attribute (not ARIA) that removes a subtree from the accessibility tree, tab order, and even Ctrl+F/find-in-page, and suppresses events like click. It's the correct, single-attribute way to make "the rest of the page" non-interactive while a modal is open — one attribute instead of manually walking the DOM to apply aria-hidden and disabling every focusable descendant.

Code Example

// Decorative icon: hide from AT, keep it visually
<button>
  <svg aria-hidden="true">...</svg>
  Save
</button>

// BUG: focusable content trapped inside aria-hidden — reachable, but silent
<div aria-hidden="true">
  <button>Hidden but still tabbable!</button>   {/* fix: also disable/remove from tab order */}
</div>

// inert: one attribute makes the whole background non-interactive
<div id="app-root" inert={isModalOpen || undefined}>
  {/* entire app: unfocusable, unclickable, hidden from AT while a modal is open */}
</div>
<Modal open={isModalOpen} />

Senior Engineer Perspective

The trap interviewers set: "you added aria-hidden="true" to the background div behind your modal — is that enough?" No — a background <button> or link is still in the tab order, so a keyboard user can Tab into invisible-to-them content. inert fixes both problems in one attribute and is now supported in all evergreen browsers, which is why it has mostly replaced the old "walk the DOM and toggle aria-hidden plus tabindex=-1 on every focusable descendant" polyfill pattern.

Q17. What is aria-describedby vs aria-details, and how do multiple ids work in aria-labelledby?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

aria-describedby points to one or more ids whose text content is concatenated and read as a supplementary description (e.g. a password hint). It exposes plain text only. aria-details instead points to a single element that can carry its own rich structure (headings, lists) — support is far patchier, so aria-describedby remains the practical default. aria-labelledby accepts a space-separated list of ids and concatenates their text, in the order listed, to build the accessible name (not description).

Detailed Answer

A subtlety that trips people up: aria-labelledby and aria-describedby both take references, not literal strings, and both concatenate in listed order — reordering the id list reorders the announced text. Also, referenced elements don't need to be visible only for sighted users; they can be visually hidden and still contribute to the name/description, which is how many hidden-label patterns work.

aria-details exists because a plain-text description is sometimes insufficient (e.g. a footnote with a link, or a rich validation message with a list of fixes). Since screen reader support for aria-details is inconsistent as of 2026, most production code still uses aria-describedby pointing at a container whose text content is flattened, accepting the loss of internal structure.

Code Example

// aria-labelledby: concatenates "Billing address" + "required"
<span id="lbl">Billing address</span>
<span id="req" className="sr-only">required</span>
<input aria-labelledby="lbl req" />

// aria-describedby: supplementary text, not part of the name
<label htmlFor="pw">Password</label>
<input id="pw" type="password" aria-describedby="pw-hint" />
<p id="pw-hint">At least 12 characters, one number.</p>

Senior Engineer Perspective

Knowing that both attributes take space-separated id lists — and that order matters — is the detail that distinguishes someone who has actually debugged a mis-ordered announcement from someone reciting definitions. The pragmatic answer on aria-details: "I know it exists for rich descriptions, but given inconsistent support I'd default to aria-describedby and flatten the content."

Q18. What are landmark roles, and how many of each should a page have?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Landmarks are roles that divide a page into navigable regions: banner (<header>), navigation (<nav>), main (<main>), complementary (<aside>), contentinfo (<footer>), and generic region (<section aria-label>). Screen reader users bring up a landmarks list to jump straight to a region. The rule of thumb: exactly one main per page, one top-level banner/contentinfo, but multiple nav or region landmarks are fine — as long as each gets a distinct aria-label so they're distinguishable in the list.

Detailed Answer

Landmarks are the SR-user equivalent of visually scanning a page's layout at a glance. Getting them right means two things: using the right element/role for each region, and naming duplicate landmarks so a list of "3 navigation regions" isn't just three identical, unhelpful entries.

complementary<aside>Many, labeled

Q19. What are aria-relevant and aria-busy, and how do they refine live-region announcements beyond polite/assertive?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

aria-relevant controls which kinds of DOM mutations inside a live region get announced — additions, removals, text changes, or all of them (default: "additions text"). aria-busy="true" tells assistive tech "I'm in the middle of a batch of updates, hold your announcements" — set it while you make several DOM changes at once, then set it back to false so the screen reader announces the final settled state instead of every intermediate mutation.

Detailed Answer

Without aria-busy, updating a live region's contents in several steps (clear it, then re-populate item by item) can cause a screen reader to announce a confusing stream of partial states. Wrapping the whole batch in aria-busy="true" ... update ... aria-busy="false" defers the announcement until the region is stable.

aria-relevant values are space-separated: additions, removals, text, or all. A search-results live region that only cares about new results appearing would use aria-relevant="additions" so removed placeholder rows don't generate noisy "item removed" announcements.

Code Example

function ResultsRegion({ results, isRefetching }) {
  return (
    <div
      aria-live="polite"
      aria-atomic="false"
      aria-relevant="additions text"
      aria-busy={isRefetching}    {/* SR waits until this flips back to false */}
    >
      {results.map((r) => <p key={r.id}>{r.title}</p>)}
    </div>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

Most engineers never touch these two attributes and get by with plain aria-live="polite" — which is fine for simple cases. Bringing up aria-busy unprompted signals you've actually debugged a "screen reader reads every single row as it streams in" bug, which is a very specific, hard-won piece of experience interviewers notice.

Q20. What's the difference between aria-disabled and the native disabled attribute?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

The native disabled attribute removes an element from the tab order, prevents it from receiving focus or firing events, and often mutes its styling automatically — but it also makes the element and its tooltip completely unreachable, so you can't explain why it's disabled to a keyboard/SR user. aria-disabled="true" only announces the disabled state — the element stays focusable and in the tab order, which lets you keep it reachable and pair it with an explanation (e.g. via aria-describedby) of why it's currently unavailable.

Detailed Answer

Native disabled is right for controls that are simply not applicable right now (a "Next" button before any option is picked) — nobody needs an explanation. But for controls that are conditionally blocked for a reason a user should learn (a "Submit" button disabled because required fields are empty, a "Publish" button blocked pending review), keeping the control focusable with aria-disabled="true" plus a description is more helpful — a keyboard user can still Tab to it and hear why it's disabled, rather than the button simply not existing to them.

A real cost of native disabled: disabled elements are removed from the tab order entirely, so a keyboard user scanning a form has no way to discover the button exists at all, let alone why it won't submit.

Code Example

// Native disabled: fine when no explanation is needed
<button disabled>Next</button>

// aria-disabled: stays focusable/announced, and can explain why
<button
  aria-disabled={!formIsValid}
  aria-describedby={!formIsValid ? "submit-reason" : undefined}
  onClick={(e) => { if (!formIsValid) e.preventDefault(); }}
>
  Submit
</button>
{!formIsValid && <p id="submit-reason">Fill in all required fields to submit.</p>}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The nuance interviewers reward: aria-disabled requires you to manually block the interaction (preventDefault / early-return in the handler) since the browser will still let the click and Enter/Space fire — unlike native disabled, which blocks all of that for free. Getting this wrong (adding aria-disabled but forgetting the guard in the handler) is a real, shippable bug.

Q22. How do implicit ARIA roles work, and when do you actually need to override them?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

Every native HTML element has a default, "implicit" ARIA role baked into the browser's accessibility mapping (a <button> is implicitly role="button", an <h1> is implicitly role="heading" with aria-level="1"). You override this with an explicit role attribute only when you must repurpose an element for something the browser doesn't natively support — e.g. a <ul> repurposed as a custom role="listbox" for a dropdown, or a <div> that must become role="tablist".

Detailed Answer

The W3C's ARIA-in-HTML spec defines this mapping precisely, and browsers/AT rely on it. Overriding a role changes what's announced but never changes native behavior that isn't role-based — e.g. giving a <ul> role="listbox" doesn't make arrow keys move between options; you still own that keyboard logic. This is why overriding roles on elements with rich native behavior (form controls, headings, tables) is riskier than doing it on largely-behaviorless elements like <div>, <span>, or list elements.

A related rule: some ARIA role changes are disallowed by spec on certain elements (you cannot give an <input type="checkbox"> role="tab" and expect reliable behavior) — when you find yourself fighting an element's implicit role, that's usually a signal to start from a plain <div>/<li> instead.

Code Example

// Overriding a list's implicit role to build a custom listbox
<ul role="listbox" aria-label="Choose a color">
  <li role="option" aria-selected={value === "red"}>Red</li>
  <li role="option" aria-selected={value === "blue"}>Blue</li>
</ul>
// You still own: arrow-key movement, aria-activedescendant or roving tabindex,
// Enter/Space to select — none of that comes free from the role override.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The senior framing: "an explicit role changes the announcement, not the behavior — I still owe every bit of keyboard logic the APG pattern specifies." Interviewers use this to check you don't think adding role="listbox" alone makes a widget accessible.

Q23. What is aria-owns, and when would you actually need it?

ROI: ★★ Frequency: Low

Short Answer

aria-owns tells assistive tech that an element should be treated as a parent of other elements in the accessibility tree, even though they aren't nested in the DOM. It's a last-resort fix for components that render children in a different DOM location than their logical parent — most commonly a dropdown/combobox whose listbox is portalled to document.body to escape a clipping/overflow container, but which must still be understood as "owned by" the input that opens it.

Detailed Answer

Screen readers build their tree primarily from DOM structure. Portals (React rendering a listbox into a node outside its logical parent to avoid overflow:hidden clipping) break that structural assumption — the popover is a DOM sibling of <body>, not a child of the combobox. aria-owns patches the accessibility tree back into the logical shape.

In practice, most modern implementations sidestep aria-owns entirely by using aria-controls plus aria-activedescendant (the combobox pattern) or by relying on aria-expanded/aria-haspopup instead — support for aria-owns is inconsistent across screen readers, so it's considered a fallback, not a first choice.

Code Example

// Portalled listbox, logically owned by the input
<input
  role="combobox"
  aria-expanded={open}
  aria-controls="portal-listbox"
  aria-owns="portal-listbox"     {/* patch the tree back together */}
/>
{ReactDOM.createPortal(
  <ul id="portal-listbox" role="listbox">...</ul>,
  document.body
)}

Senior Engineer Perspective

Low-frequency but a great "have you actually built a portalled combobox" signal. The mature answer acknowledges aria-owns' inconsistent support and that most component libraries (Radix, React Aria) avoid needing it by managing focus and aria-activedescendant carefully instead of relying on tree-patching.

Q24. What is a focus trap exactly, and what edge cases break naive implementations?

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

A focus trap confines Tab/Shift+Tab cycling to a subset of the page (typically a modal) so focus can never escape to the content behind it. A naive implementation — find all focusable elements once, wrap Tab at the ends — breaks the moment the trapped content changes dynamically: elements added/removed, elements becoming disabled, or nested focus traps (a confirm dialog opened from within a modal).

Detailed Answer

Edge cases that break a naive trap:

  • Stale focusable list — computing focusable elements once on mount, then the modal's content changes (an accordion expands, a row is deleted) and the cached first/last elements no longer exist or aren't the right boundary.
  • Zero focusable elements — an empty or all-disabled modal has nothing to trap focus to; you must fall back to focusing the dialog container itself (tabIndex={-1}).
  • Nested traps — a confirm dialog opened from inside a modal needs its own trap layered on top, and closing it must hand focus back to the parent trap, not the original page trigger.
  • Focus leaving via non-Tab means — clicking a link that programmatically calls .focus() on something outside the trap, or an iframe inside the modal capturing Tab differently.
  • Elements hidden mid-interaction — the currently focused element gets conditionally unmounted (e.g. it was the last item in a list and got deleted), leaving focus on <body>, effectively escaping the trap.

Because of these edge cases, computing the focusable set on every Tab keypress (not once on mount) and falling back to the container when the set is empty are the two fixes that matter most — and they're exactly why battle-tested libraries exist rather than everyone re-deriving this.

Code Example

function getFocusable(container) {
  // Recompute fresh on every keydown — never cache this list
  return Array.from(
    container.querySelectorAll(
      'a[href], button:not([disabled]), input:not([disabled]), select:not([disabled]), textarea:not([disabled]), [tabindex]:not([tabindex="-1"])'
    )
  ).filter((el) => el.offsetParent !== null); // exclude hidden elements
}

function onKeyDown(e, container) {
  if (e.key !== "Tab") return;
  const focusable = getFocusable(container);
  if (focusable.length === 0) { e.preventDefault(); container.focus(); return; }
  const first = focusable[0];
  const last = focusable[focusable.length - 1];
  if (e.shiftKey && document.activeElement === first) { e.preventDefault(); last.focus(); }
  else if (!e.shiftKey && document.activeElement === last) { e.preventDefault(); first.focus(); }
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The best answer explicitly says "I would not hand-roll this beyond understanding it" — naming the empty-focusable-set and nested-trap edge cases is what separates someone who has actually shipped a modal from someone reciting the Tab-wrap algorithm. Radix's FocusScope and React Aria's useFocusTrap handle all of these.

Q25. Explain roving tabindex in depth — why not just put tabIndex={0} on every item?

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Roving tabindex keeps exactly one item in a composite widget (a toolbar, tab list, radio group, menu) at tabIndex={0} while all siblings sit at tabIndex={-1}; arrow keys move the "0" between items and call .focus(). If every item were tabIndex={0}, Tab would stop on each one individually, forcing a keyboard user to Tab N times to get past an N-item toolbar — composite widgets are supposed to be a single Tab stop, with arrows moving inside them.

Detailed Answer

This reflects the ARIA APG's two-level navigation model: Tab moves between widgets, arrow keys move within one. A tab list, for instance, is one Tab stop overall — pressing Tab from outside lands on whichever tab is currently "active," and Left/Right move among the tabs without ever leaving that Tab stop. Only when the user tabs again does focus leave the widget entirely (to the tab panel or the next control after it).

There's an alternative to roving tabindex — aria-activedescendant — where a single container stays focused and you declare which descendant is "virtually" active via an id reference, without moving real DOM focus at all. It avoids re-mounting focus but requires the container to manage all the visual "focused" styling itself and is trickier to get right; roving tabindex is simpler and more broadly recommended for most composite widgets.

Code Example

// Roving tabindex: one Tab stop, arrows move inside
function RadioGroup({ options, value, onChange }) {
  const refs = React.useRef([]);
  function onKeyDown(e, i) {
    let next = i;
    if (e.key === "ArrowDown") next = (i + 1) % options.length;
    if (e.key === "ArrowUp") next = (i - 1 + options.length) % options.length;
    if (next !== i) {
      onChange(options[next].value);
      refs.current[next].focus();
    }
  }
  return (
    <div role="radiogroup" aria-label="Plan">
      {options.map((opt, i) => (
        <div
          key={opt.value}
          role="radio"
          aria-checked={value === opt.value}
          tabIndex={value === opt.value ? 0 : -1}   {/* only the checked one is a Tab stop */}
          ref={(el) => (refs.current[i] = el)}
          onKeyDown={(e) => onKeyDown(e, i)}
          onClick={() => onChange(opt.value)}
        >
          {opt.label}
        </div>
      ))}
    </div>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The line that lands: "Tab moves between widgets, arrows move within a widget — roving tabindex is how you implement that second layer." A great follow-up to preempt: "what's the alternative?" — aria-activedescendant, and knowing the tradeoff (real DOM focus movement vs virtual focus with more manual styling) shows genuine depth beyond a memorized pattern.

Q26. How do you build an accessible autocomplete/combobox keyboard model?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

The APG combobox pattern uses role="combobox" on the input, aria-expanded/aria-controls pointing at a role="listbox" popup, and aria-activedescendant on the input that references the currently-highlighted role="option" — the input keeps real DOM focus the entire time (so typing still works) while Down/Up move a virtual highlight, Enter selects, and Escape closes.

Detailed Answer

This is the canonical use case for aria-activedescendant instead of roving tabindex: you cannot move real focus off the input while the user is typing, so "which option is highlighted" has to be communicated without moving focus. The input announces itself as role="combobox" with aria-autocomplete="list", and each keypress updates aria-activedescendant to the id of the highlighted option — the screen reader announces that option even though DOM focus never left the input.

Required wiring: aria-expanded reflects whether the listbox is open, aria-controls links input → listbox, each option needs a stable id, and aria-selected marks the chosen option once picked.

Code Example

function Combobox({ options, value, onChange }) {
  const [open, setOpen] = React.useState(false);
  const [activeIndex, setActiveIndex] = React.useState(-1);

  function onKeyDown(e) {
    if (e.key === "ArrowDown") { setOpen(true); setActiveIndex((i) => Math.min(i + 1, options.length - 1)); }
    if (e.key === "ArrowUp") setActiveIndex((i) => Math.max(i - 1, 0));
    if (e.key === "Enter" && activeIndex >= 0) { onChange(options[activeIndex]); setOpen(false); }
    if (e.key === "Escape") setOpen(false);
  }

  return (
    <>
      <input
        role="combobox"
        aria-expanded={open}
        aria-controls="cb-listbox"
        aria-autocomplete="list"
        aria-activedescendant={activeIndex >= 0 ? "opt-" + activeIndex : undefined}
        onKeyDown={onKeyDown}
        onChange={(e) => { setOpen(true); /* filter options */ }}
      />
      {open && (
        <ul id="cb-listbox" role="listbox">
          {options.map((opt, i) => (
            <li
              key={opt}
              id={"opt-" + i}
              role="option"
              aria-selected={i === activeIndex}
            >
              {opt}
            </li>
          ))}
        </ul>
      )}
    </>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

Comboboxes are one of the highest-stakes widgets to hand-roll — the APG pattern itself went through several revisions because early versions had real bugs. The mature signal: "this is exactly where I reach for React Aria's useComboBox or Radix's combobox primitive rather than hand-rolling aria-activedescendant synchronization, because the edge cases (empty results, async loading, mouse-vs-keyboard highlight conflicts) are numerous and easy to get subtly wrong."

Q27. What is the difference between .focus() and :focus-visible, and when does the focus ring actually matter?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

.focus() is imperative JS that moves DOM focus to an element. :focus-visible is a CSS pseudo-class that matches only when the browser heuristically decides the focus ring should be shown — typically for keyboard navigation, not for a mouse click. This solves the old problem where teams either always showed an outline (annoying for mouse users clicking buttons) or removed it entirely with outline: none (breaking keyboard users) — :focus-visible gives keyboard users a ring without cluttering mouse interactions.

Detailed Answer

Before :focus-visible shipped broadly, the plain :focus pseudo-class matched on every focus event regardless of input modality, so many teams "fixed" the visual noise by disabling outlines globally — a widespread and serious WCAG 2.4.7 violation. :focus-visible encodes the browser's own heuristic (roughly: show it after keyboard interaction, form-control focus, or programmatic .focus() in most cases; suppress it after a mouse click on most non-form elements) so you get the right behavior without JS.

You should never fully remove focus indication — WCAG 2.4.7 requires it, and WCAG 2.2 adds 2.4.11 "Focus Appearance," which sets minimum size/contrast requirements for the ring itself. A common senior-level addition: style :focus minimally and reserve a strong, high-contrast ring for :focus-visible.

Code Example

/* No visual noise on a mouse click... */
button:focus {
  outline: none;
}
/* ...but a strong, WCAG 2.2-compliant ring for keyboard users */
button:focus-visible {
  outline: 3px solid #1f4e79;
  outline-offset: 2px;
}

// Imperative focus is orthogonal — it just moves DOM focus;
// :focus-visible still decides whether a ring renders for that move
inputRef.current.focus();

Senior Engineer Perspective

The historical context matters here: "teams used to disable outlines entirely because :focus fired on every mouse click too — :focus-visible made that entire tradeoff obsolete." Mention WCAG 2.2's new focus-appearance criterion (2.4.11) to show you're current on the spec, not just repeating 2018-era CSS advice.

Q28. How do you handle focus when content is removed from the DOM (e.g., deleting the currently focused row from a list)?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

When the element holding focus is unmounted, the browser silently moves focus to <body> — a keyboard/SR user is effectively dumped back to the top of the page with no announcement. The fix is to explicitly move focus before or immediately after removal to a sensible neighbor: the next item in the list, the previous item if it was the last one, or a stable container/heading if the list becomes empty.

Detailed Answer

The pattern: capture which index/id was focused, remove the item, then focus the item that took its place (same index) or the previous item if you deleted the last row, or an "empty state" message if the list is now empty. Doing this in a useEffect keyed off the list length, rather than inline in the delete handler, avoids racing with React's render commit.

This same problem shows up whenever conditionally-rendered content disappears: closing an accordion panel that had focus inside it, dismissing a toast that had a focused "Undo" button, or a tab panel unmounting on tab switch. The unifying rule: never let removal silently strand focus at <body>.

Code Example

function List({ items, onDelete }) {
  const refs = React.useRef({});
  const focusIndexRef = React.useRef(null);

  function handleDelete(id, index) {
    focusIndexRef.current = index;
    onDelete(id);
  }

  React.useEffect(() => {
    const idx = focusIndexRef.current;
    if (idx == null) return;
    // Focus the item that now occupies this index, or the previous one, or the empty state
    const target =
      refs.current[Math.min(idx, items.length - 1)] ?? refs.current["empty"];
    target?.focus();
    focusIndexRef.current = null;
  }, [items.length]);

  if (items.length === 0) {
    return <p tabIndex={-1} ref={(el) => (refs.current["empty"] = el)}>No items left.</p>;
  }

  return (
    <ul>
      {items.map((item, i) => (
        <li key={item.id}>
          <button
            ref={(el) => (refs.current[i] = el)}
            tabIndex={-1}
            onClick={() => handleDelete(item.id, i)}
          >
            Delete {item.name}
          </button>
        </li>
      ))}
    </ul>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

This is a bug most teams only discover once a screen reader user files a report — it's invisible in a sighted, mouse-driven QA pass because sighted users don't notice focus quietly landing on <body>. Naming it unprompted, and the "focus the neighbor, or the empty state" fix, is a strong senior signal.

Q29. What are common keyboard traps, and how do you detect and avoid them?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

A keyboard trap is any place a keyboard-only user can Tab into but cannot Tab (or Escape) out of — the opposite failure of an intentional modal trap, and a WCAG 2.1.2 violation. Classic culprits: third-party embeds (some rich-text editors, date pickers, or ad iframes) that capture Tab internally, custom widgets that intercept Tab expecting arrow-key navigation instead, and infinite-loop focus-wrapping logic with an off-by-one bug. Detect them by literally unplugging your mouse and tabbing through every flow.

Detailed Answer

Concrete sources seen in production:

  • A rich-text editor (e.g. an embedded iframe editor) that binds Tab to insert an actual tab character instead of letting focus leave, with no documented escape key.
  • A custom widget's onKeyDown calling e.preventDefault() on every key including Tab, intending only to handle arrows but accidentally swallowing Tab too.
  • A buggy focus-trap implementation (see Q24) that wraps Tab even outside the intended modal because the "am I inside the trap" check is wrong.
  • Auto-focus loops: a component that re-focuses itself in a useEffect on every render, fighting the user's attempt to Tab away.

The fix is almost always: never call preventDefault() on Tab unless you are deliberately implementing a bounded trap (modal) with a documented, working escape path, and always test third-party embeds for this specifically since you don't control their code.

Code Example

// BAD: intercepts every key, accidentally swallowing Tab
function onKeyDown(e) {
  e.preventDefault();          // trap! Tab never reaches the browser
  if (e.key === "ArrowDown") moveDown();
}

// GOOD: only intercept the keys you actually handle
function onKeyDown(e) {
  if (e.key === "ArrowDown") { e.preventDefault(); moveDown(); }
  if (e.key === "ArrowUp") { e.preventDefault(); moveUp(); }
  // Tab, Shift+Tab, Escape fall through untouched
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

Interviewers like hearing you test by literally disconnecting the mouse — it's the cheapest, highest-signal accessibility test that exists and catches keyboard traps immediately. Flagging that third-party embeds are a frequent, hard-to-fix source (you can't patch their internals) shows real production experience versus textbook knowledge.

Q30. How does visual focus order interact with CSS order, flex-direction, and portals?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

Tab order always follows DOM order, never visual/paint order. CSS properties like flex/grid order, flex-direction: row-reverse, or absolute positioning can visually reorder elements without touching the DOM — creating a mismatch where Tab jumps around the screen unpredictably relative to what's visually next. Portals (rendering into a different DOM subtree, e.g. document.body) can also detach an element's tab position from its visual/logical context entirely.

Detailed Answer

WCAG 2.4.3 (Focus Order) requires that the sequence in which elements receive focus preserves meaning and operability — in practice, that means visual order and Tab order should match for sighted keyboard users to reason about "what's next." The classic bug: a two-column responsive layout uses order: 2 on the sidebar to move it visually without changing markup — a Tab press jumps from the main content into the sidebar in an order a sighted user wouldn't predict from the layout, or vice versa on mobile where the columns visually stack differently than the DOM.

Portals compound this: a tooltip or dropdown portalled to document.body for stacking-context reasons is now a DOM sibling far from its trigger, so unless you explicitly manage focus (send it into the portal on open, restore it on close — see Q9), Tab order around it becomes essentially arbitrary relative to the trigger's visual position.

Code Example

/* BAD: visual order no longer matches DOM/tab order */
.sidebar { order: -1; }  /* moves visually first, but is still last in the DOM/tab order */

/* Prefer reordering in markup over CSS order when both visual and
   keyboard order need to match — or explicitly test and accept the mismatch
   only for content with no interactive elements */

Senior Engineer Perspective

The strongest answer: "I treat DOM order as the source of truth for both — if design needs a different visual arrangement, I reorder the markup first and reach for CSS order only as a last resort, and never on layouts containing interactive elements." Portals get a special callout because they're an easy blind spot even for teams who've otherwise nailed focus order.

Q31. What is the inert attribute, and how does it simplify focus management compared to manually walking an aria-hidden tree?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

inert is a native, boolean HTML attribute that makes an entire subtree unfocusable, unclickable, unselectable, and hidden from the accessibility tree — in one attribute. Before it existed (and still in older-browser fallbacks), teams had to manually walk every focusable descendant of the "background" and apply both aria-hidden="true" and tabIndex={-1}, then carefully restore the original tabIndex values afterward without clobbering ones that were already -1 for other reasons.

Detailed Answer

The manual approach is genuinely error-prone: you must snapshot each element's original tabIndex before overwriting it (some may already have been -1 deliberately), reapply aria-hidden only at the boundary (not on every descendant, or you fight the accessibility tree unnecessarily), and reverse all of it exactly when the modal closes — miss one step and you leave stray unfocusable or still-focusable elements behind. inert replaces all of that with a single attribute toggle that the browser guarantees behaves correctly, including automatically excluding the subtree from Ctrl+F find-in-page and text selection.

It's supported in all evergreen browsers as of 2026, so for modern targets it has fully replaced the "aria-hidden + tabindex walk" polyfill pattern that libraries like focus-trap-react used to implement by hand.

Code Example

// Old manual approach (fragile, easy to get wrong):
function setBackgroundInert(root, isInert) {
  const focusables = root.querySelectorAll('a, button, input, [tabindex]');
  focusables.forEach((el) => {
    if (isInert) {
      el.dataset.prevTabIndex = el.getAttribute("tabindex") ?? "";
      el.setAttribute("tabindex", "-1");
    } else {
      el.setAttribute("tabindex", el.dataset.prevTabIndex || "0");
    }
  });
  root.setAttribute("aria-hidden", String(isInert));
}

// Modern approach: one attribute
<div id="app-root" inert={isModalOpen || undefined}>
  {/* everything here becomes fully non-interactive and hidden from AT */}
</div>

Senior Engineer Perspective

Knowing the manual algorithm well enough to explain why it's error-prone (the tabIndex-snapshot problem specifically) while recommending the modern one-attribute solution is exactly the "know the fundamentals, use the platform" answer interviewers want across this whole chapter.

Q33. How do a screen reader's "browse mode" and "focus mode" affect keyboard handling in a custom widget?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

Windows screen readers (NVDA, JAWS) have two keyboard modes: browse mode, where the screen reader intercepts most keys itself (letting users navigate by heading, landmark, or element type with single-letter shortcuts) and focus/forms mode, where keystrokes pass straight through to the web page/widget. They auto-switch based on context — landing on a form field or an ARIA widget with the right role typically triggers focus mode — but a custom widget with the wrong role can leave the user stuck in browse mode, where your arrow key handlers never even fire.

Detailed Answer

This is why using the correct ARIA role from the APG matters beyond just "announcing correctly" — the role itself is often the signal the screen reader uses to decide whether to hand keystrokes to your JavaScript. A <div> with click handlers but no interactive role stays in browse mode forever; a properly-roled role="grid", role="listbox", or actual form control triggers focus mode reliably. This is a frequent, hard-to-diagnose bug: a widget "works" when tested with a mouse and even with plain Tab, but arrow-key navigation silently fails only under a screen reader because it never receives focus mode.

VoiceOver doesn't use this exact two-mode model (it has its own "Quick Nav" concept instead), which is another concrete reason cross-engine testing matters — a bug specific to NVDA/JAWS browse-mode interception won't reproduce on a Mac at all.

Code Example

// Missing role: stays in NVDA/JAWS browse mode, arrow keys never reach onKeyDown
<div className="grid" onKeyDown={handleGridKeys}>...</div>

// Correct role: triggers focus/forms mode reliably
<div role="grid" onKeyDown={handleGridKeys} tabIndex={0}>...</div>

Senior Engineer Perspective

This is a genuinely advanced, easy-to-miss detail: correct ARIA roles aren't just "announcement sugar" — for Windows screen readers they're the literal mechanism that decides whether your keyboard handlers ever receive events. Bringing this up unprompted is a strong "I've actually debugged NVDA-specific bugs" signal.

Q34. What are aria-atomic and aria-relevant, and how do they refine live-region announcements?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

aria-atomic="true" tells the screen reader to re-read the entire live region on any change, not just the changed fragment — useful when partial context is confusing (e.g. "5 results" alone means nothing without "found"). Without it, the default is to announce only the specific node that changed, which for a region built from several <span>s can produce a fragmented, hard-to-parse announcement.

Detailed Answer

Consider a region rendering <span>{count}</span> results found. Without aria-atomic, only the number's text node changed, so some screen readers announce just "5" — the user has no idea what changed. With aria-atomic="true", the whole region's text ("5 results found") is re-read as a unit, giving full context every time.

aria-relevant (default "additions text") narrows which mutation types count as "relevant" for the purposes of announcement — additions, removals, text changes, or all. A live log that only cares about new lines being appended would set aria-relevant="additions" so that removing old lines (e.g. to cap the log's length) doesn't also trigger a noisy "item removed" announcement.

Code Example

// Whole-region re-announcement for clarity
<div aria-live="polite" aria-atomic="true">
  {count} results found
</div>

// Only announce new lines being appended to a live log, ignore trimming old ones
<div aria-live="polite" aria-relevant="additions">
  {logLines.map((l) => <p key={l.id}>{l.text}</p>)}
</div>

Senior Engineer Perspective

This question tests whether you've actually listened to the announcements a live region produces, not just read that aria-live exists. The concrete "5" vs "5 results found" example is the kind of specific, testable detail that convinces an interviewer you've used a screen reader for real debugging.

Q35. Walk through how you'd manually test a React signup flow with a screen reader.

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Turn on NVDA (or VoiceOver), put the mouse away, and drive the entire flow by keyboard and ear: Tab through in DOM order confirming every control has a clear name and role, submit with validation errors present and confirm they're announced, fix errors and resubmit, confirm the success state or navigation is announced, and check that headings/landmarks let you jump directly to the form.

Detailed Answer

A concrete pass:

  1. Load the page fresh; confirm the page title and an initial heading are announced, confirming there's a sensible entry point.
  2. Use heading navigation (H in NVDA/JAWS, VO+Cmd+H in VoiceOver) to jump to the form's section heading directly.
  3. Tab through every field in order; for each, listen for name + role + current value/state (e.g. "Email, edit text" then "Password, edit text, required").
  4. Submit with empty/invalid fields; confirm errors are announced immediately (not silently shown only visually) and that focus lands somewhere useful (first invalid field or an error summary).
  5. Fix the fields and resubmit; confirm a success message or the post-submit navigation is announced, and that focus lands on the new page's heading.
  6. Zoom to 200% and re-test the critical path to confirm nothing overlaps or gets clipped.

The goal isn't finding every WCAG violation — automated tools do that more efficiently — it's confirming the experience is coherent: does the story the screen reader tells make sense end to end?

Senior Engineer Perspective

Interviewers use this to separate "I know the theory" from "I've done this." A strong answer references specific keyboard commands (heading navigation, landmark navigation) by name and explicitly separates what automated tools already cover from what only a live listen-through reveals — the coherence of the experience, not just the presence of attributes.

Q36. Why might the same code behave differently in Chrome+NVDA vs Safari+VoiceOver?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

A screen reader doesn't talk to the DOM directly — it talks to the browser's accessibility API (e.g. Windows UI Automation, macOS Accessibility API), and the browser is responsible for translating DOM + ARIA into that platform API. Different browsers implement that translation slightly differently (a known example: certain ARIA attributes' support historically varied between Chromium and WebKit), and screen readers themselves apply their own heuristics on top — so identical markup can genuinely produce a different experience depending on the browser/AT pairing.

Detailed Answer

The chain is: DOM + ARIA → browser accessibility tree → platform accessibility API → screen reader. Each arrow is a potential point of divergence. Historically, live-region timing, certain landmark roles, and some newer ARIA 1.2 roles have had inconsistent support across this chain, meaning a "correct" implementation per spec can still render imperfectly on one specific pairing. This is precisely why testing matrices for serious products specify a primary pairing (e.g. NVDA+Chrome as the CI-adjacent baseline) and a secondary spot-check pairing (VoiceOver+Safari) rather than trusting one combination.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The senior-level framing: "accessibility isn't purely a spec-compliance problem — it's an integration across four layers (markup, browser, platform API, AT), and any of those layers can introduce a discrepancy." That framing explains, without excuses, why "it works on my machine" is a weaker claim in accessibility testing than almost anywhere else in frontend work.

Q37. What is the accessibility tree, and how does it differ from the DOM tree?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

The accessibility tree is a parallel structure the browser derives from the DOM, containing only the semantic information assistive tech needs: each node's computed role, accessible name, description, states, and relevant relationships. It's smaller than the DOM — purely decorative or aria-hidden nodes are pruned — and it's what screen readers, not the DOM, actually read.

Detailed Answer

Every DOM node's presence in the accessibility tree, and its computed role/name, follows published algorithms (the "core-aam" and "accname" specs). Understanding this distinction explains several things at once: why display:none/visibility:hidden remove a node from the tree entirely (nothing to compute), why aria-hidden="true" prunes a whole subtree regardless of its visual state, and why changing an ARIA attribute can alter the tree without any visual change at all.

Both Chrome DevTools (the "Accessibility" pane) and Firefox's Accessibility Inspector let you view this tree directly — it's the single best tool for answering "what will actually be announced here" without opening a real screen reader.

Code Example

// DOM tree has 3 nodes here, but the accessibility tree has none for the icon

Q38. How do you debug an incorrect accessible name being announced?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Open the browser's Accessibility inspector (Chrome DevTools' Accessibility pane, or Firefox's), select the element, and read its computed "Name" field directly — this shows exactly what a screen reader will say without needing to run one. Then walk the accessible-name computation order backwards: is there an aria-labelledby pointing at the wrong (or an empty) element? Is aria-label stale text left over from a refactor? Is a wrapping <label> accidentally including unrelated sibling text?

Detailed Answer

Common root causes, roughly in order of frequency:

  • An aria-labelledby id typo or reference to an element that got renamed/ removed during a refactor — it silently resolves to nothing, and the accessible name falls through to the next priority level (often empty or unhelpful visible text).
  • An icon-only button whose aria-label was hardcoded once and never updated when the button's behavior changed (e.g. label still says "Play" after the icon/action became "Pause").
  • A <label> wrapping more than intended — e.g. wrapping both a checkbox and an unrelated helper link, so the checkbox's name includes the link text.
  • Whitespace-only or duplicate aria-label/aria-labelledby pairs where both are present and browsers resolve one over the other in a way the author didn't expect (labelledby always wins).

Testing Library's getByRole(role, { name }) queries double as a regression guard here — if a test that previously passed with a given accessible name starts failing, you've caught the exact bug a screen reader user would hit.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The strongest signal is naming the priority order and debugging it like a lookup chain — "labelledby wins, so I check that reference first" — rather than guessing. Mentioning that Testing Library's role/name queries catch these regressions automatically in CI ties this back to a practical, already-in-place safety net rather than a purely manual process.

SECTION 8 - ACCESSIBLE FORMS DEEP DIVE

Q40. How do you announce validation errors so they aren't missed, without being obnoxious?

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: Extremely High

Short Answer

On submit, move focus to either the first invalid field or an error summary at the top of the form — this is what actually guarantees the error gets heard, because it puts the screen-reader cursor right where the message lives. Use role="alert" (implicitly assertive) sparingly for the summary itself, but avoid making every individual field's error assertive, or a screen reader user gets interrupted repeatedly as they fix each one.

Detailed Answer

Two valid patterns, both real-world common:

  • Focus-first-invalid-field — simplest, works well for short forms. Set focus to the first invalid input on submit; its aria-describedby error text is read automatically because focus landed there.
  • Error summary — for longer forms, render a summary block at the top (role="alert" or a focused <div tabIndex={-1}> with a heading like "3 errors found") listing every error as a link/anchor to its field. This scales far better than field-by-field focus for forms with many fields, and gives the user an overview before diving in.

Whichever pattern you pick, never rely on a purely visual error indicator with no focus movement and no live region — a screen reader user who has already tabbed past the invalid field on their way to Submit will never learn it failed.

Code Example

function Form({ errors, onSubmit }) {
  const summaryRef = React.useRef(null);
  React.useEffect(() => {
    if (errors.length > 0) summaryRef.current?.focus();
  }, [errors]);

  return (
    <form onSubmit={onSubmit}>
      {errors.length > 0 && (
        <div ref={summaryRef} tabIndex={-1} role="alert">
          <h2>{errors.length} error{errors.length > 1 ? "s" : ""} found</h2>
          <ul>
            {errors.map((e) => (
              <li key={e.field}><a href={"#" + e.field}>{e.message}</a></li>
            ))}
          </ul>
        </div>
      )}
      {/* fields */}
    </form>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The distinction interviewers listen for: focus movement is what actually guarantees the announcement, not the live region alone — a live region only helps if the user's attention/cursor is anywhere reasonably nearby. The error-summary pattern is the more senior answer for anything beyond a 2-3 field form because it front-loads context instead of forcing a linear re-discovery of each problem.

Q41. What's the right way to mark a field as required — visually and programmatically?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Use the native required attribute (it's understood by assistive tech and triggers built-in browser validation), and pair it with a visible textual indicator — "Email (required)" or a marked asterisk that's explained once at the top of the form ("* indicates a required field") — rather than a bare asterisk with no legend, which conveys nothing to a screen reader or anyone unfamiliar with the convention.

Detailed Answer

A bare * character has no inherent meaning to assistive tech, and even visually it's ambiguous without a legend. The reliable pattern: put "(required)" in the visible label text itself, or add a legend once ("Fields marked * are required") and ensure the asterisk is wrapped in an element with an accessible description (aria-label="required") rather than being a plain, unannounced glyph.

aria-required="true" exists for custom widgets that can't use the native attribute (a role="combobox" built from a <div>, for instance) but should never replace required on a real <input>/<select>/<textarea> — the native attribute gets you built-in validation UX and constraint-validation API integration for free.

Code Example

  Email <span aria-hidden="true">*</span><span className="sr-only">(required)</span>
</label>

Senior Engineer Perspective

The trap: a candidate who says "just add an asterisk" without mentioning it needs a legend or an accessible description hasn't actually thought about how a screen reader parses a lone glyph. Native required plus a properly-announced visual convention is the complete answer.

Q42. How do you build an accessible multi-step form / wizard?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

Treat each step like a mini route change (see Q10/Q72): move focus to the new step's heading when it appears, announce progress ("Step 2 of 4") in a live region or as part of that heading, keep a visible and accessible step indicator, and ensure Back/Next are real buttons with clear names rather than icon-only arrows. Validate the current step before allowing Next, using the same error-announcement pattern as any other form (Q40).

Detailed Answer

Wizards fail accessibility most often by silently swapping step content without moving focus — a screen reader user who submitted step 1 has no idea step 2 appeared unless focus or a live region tells them. The fix mirrors SPA route-change focus management: give each step a heading, move focus there (tabIndex={-1} + .focus()) when the step changes, and include the step number/progress in that heading's text so it's announced in one shot ("Step 2 of 4: Shipping address").

Also worth calling out: a progress indicator implemented purely with styled dots/bars needs text or an aria-label conveying the same info ("Step 2 of 4") — a purely visual progress bar communicates nothing to a screen reader.

  const headingRef = React.useRef(null);
  React.useEffect(() => { headingRef.current?.focus(); }, [step]);

  return (
    <section aria-labelledby="step-heading">
      <h2 id="step-heading" ref={headingRef} tabIndex={-1}>
        Step {step} of {total}: {title}
      </h2>
      {children}
    </section>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The unifying insight interviewers want: "a wizard step change is just a client-side route change wearing a different name — it needs the exact same focus-management fix." Recognizing that pattern reuse across seemingly different UI (SPA routing, wizards, tab panels) is a strong signal of transferring principles rather than memorizing isolated recipes.

Q43. How do you handle async/debounced validation without confusing screen reader users?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

Debounced/async validation (e.g. "checking username availability...") needs its own aria-live="polite" status region separate from the final error message, so the "checking..." state and the eventual "taken"/"available" result are both announced in sequence rather than one overwriting the other silently or firing multiple rapid announcements as the user types.

Detailed Answer

The failure mode: if you update the same live region text on every keystroke while debouncing, a screen reader can queue up a burst of "checking... checking... checking..." announcements once the debounce timer finally fires and settles, or worse, updates so fast some SRs drop intermediate states silently. The fix is to only push a change into the live region at meaningful transitions — start checking, finished checking (success or failure) — not on every keystroke, and to set aria-busy="true" on the region while the check is in flight so assistive tech can defer.

Also mark the field's aria-invalid only once validation has actually settled — flipping it true/false rapidly while typing is itself a source of noisy, premature announcements.

Code Example

function UsernameField() {
  const [status, setStatus] = React.useState("idle"); // idle | checking | available | taken
  const debouncedCheck = useDebouncedCallback(async (value) => {
    setStatus("checking");
    const ok = await checkAvailability(value);
    setStatus(ok ? "available" : "taken");
  }, 400);

  return (
    <>
      <label htmlFor="u">Username</label>
      <input
        id="u"
        onChange={(e) => debouncedCheck(e.target.value)}
        aria-invalid={status === "taken"}
        aria-describedby="u-status"
      />
      <p id="u-status" aria-live="polite" aria-busy={status === "checking"}>
        {status === "checking" && "Checking availability…"}
        {status === "available" && "Username available."}
        {status === "taken" && "That username is taken."}
      </p>
    </>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The senior answer explicitly separates "when to update the DOM" from "when to update the live region," and uses aria-busy to bridge the gap during the async wait — a detail most engineers never think through until a real user complains about a stuttering announcement.

Q44. What's the difference between aria-invalid and native :invalid / :user-invalid?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

:invalid is a CSS pseudo-class matched by the browser's built-in constraint validation (based on required, pattern, type, etc.) — it's purely a styling hook and carries no information to assistive tech. aria-invalid="true" is the attribute that actually tells a screen reader "this field currently fails validation" — you must set it yourself in your validation logic; the browser does not sync it from :invalid automatically.

Detailed Answer

A common mistake: styling errors with :invalid and assuming the accessibility story is covered because "the browser knows it's invalid." It doesn't communicate that to assistive tech — :invalid is CSS-only. You still must explicitly set aria-invalid (usually from your form library's validation state) and connect the error text via aria-describedby.

:user-invalid (newer, gaining support) improves on :invalid by only matching after the user has actually interacted with the field, avoiding the classic "every required field is red before I've even typed anything" bad UX — but again, it's styling-only and doesn't touch the accessibility tree.

Code Example

/* Styling only — no effect on what a screen reader announces */
input:user-invalid { border-color: #c0392b; }

// The line that actually matters for accessibility:
<input aria-invalid={hasError} aria-describedby={hasError ? "err" : undefined} />

Senior Engineer Perspective

The one-line answer that scores here: "CSS pseudo-classes style, ARIA attributes announce — they're separate systems, and only one of them costs you a screen-reader bug if you forget it." A precise, worked-example answer beats a vague "they're related" response.

Q45. How do you make a custom select/dropdown as accessible as a native <select>?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Genuinely matching a native <select>'s accessibility is hard: it gets typeahead-by-letter, full keyboard support, correct mobile behavior, and screen-reader familiarity entirely for free. A custom version needs role="combobox"/listbox wiring, roving focus or aria-activedescendant, arrow-key movement, Home/End, typeahead search, and Escape-to-close — all hand-built. The honest recommendation: only build a custom select when the native one truly can't meet a design requirement (rich option content, multi-select with custom rendering), and prefer a tested primitive (Radix Select, React Aria) over a from-scratch build.

Detailed Answer

What you have to reimplement, one by one:

  • Typeahead — typing "b" jumps to the first option starting with B; typing quickly cycles among matches.
  • Keyboard model — Enter/Space opens, arrows move highlight, Enter selects, Escape closes without selecting, Home/End jump to first/last option.
  • Mobile behavior — native selects use the OS's own picker UI on mobile; a custom dropdown must reimplement something comparably usable with touch.
  • Accessible name and value announcement — the trigger button must announce both its label and its current selected value, updating live as selection changes.
  • Form integration — native selects participate in form submission and constraint validation automatically; a custom one needs a hidden native input or manual wiring to work with plain HTML forms.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The mature answer leads with the cost-benefit: "the native select is free and correct — I only reach for a custom one when the design genuinely requires something HTML can't do, and even then I use Radix/React Aria's Select rather than building the combobox model myself, because typeahead plus keyboard plus mobile plus form-integration is a lot of surface area to get exactly right."

Q46. How should file upload and drag-and-drop be made accessible?

Q47. How do you handle grouped checkboxes vs a single multi-select — what are the accessibility implications?

The decision hinges on scale and interaction model. SECTION 9 - COLOR CONTRAST & VISUAL ACCESSIBILITY

Q48. Walk through the WCAG contrast ratio formula — how is it actually calculated?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

WCAG contrast is (L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05), where L1 is the relative luminance of the lighter color and L2 the darker, and both colors are converted to relative luminance first — a formula that weights each RGB channel differently (green contributes most, blue least) to approximate human perceived brightness, then linearizes the sRGB gamma. The ratio ranges from 1:1 (identical) to 21:1 (pure black on pure white).

Detailed Answer

The steps: convert each sRGB channel (0-255) to 0-1, apply the sRGB-to-linear gamma correction (values ≤0.03928 divide by 12.92, others use a power curve), then combine with the fixed luminance weights 0.2126*R + 0.7152*G + 0.0722*B. That weighted sum is relative luminance. Plug the lighter and darker luminance into the ratio formula above. You virtually never need to hand-calculate this — DevTools' color picker, the axe extension, and online contrast checkers all do it — but understanding why green dominates the formula (human eyes are most sensitive to green) helps you reason about tricky edge cases like a green-on-yellow combination that "looks" high contrast but measures poorly.

Code Example

function luminance([r, g, b]) {
  const [rl, gl, bl] = [r, g, b].map((c) => {
    c /= 255;
    return c <= 0.03928 ? c / 12.92 : Math.pow((c + 0.055) / 1.055, 2.4);
  });
  return 0.2126 * rl + 0.7152 * gl + 0.0722 * bl;
}

function contrastRatio(rgbA, rgbB) {
  const [l1, l2] = [luminance(rgbA), luminance(rgbB)].sort((a, b) => b - a);
  return (l1 + 0.05) / (l2 + 0.05);
}

contrastRatio([89, 89, 89], [255, 255, 255]); // #595959 on white ≈ 7.0 (passes AAA)

Senior Engineer Perspective

You don't need to recite the formula from memory in an interview, but knowing the shape of it — luminance-weighted, gamma-corrected, asymmetric across channels — signals you understand why some intuitively "high contrast" color pairs (bright yellow on white, certain blue-on-black combos) can still fail, rather than treating contrast checking as an opaque tool you run without understanding.

Q49. How do you support prefers-reduced-motion, and why does it matter beyond "nice to have"?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

prefers-reduced-motion is a media query reflecting an OS-level accessibility setting for users with vestibular disorders, migraines, or motion sensitivity, for whom large animations, parallax, and auto-playing motion can cause real physical symptoms (dizziness, nausea) — not just annoyance. Respecting it means replacing or removing non-essential motion (fade instead of slide/zoom, no auto-scroll/parallax, disabling autoplay carousels) when the query matches reduce.

Detailed Answer

This is a genuine medical-accessibility need (WCAG 2.3.3, AAA, but treated as a practical AA-adjacent expectation by most teams), not aesthetic preference. Vestibular disorders can make large-scale animation (parallax scrolling, zooming transitions, spinning loaders that fill the viewport) genuinely nauseating or disorienting. The fix is almost always CSS-only: wrap non-essential transitions/animations in the media query and fall back to an instant or minimal-motion alternative.

In React, the same preference is readable via window.matchMedia("(prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)") for cases where motion is driven by JS (e.g. a physics-based drag library, or deciding whether to run a Framer Motion animation at all) rather than pure CSS.

Code Example

/* CSS: the most common and complete fix */
.modal {
  transition: transform 300ms ease, opacity 300ms ease;
}
@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce) {
  .modal {
    transition: opacity 150ms ease;  /* fade only, no transform/slide */
  }
  * {
    animation-duration: 0.001ms !important;
    animation-iteration-count: 1 !important;
  }
}

// JS: gate a library-driven animation
function usePrefersReducedMotion() {
  const [reduced, setReduced] = React.useState(
    () => window.matchMedia("(prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)").matches
  );
  React.useEffect(() => {
    const mq = window.matchMedia("(prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)");
    const onChange = () => setReduced(mq.matches);
    mq.addEventListener("change", onChange);
    return () => mq.removeEventListener("change", onChange);
  }, []);
  return reduced;
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The framing that lands: this isn't a design-preference toggle, it's an accommodation for a real physiological condition — treating it with the same seriousness as color contrast or keyboard support is what separates a senior answer from "oh yeah I've heard of that media query." Bonus: note it's a blanket CSS catch-all (* { animation-duration: 0.001ms !important }) that many teams add as a safety net alongside targeted per-component fixes.

Q50. How do you handle forced-colors mode / Windows High Contrast Mode in CSS?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

Forced-colors mode (Windows High Contrast, matched via @media (forced-colors: active)) overrides your author-defined colors with a small, user-chosen palette (background, text, links, buttons, etc.) for users who need maximum contrast — your custom CSS colors are largely ignored. The main engineering risk is content that relies purely on background-color or box-shadow to convey meaning (a colored "selected" state with no border, an icon rendered as a CSS background-image), since forced-colors mode can strip those away entirely, leaving no visible signal at all.

Detailed Answer

In forced-colors mode, the browser substitutes a limited set of system colors (CanvasText, ButtonFace, LinkText, etc. — the CSS system-color keywords) for your custom palette, specifically to guarantee sufficient contrast for users who've opted into it at the OS level. This is good for text, but it means:

  • A "selected" list item styled only with a subtle background tint may lose that tint entirely — use a border or an icon as a redundant, forced-colors-safe signal.
  • CSS background-image icons often disappear (they're not text, so they're not guaranteed contrast) — prefer inline SVG or real <img> content for meaningful icons, and use forced-color-adjust: none sparingly and deliberately when you must preserve a specific graphic's original colors (e.g. a brand logo).
  • Test with the forced-colors media query directly rather than assuming your dark-mode styles are equivalent — they solve a different problem.

Code Example

/* Add a border as a forced-colors-safe redundant signal for "selected" */
.option[aria-selected="true"] {
  background: #eef4fb;               /* may be overridden in forced-colors mode */
  border: 2px solid transparent;
}
@media (forced-colors: active) {
  .option[aria-selected="true"] {
    border-color: Highlight;         /* a forced-colors system color keyword */
  }
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

Few frontend engineers test forced-colors mode at all, so bringing it up unprompted — especially the "meaning conveyed only by a background tint disappears" failure mode — is a strong differentiator. The correct instinct is the same one from Q12 (don't rely on color alone), just applied to an OS-level override you don't fully control.

Q51. What are prefers-contrast and prefers-color-scheme, and how do you use them together?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

prefers-color-scheme (light/dark) reflects the user's OS theme preference. prefers-contrast (more/less/custom/no-preference) is a separate, newer preference specifically about contrast intensity, independent of theme — a user can want dark mode and increased contrast simultaneously. Treating them as one dimension (assuming dark mode = high contrast) misses users who want either combination independently.

Detailed Answer

These are orthogonal axes: theme (light/dark) is about aesthetic/eye-strain preference, while contrast preference is specifically about the intensity of the contrast between foreground and background regardless of theme. A robust design system defines both independently — a "high contrast light" and "high contrast dark" variant, not just "dark = automatically high contrast," which is a common but incorrect assumption.

Code Example

:root {
  --text: #333;
  --bg: #fff;
}
@media (prefers-color-scheme: dark) {
  :root { --text: #eee; --bg: #1a1a1a; }
}
@media (prefers-contrast: more) {
  :root { --text: #000; --bg: #fff; }         /* max contrast, independent of theme */
}
@media (prefers-color-scheme: dark) and (prefers-contrast: more) {
  :root { --text: #fff; --bg: #000; }         /* both preferences honored together */
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The nuance that separates a thoughtful answer: explicitly naming that these two media queries are independent axes, not one derived from the other. Most teams that only support prefers-color-scheme are quietly failing users who specifically opted into higher contrast at the OS level regardless of theme.

Q52. How do you ensure focus indicators meet WCAG 2.2's new focus-appearance criterion (2.4.11)?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

WCAG 2.2's 2.4.11 (AA) requires a focus indicator with a minimum area/perimeter thickness (roughly equivalent to a 2px solid border around the whole component), at least 3:1 contrast against both the unfocused and adjacent colors, and it must not be obscured by other content. In practice: a thin 1px outline in a low-contrast color that used to "pass" visually often fails 2.4.11 outright — favor a thick, high-contrast outline with a visible offset.

Detailed Answer

2.4.11 is new relative to 2.1 and tightens what "has a visible focus indicator" (2.4.7, the older, vaguer criterion) actually means quantitatively — it specifies a minimum area (an indicator that's at least as thick as a 2 CSS-pixel-wide perimeter around the smallest bounding box of the component) and a minimum 3:1 contrast ratio against adjacent colors in both focused and unfocused states. It also explicitly disallows a focus indicator that gets clipped or hidden by overflow:hidden containers or sticky headers — a real bug in scrollable panels where a focused element's ring gets cut off at the viewport edge.

Code Example

/* Meets 2.4.11: thick, high-contrast, offset outline */
:focus-visible {
  outline: 3px solid #1f4e79;   /* thick + verify 3:1 against surrounding colors */
  outline-offset: 2px;
}

/* Watch for containers that clip the ring — a 2.4.11 failure */
.scroll-panel {
  overflow: auto;   /* fine */
  /* avoid overflow:hidden directly around focusable elements without extra padding */
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

Being current on WCAG 2.2 specifically (versus only knowing 2.1) is a genuine differentiator in 2026 interviews — many senior engineers still cite only the older, vaguer "must have a visible focus indicator" rule. Mentioning the clipping/overflow failure mode shows you've hit this in a real scrollable UI, not just read the spec text.

Q53. What text-resizing / zoom requirements does WCAG impose, and how do you test for them?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

WCAG 1.4.4 (AA) requires text to be resizable up to 200% without loss of content or functionality using only the browser's standard zoom — no custom "increase font size" button required. 1.4.10 (AA) additionally requires content to reflow at 400% zoom (equivalent to a 320px-wide viewport) without needing horizontal scrolling for anything except things that are inherently 2D (data tables, images, wide code blocks). Test by zooming the browser to 200% and 400% and checking nothing is clipped, overlapping, or requires horizontal scroll.

Detailed Answer

Common failures at 200-400% zoom: fixed-height containers that clip overflowing text instead of growing, text set in pixel units combined with overflow:hidden on a parent (some teams still use hardcoded pixel heights instead of allowing content to reflow), sticky headers that consume so much vertical space at high zoom that content below becomes unreachable, and horizontal-scroll-inducing fixed-width layouts that weren't tested at 320px-equivalent width.

The practical test procedure: zoom to 200% and confirm no text is cut off and everything remains operable; zoom to 400% (or resize the viewport to 320px wide) and confirm the layout reflows to a single column without horizontal scrolling except for legitimately 2D content like data tables (which get an explicit exception in 1.4.10) or wide preformatted code.

Code Example

/* BAD: fixed height clips text as it grows with zoom/font-size increases */
.card-title { height: 24px; overflow: hidden; }

/* GOOD: let content define its own height */
.card-title { min-height: 24px; }

/* Use rem for font sizes so browser zoom and OS text-size settings both work */
body { font-size: 1rem; }        /* not 16px hardcoded in odd places */

Senior Engineer Perspective

The two-criteria distinction (1.4.4 zoom-to-200% vs 1.4.10 reflow-at-400%/320px) is a detail many engineers blur together. Naming both, and the specific "data tables are exempted from the no-horizontal-scroll rule" nuance, signals real spec familiarity rather than a vague "make sure zoom works" answer.

Q54. How do you handle iconography and non-text content contrast (WCAG 1.4.11)?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

WCAG 1.4.11 ("Non-text Contrast," AA) requires a 3:1 contrast ratio for meaningful graphical objects — icons that convey information or state, input borders, focus indicators, chart lines/segments — against their immediate background. It's easy to miss because most contrast tooling historically focused on text; a beautifully-designed but low-contrast line icon (a thin light-gray checkmark on white) can pass every text-contrast check while failing 1.4.11 outright.

Detailed Answer

What counts as "meaningful" for 1.4.11: icons that stand in for or supplement text (a trash-can delete icon, a status checkmark/x), form-control borders (so users can tell where an input field's boundary is), and UI state indicators (a toggle switch's on/off visual). It does not apply to purely decorative graphics or logos, and text itself is covered by the separate 1.4.3 text-contrast criterion, not this one.

Practical audit approach: identify every icon/border/indicator that communicates something functional, then check its contrast against the immediate background it sits on (not the overall page background) — a light input border on a white card inside a light-gray page can pass against the card but still needs checking at that specific boundary.

Code Example

/* BAD: light gray border barely visible against a white input on a white card */
input { border: 1px solid #e0e0e0; }  /* often < 3:1 against white */

/* GOOD: meets 3:1 for a UI component boundary */
input { border: 1px solid #767676; }  /* ~4.5:1 against white */

Senior Engineer Perspective

Most engineers can recite the 4.5:1 text-contrast number but blank on 1.4.11 entirely — naming it and giving the "thin gray input border" example as a concrete real-world failure is a strong, specific signal of design-system-level accessibility experience rather than page-level knowledge only.

No — they're two distinct WCAG success criteria with different thresholds. Text contrast (1.4.3) requires 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. Graphical/UI-component contrast (1.4.11) requires only 3:1, full stop, regardless of size, because icons and borders are judged by a different visual-perception standard than reading continuous text.

SECTION 10 - ACCESSIBLE COMPONENT IMPLEMENTATION PATTERNS

Q56. Build an accessible dropdown/menu from scratch — what are the required pieces?

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

A trigger <button> with aria-haspopup="menu" and aria-expanded, a role="menu" container of role="menuitem"s using roving tabindex, arrow keys to move between items, Enter/Space to activate, Escape to close and return focus to the trigger, and clicking outside also closes it. Home/End jump to first/last item; typeahead-by-letter is a nice-to-have.

Detailed Answer

The full checklist, matching the APG menu-button pattern:

  • Trigger — real <button>, aria-haspopup="menu", aria-expanded synced to open state, aria-controls pointing at the menu's id.
  • Opening — Enter/Space/ArrowDown on the trigger opens the menu and moves focus to the first (or last, for ArrowUp) item.
  • Menu — role="menu", items are role="menuitem" using roving tabindex; ArrowDown/Up move between items, Home/End jump to ends.
  • Activation — Enter or Space on a menuitem activates it and closes the menu, returning focus to the trigger.
  • Closing — Escape closes without activating; clicking/tapping outside also closes; focus always returns to the trigger, never left stranded.

Code Example

function Menu({ items, onSelect }) {
  const [open, setOpen] = React.useState(false);
  const [active, setActive] = React.useState(0);
  const triggerRef = React.useRef(null);
  const itemRefs = React.useRef([]);

  function openMenu(focusLast = false) {
    setOpen(true);
    setActive(focusLast ? items.length - 1 : 0);
  }
  React.useEffect(() => { if (open) itemRefs.current[active]?.focus(); }, [open, active]);

  function onMenuKeyDown(e) {
    if (e.key === "ArrowDown") setActive((i) => (i + 1) % items.length);
    if (e.key === "ArrowUp") setActive((i) => (i - 1 + items.length) % items.length);
    if (e.key === "Enter" || e.key === " ") { onSelect(items[active]); close(); }
    if (e.key === "Escape") close();
  }
  function close() { setOpen(false); triggerRef.current?.focus(); }

  return (
    <>
      <button
        ref={triggerRef}
        aria-haspopup="menu"
        aria-expanded={open}
        aria-controls="menu"
        onClick={() => (open ? close() : openMenu())}
        onKeyDown={(e) => { if (e.key === "ArrowDown") { e.preventDefault(); openMenu(); } }}
      >
        Actions
      </button>
      {open && (
        <ul id="menu" role="menu" onKeyDown={onMenuKeyDown}>
          {items.map((item, i) => (
            <li
              key={item}
              role="menuitem"
              tabIndex={-1}
              ref={(el) => (itemRefs.current[i] = el)}
              onClick={() => { onSelect(item); close(); }}
            >
              {item}
            </li>
          ))}
        </ul>
      )}
    </>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

Interviewers grade this on completeness of the checklist more than the code itself — naming Home/End and "focus always returns to the trigger" without prompting is a strong signal. The honest closer: "in production I'd reach for Radix's DropdownMenu or React Aria's useMenu rather than maintain this by hand."

Q57. Build an accessible accordion — roles, states, and keyboard model?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Each accordion header is a real <button> (often wrapped in an <h3> for outline purposes) with aria-expanded reflecting open state and aria-controls pointing at its panel; the panel itself carries role="region" with aria-labelledby back to the header. The APG keyboard model adds Up/Down to move between headers and Home/End to jump to the first/last — but plain Tab between headers is also an acceptable, simpler baseline many implementations use.

Detailed Answer

Structurally, an accordion is a set of headers (usually <h3> for a correct document outline) each wrapping a <button> that toggles a sibling panel. Critical wiring: aria-expanded must update with real state (not be hardcoded, echoing the Q21 anti-pattern), and each panel needs role="region" + aria-labelledby only if there are enough panels to warrant them showing up in the landmarks list — for a long FAQ accordion, tagging every panel as a region can clutter that list, so many implementations reserve region for accordions with just a few, meaningful sections.

Code Example

function AccordionItem({ id, title, children, isOpen, onToggle }) {
  return (
    <>
      <h3>
        <button
          id={"header-" + id}
          aria-expanded={isOpen}
          aria-controls={"panel-" + id}
          onClick={onToggle}
        >
          {title}
        </button>
      </h3>
      <div
        id={"panel-" + id}
        role="region"
        aria-labelledby={"header-" + id}
        hidden={!isOpen}
      >
        {children}
      </div>
    </>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

A pragmatic senior answer distinguishes "must-have" (real button, synced aria-expanded, correct heading level) from "nice-to-have" (Up/Down/Home/End navigation) — plain Tab-between-headers with correct semantics is a legitimate, still-WCAG- conformant baseline, and knowing where the line sits avoids over-engineering a simple FAQ widget.

Q58. Build an accessible toast/notification system that's both visible and properly announced?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Render a single, permanently-mounted aria-live region (see Q13) and push toast messages into it as they appear, using polite for routine confirmations and assertive only for urgent/error toasts. Visually, toasts also need enough display duration for a screen-reader announcement to finish (don't auto-dismiss on a fixed short timer that outruns speech), and any action inside a toast (like "Undo") must be reachable by keyboard before it disappears.

Detailed Answer

Common toast accessibility failures: auto-dismissing after a fixed 3 seconds regardless of message length, so a longer announcement gets cut off mid-speech for screen reader users, or a mouse-only "Undo" link inside a toast that a keyboard user can never Tab to before it disappears. The fix: base the auto-dismiss timer on message length (or, better, pause the timer while the toast has focus or on hover/focus-within), and if a toast contains an action, seriously consider not auto-dismissing it at all until the user interacts or dismisses it manually.

Queueing multiple toasts also needs care: if several arrive in a burst, dumping all of them into one aria-live="assertive" region back-to-back creates a jarring, unintelligible stream — throttle or queue them so each is announced with a sensible gap.

Code Example

function ToastRegion({ toasts }) {
  // permanently mounted; only its children change
  return (
    <div aria-live="polite" aria-atomic="false" className="toast-region">
      {toasts.map((t) => (
        <div key={t.id} role={t.type === "error" ? "alert" : "status"}>
          {t.message}
          {t.action && <button onClick={t.action.onClick}>{t.action.label}</button>}
        </div>
      ))}
    </div>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The detail that impresses: tying auto-dismiss duration to message length (or pausing on focus/hover) rather than a flat timer — a subtle but very real bug that only surfaces once you actually listen to a screen reader race against a disappearing toast.

Q59. Build an accessible tooltip — what's wrong with using the title attribute alone?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

The native title attribute is a poor tooltip mechanism: it's mouse-hover-only (no keyboard trigger), has an inconsistent, often long delay, isn't reliably exposed to screen readers at all, and can't be styled. An accessible custom tooltip instead shows on both hover and keyboard focus of its trigger, uses aria-describedby to link the trigger to the tooltip's text, and dismisses on Escape or when focus/hover leaves.

Detailed Answer

The APG tooltip pattern: the tooltip element itself typically has role="tooltip", the trigger references it via aria-describedby (supplementary description, not the accessible name), and visibility is driven by both :hover and :focus — critically, a keyboard user tabbing to the trigger must see the tooltip too, which title never provides. Escape should dismiss the tooltip without also moving focus away or closing an enclosing dialog (a real implementation trap when tooltips live inside modals).

A common mistake: making the tooltip the only place critical information lives (e.g. only revealing the full meaning of a truncated table header via tooltip) — this fails for touch-only users who have no hover, so critical info always needs a non-tooltip fallback.

Code Example

function Tooltip({ label, children }) {
  const [visible, setVisible] = React.useState(false);
  const id = React.useId();
  return (
    <span
      onMouseEnter={() => setVisible(true)}
      onMouseLeave={() => setVisible(false)}
      onFocus={() => setVisible(true)}     {/* keyboard trigger — title never does this */}
      onBlur={() => setVisible(false)}
      onKeyDown={(e) => { if (e.key === "Escape") setVisible(false); }}
    >
      {React.cloneElement(children, { "aria-describedby": visible ? id : undefined })}
      {visible && <span role="tooltip" id={id}>{label}</span>}
    </span>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The line that lands: "title is keyboard-inaccessible and inconsistently exposed — a real tooltip has to show on focus, not just hover." Flagging the touch-device fallback (no hover at all) shows you're thinking beyond the desktop-mouse-and-keyboard-only test matrix.

Q60. Build an accessible carousel/slider — what makes carousels notoriously hard to get right?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

Carousels are hard because they combine several accessibility challenges at once: auto-advancing content (a moving-target problem for screen reader/low-vision users, and a WCAG 2.2.2 "pause, stop, hide" requirement), keyboard-operable prev/next/pagination controls, correct announcement of "slide 2 of 5," and avoiding aria-hidden duplicate slides confusing the accessibility tree. Many accessibility experts recommend avoiding auto-advancing carousels entirely in favor of user-controlled ones.

Detailed Answer

Requirements for a genuinely accessible carousel:

  • Pause/stop control — WCAG 2.2.2 requires any auto-advancing content lasting more than 5 seconds to have a visible, keyboard-reachable pause mechanism.
  • Structure — role="region" with aria-roledescription="carousel" wrapping the whole thing, each slide as aria-roledescription="slide" with a label like "2 of 5".
  • Off-screen slides hidden from AT — inactive slides need aria-hidden="true" (and inert) so a screen reader doesn't read content that isn't currently visible, while the current slide stays exposed.
  • Keyboard controls — real, labeled prev/next buttons and, ideally, pagination dots that are real buttons with clear names ("Go to slide 3").
  • No motion-sickness triggers — respect prefers-reduced-motion for the slide transition itself.

Code Example

<div role="region" aria-roledescription="carousel" aria-label="Featured products">
  <button onClick={pause}>{isPlaying ? "Pause" : "Play"}</button>
  {slides.map((s, i) => (
    <div
      key={s.id}
      role="group"
      aria-roledescription="slide"
      aria-label={`${i + 1} of ${slides.length}`}
      aria-hidden={i !== current}
      inert={i !== current || undefined}
    >
      {s.content}
    </div>
  ))}
  <button onClick={prev} aria-label="Previous slide">‹</button>
  <button onClick={next} aria-label="Next slide">›</button>
</div>

Senior Engineer Perspective

The most senior answer here is actually pushback: "carousels are one of the least accessible common UI patterns, and my first recommendation is usually to challenge whether the product actually needs auto-advancing motion at all — a static grid or a user-driven tab-like selector is often both more accessible and better for conversion." Knowing when not to build the pattern is as valuable as knowing how.

Q61. Build an accessible rating/star-picker widget?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Low

Short Answer

Model it as a role="radiogroup" of role="radio" stars (you're picking exactly one value from a fixed set), each with a clear accessible name ("3 stars"), using roving tabindex and arrow keys to change the value, Space/click to confirm. Avoid a purely visual-only star display with click handlers and no underlying semantics — a screen reader user needs to hear the current and selectable values, not just see filled/unfilled icons.

Detailed Answer

A star rating is fundamentally a single-choice input from an ordered set (1-5), which maps cleanly onto the radio-group pattern already covered in Q25/Q47 — reuse it rather than inventing a bespoke widget. Each "star" is a radio with an accessible name stating its numeric value, not just a decorative glyph; the currently selected value is aria-checked, and arrow keys move the roving-tabindex selection exactly like any other radio group.

For a read-only rating display (not an input, just showing "4.5 out of 5 stars" on a product card), skip the widget semantics entirely — a plain text equivalent (aria-label="Rated 4.5 out of 5") on a decorative star graphic is simpler and sufficient, since there's no interaction to model.

Code Example

function StarRating({ value, onChange }) {
  const refs = React.useRef([]);
  function onKeyDown(e, i) {
    let next = i;
    if (e.key === "ArrowRight") next = Math.min(i + 1, 4);
    if (e.key === "ArrowLeft") next = Math.max(i - 1, 0);
    if (next !== i) { onChange(next + 1); refs.current[next].focus(); }
  }
  return (
    <div role="radiogroup" aria-label="Rating">
      {[0, 1, 2, 3, 4].map((i) => (
        <span
          key={i}
          role="radio"
          aria-checked={value === i + 1}
          aria-label={`${i + 1} star${i === 0 ? "" : "s"}`}
          tabIndex={value === i + 1 ? 0 : -1}
          ref={(el) => (refs.current[i] = el)}
          onKeyDown={(e) => onKeyDown(e, i)}
          onClick={() => onChange(i + 1)}
        >
          {i < value ? "★" : "☆"}
        </span>
      ))}
    </div>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The strongest signal here is pattern reuse: recognizing "this is just a radio group with a star skin" rather than inventing new semantics from scratch. Also drawing the read-only-vs- interactive distinction (a static rating display needs no widget semantics at all) shows you don't over-engineer accessibility onto non-interactive content.

Q62. Build an accessible tree view (nested, expandable) — roles and keys?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Low

Short Answer

A tree view uses role="tree" on the container, role="treeitem" on each node (with aria-expanded if it has children, and aria-level/aria-setsize/aria-posinset for depth/position context), and roving tabindex across the visible items. The keyboard model: Down/Up move between visible items, Right expands (or moves into the first child if already expanded), Left collapses (or moves to the parent if already collapsed), and Enter activates the selected item.

Detailed Answer

Trees are one of the more state-heavy APG patterns because "visible items" changes dynamically as nodes expand/collapse — your roving-tabindex bookkeeping and Down/Up navigation must only consider currently-visible items, skipping collapsed subtrees entirely. aria-level communicates nesting depth (screen readers announce "level 2" so users understand hierarchy even without seeing indentation), while aria-setsize/aria-posinset announce "item 2 of 5" within a given level when the full set isn't rendered (e.g. lazy-loaded/virtualized trees).

Code Example

function TreeItem({ node, level, isExpanded, onToggle, isFocused }) {
  return (
    <li
      role="treeitem"
      aria-expanded={node.children ? isExpanded : undefined}
      aria-level={level}
      tabIndex={isFocused ? 0 : -1}
      onKeyDown={(e) => {
        if (e.key === "ArrowRight" && node.children && !isExpanded) onToggle();
        if (e.key === "ArrowLeft" && isExpanded) onToggle();
      }}
    >
      {node.label}
      {isExpanded && node.children && (
        <ul role="group">
          {node.children.map((c) => <TreeItem key={c.id} node={c} level={level + 1} />)}
        </ul>
      )}
    </li>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

Low frequency but high signal when it comes up — trees are genuinely one of the more complex APG patterns. The mature answer flags that "visible items only" bookkeeping for keyboard navigation is the trickiest part, and that for anything beyond a simple file-tree demo, a maintained tree component (e.g. from React Aria) is usually the pragmatic choice.

SECTION 11 - AUTOMATED ACCESSIBILITY TESTING

Q64. How does axe-core actually detect violations — rule-based static analysis or something else?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

axe-core runs against the live, rendered DOM (not source code) using a set of independently-verifiable rules, each checking a specific, objectively-testable condition — "does this image have an accessible name," "does this element have sufficient color contrast," "is this ARIA attribute valid for this role." Each rule is designed to have (ideally) zero false positives — if axe flags something, it's a real, spec-verifiable defect — which is why axe deliberately doesn't try to catch everything: it trades recall for very high precision.

Detailed Answer

Because it inspects the rendered DOM rather than JSX/source, axe-core catches issues regardless of framework — React, Vue, plain HTML all get the same rule engine applied to their final output. Each rule maps to specific WCAG success criteria and is documented with exactly why it fails and how to fix it. The design philosophy (documented by Deque, axe's maintainer) is explicitly "no false positives" — rules that would require guessing intent (like "is this alt text actually descriptive enough") are deliberately left out, which is precisely why automated coverage tops out around a third of all WCAG criteria (see Q39/Q67).

Senior Engineer Perspective

The "trades recall for precision, by design" framing is the detail that separates a surface-level "axe finds a11y bugs" answer from someone who understands why automated tools have a hard ceiling — it's not an implementation gap, it's an intentional, principled design choice by the tool's maintainers.

Q65. How do you integrate jest-axe into a CI pipeline effectively — thresholds, false positives, flakiness?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Add toHaveNoViolations() assertions to existing component tests (rendering with Testing Library, then running axe(container)) rather than a separate, easily-ignored a11y test suite — this keeps the check close to the code and makes failures block the same PR that introduced them. Configure rule exceptions deliberately and documented (e.g. disabling a rule that's a known false positive for a specific third-party-rendered widget) rather than disabling broadly to silence noise.

Detailed Answer

Practical setup notes:

  • Run it as part of normal component tests, not a separate nightly job — a failing a11y assertion should block the same PR, the same way a broken unit test would.
  • Configure axe rules per-project via a shared config (e.g. disabling color-contrast in jsdom, which can't render real computed styles reliably — that check is better left to a real-browser tool like Playwright + axe or Lighthouse).
  • Avoid snapshotting the full violations array; assert toHaveNoViolations() so the test fails clearly rather than needing a diff read.
  • For known, accepted exceptions (a third-party embed you don't control), scope the exclusion narrowly with axe's exclude/rule options and leave a comment explaining why, rather than disabling the whole test.

Code Example

import { render } from "@testing-library/react";
import { axe, toHaveNoViolations } from "jest-axe";
expect.extend(toHaveNoViolations);

test("SignupForm has no automated a11y violations", async () => {
  const { container } = render(<SignupForm />);
  const results = await axe(container, {
    rules: { "color-contrast": { enabled: false } }, // jsdom can't compute real contrast reliably
  });
  expect(results).toHaveNoViolations();
});

Senior Engineer Perspective

Knowing that jsdom can't reliably evaluate color-contrast (because it doesn't do real layout/paint) — and therefore that check belongs in a real-browser tool instead — is a practical, easy-to-verify detail that shows hands-on CI experience rather than copy-pasted boilerplate.

Q66. How does Lighthouse's accessibility audit work, and what's its scoring based on?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

Lighthouse's accessibility category runs a subset of axe-core's rules against a real, rendered Chrome page and produces a weighted 0-100 score, where each failing audit's weight reflects its estimated impact on users. It's a useful, CI-friendly regression signal (especially wired into a performance budget alongside Core Web Vitals), but a 100 score means "no automated issues found," never "fully accessible" — it inherits axe's precision-over- recall ceiling.

Detailed Answer

Because Lighthouse runs in a real Chrome instance (not jsdom), it can reliably check things jest-axe struggles with in a test environment — actual computed contrast, actual layout/zoom behavior. It's commonly wired into CI via Lighthouse CI to fail a build if the accessibility score regresses below a threshold, which is a good coarse-grained guardrail, but teams should never treat "Lighthouse: 100" as a substitute for manual keyboard/screen-reader testing — it's the same axe rule-set under the hood, just run against a live page instead of a component tree.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The key correction interviewers want you to make unprompted: "a 100 Lighthouse accessibility score means zero automated-detectable issues, not 'accessible' — I'd treat it as a regression guardrail in CI, layered underneath manual testing, never as a certification."

Q67. What accessibility bugs can automated tools never catch, and why?

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Anything requiring judgment about meaning or experience: whether alt text actually describes the image, whether a heading hierarchy makes logical sense, whether reading/focus order matches the intended flow, whether a live-region announcement is helpful or noisy, whether error messages are actually understandable, and whether an entire multi-step interaction feels coherent when driven by keyboard/screen reader. These require a human (or genuinely a human relying on assistive tech) making a subjective call — no static or DOM-based tool can evaluate them.

Detailed Answer

This is deliberately the same theme as Q15/Q39 revisited from the pure-testing angle, because it's one of the highest-value ideas in the whole chapter: automated tools verify presence and syntactic correctness of accessibility features, never their quality. An image with alt="IMG_4821.jpg" passes every automated check (an alt attribute exists, non-empty) while being completely useless to a screen reader user. A form can have perfect ARIA wiring and still be a confusing, illogical mess to navigate by keyboard. Closing this gap is why every serious accessibility program budgets time for manual and assistive-tech-user testing, not just a green CI badge.

Senior Engineer Perspective

Given how often this exact theme recurs across this chapter's questions, expect interviewers to probe it from several angles in one interview — they're testing whether you genuinely internalize it or just memorized one phrasing of it. Always ground it in a concrete example (the meaningless alt-text case is the strongest, most memorable one) rather than restating the abstract principle alone.

Q68. How do you set up eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y rules, and how do you handle false positives?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Extend the plugin's recommended (or stricter strict) ruleset in your ESLint config so violations surface at write-time, in the editor, before code is even committed — the cheapest point in the pipeline to catch an a11y bug. For rules that produce genuine false positives on a specific, deliberate pattern (a custom component that wraps a native input, which the linter can't statically know is accessible), disable that single rule on that single line with a comment explaining why, rather than disabling it project-wide.

Detailed Answer

The plugin's most valuable rules for a React app: anchor-is-valid (catches fake <a> buttons — the Q3 anti-pattern), click-events-have-key-events /no-static-element-interactions (catches div-onClick soup), label-has-associated-control, aria-props/aria-role (catches invalid/misspelled ARIA), and alt-text. A common false-positive source: the plugin can't see through custom wrapper components (e.g. your own <Button> that renders a native <button> under the hood) — configure components mappings in the rule options so the linter knows your <Button> counts as a real button, rather than disabling checks on it.

// .eslintrc

  "extends": ["plugin:jsx-a11y/recommended"],
  "settings": {
    "jsx-a11y": {
      "components": {
        "Button": "button",   // teach the linter your wrapper IS a real button
        "Link": "a"
      }
    }
  },
  "rules": {
    // Deliberate, documented exception — not a blanket disable
    "jsx-a11y/no-autofocus": ["error", { "ignoreNonDOM": true }]
  }
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

Naming the components mapping option specifically is the detail that proves you've actually configured this plugin on a real design-system-heavy codebase rather than just enabling the recommended preset and moving on — wrapper components are the single most common source of false positives in practice.

Q69. How do you accessibility-test in Storybook (addon-a11y) as part of a component library workflow?

Storybook's accessibility addon runs axe-core against each story in an isolated panel inside the Storybook UI, giving component authors instant, per-variant feedback (including edge-case states like disabled, error, or loading) without needing a full page or app context. For a component library specifically, this catches issues at the source — a fixed primitive component benefits every consumer at once, unlike page-level testing that only covers the states a given page happens to render.

Q70. What is Playwright/Cypress's role in accessibility testing versus unit-level jest-axe?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

E2E tools run axe against a real browser rendering a real, fully-integrated page (or an entire user flow across multiple pages), which catches things jsdom-based unit tests structurally cannot: real computed color contrast, real layout/overlap issues, and accessibility state across multi-step flows (does focus land correctly after a route change, does a modal's focus trap survive real browser event timing). Unit-level jest-axe is faster and catches component-level regressions earlier; E2E axe is slower but closer to what a real user (and a real screen reader) actually experiences.

Detailed Answer

The two layers are complementary, not redundant, mirroring the standard test-pyramid argument: unit-level jest-axe runs on every component in isolation, is fast enough to run on every PR, and catches "this component's markup is structurally broken" early. E2E-level axe (via @axe-core/playwright or cypress-axe) runs less frequently (e.g. on a critical-path smoke suite) but validates real integrated behavior: does the actual rendered contrast pass (not fakeable in jsdom), does focus genuinely move correctly across a real route transition, does a real modal's trap survive real DOM timing rather than a simulated one.

Some teams also use Playwright specifically to drive real keyboard interaction assertions (press Tab N times, assert the expected element has focus) as a semi-automated substitute for part of the manual keyboard pass — not a full replacement, but a useful regression guard for previously-fixed focus-order bugs.

Code Example

import { test, expect } from "@playwright/test";
import AxeBuilder from "@axe-core/playwright";

test("checkout page has no a11y violations", async ({ page }) => {
  await page.goto("/checkout");
  const results = await new AxeBuilder({ page }).analyze();
  expect(results.violations).toEqual([]);
});

test("Tab order reaches the submit button", async ({ page }) => {
  await page.goto("/checkout");
  for (let i = 0; i < 5; i++) await page.keyboard.press("Tab");
  await expect(page.getByRole("button", { name: "Place order" })).toBeFocused();
});

Senior Engineer Perspective

The strong answer treats this as a layered pipeline rather than choosing one tool over the other — unit-level for speed and coverage breadth, E2E for real-browser fidelity and cross-page flow validation, both feeding into (never replacing) a periodic manual screen-reader pass.

Q71. How do you triage and prioritize a long list of axe violations across a legacy codebase?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Don't try to fix everything at once. Group violations by root cause rather than by individual instance (one missing-label bug in a shared component might account for hundreds of reported violations across pages), prioritize by severity/impact (critical: blocks task completion entirely, e.g. an unusable form; serious: significant friction; moderate/minor: polish), and prioritize high-traffic, revenue-critical, or legally-exposed flows (checkout, signup, account settings) first.

Detailed Answer

A practical triage framework:

  1. Deduplicate by root cause — run axe across the whole app first, then group by the underlying component/pattern responsible; a single shared <Select> component missing a name might be logged as 200 separate violations across pages but is one fix.
  2. Rank by severity — axe already tags each violation critical/serious/moderate/minor; start with anything that fully blocks task completion for a keyboard/SR user (an unlabeled required field, a completely unreachable control).
  3. Rank by traffic and risk — a checkout flow or the login page matters more than a rarely-visited settings sub-page, both for user impact and legal exposure (see Section 13).
  4. Fix upstream, shared components first — fixing a design-system primitive has multiplicative payoff (see Q69) versus patching each page's usage individually.
  5. Track as a real backlog with a budget — allocate a fixed percentage of each sprint to a11y debt rather than treating it as an occasional, unscheduled fire-drill.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The insight interviewers want to hear: "the raw violation count is almost always misleading — dozens or hundreds of reported issues often collapse into a handful of root causes once you group by component." That reframing from "we have 400 a11y bugs" (paralyzing) to "we have 6 root causes, 3 of which are shared components" (actionable) is exactly the kind of leadership move a senior engineer brings to a legacy-remediation project.

SECTION 12 - ACCESSIBILITY IN DYNAMIC SPA CONTENT

Q72. How do you announce async content loading — e.g., infinite scroll or lazy-loaded sections?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Wrap the loading/loaded transition in a polite aria-live region that announces state changes ("Loading more items…" then "10 more items loaded") without stealing focus — infinite scroll is a background enhancement, not a navigation event, so it shouldn't interrupt the user's current reading position. For lazy-loaded sections revealed by intersection observers, the same principle applies: announce that new content appeared, don't forcibly move focus into it.

Detailed Answer

Infinite scroll is a specifically tricky pattern for screen reader users because scrolling itself isn't a first-class screen-reader action the way it is for sighted mouse users — a screen reader user typically navigates by heading/landmark/Tab, not by literal scroll position, so newly-appended content at the bottom of an infinitely-scrolling list might never be discovered unless it's announced. A live region confirming new items loaded (with a count) gives that feedback; some implementations also add a manual "Load more" button as an explicit, discoverable alternative to purely automatic scroll-triggered loading, which is generally the more robust and predictable pattern for accessibility.

Whatever you do, never auto-focus into newly-loaded content — that would yank a screen reader user's cursor away from whatever they were reading, which is far more disruptive than helpful.

Code Example

function InfiniteList({ items, isLoadingMore, loadMore }) {
  return (
    <>
      <ul>{items.map((i) => <li key={i.id}>{i.title}</li>)}</ul>
      <button onClick={loadMore} disabled={isLoadingMore}>
        {isLoadingMore ? "Loading…" : "Load more"}
      </button>
      <div aria-live="polite" className="sr-only">
        {isLoadingMore ? "Loading more items…" : `${items.length} items loaded.`}
      </div>
    </>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The strongest answer names the underlying reason infinite scroll is genuinely harder for screen reader users (scroll position isn't their primary navigation mechanism) rather than just prescribing "add a live region" mechanically — and recommends a manual "Load more" fallback as often the more robust, universally-accessible pattern over purely automatic scroll-triggered fetching.

Q73. How do you manage focus for optimistic UI updates that add or remove items?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Optimistic updates change the DOM before server confirmation, which can shift focus unexpectedly if the item holding focus moves position or briefly disappears during a rollback. The rule: keep focus anchored to the acting control (e.g. the "Add" button stays focused after adding an item, rather than focus jumping to the new item), and if an optimistic update later rolls back due to a server error, restore both the data and focus to a sane, explained state rather than silently reverting.

Detailed Answer

A concrete failure mode: a user clicks "Add to cart," the item is optimistically added to a list rendered above the button, the list re-flows, and if focus was ever moved into that list item (rather than staying on the button), a rollback removing the item on a server error can strand focus on a now-nonexistent element (the Q28 problem, triggered by network failure rather than a user action). Keeping focus on the initiating control throughout — and communicating success/failure via a toast or live region rather than by moving focus around — sidesteps this entire class of bug.

For optimistic removals (e.g. an "archive" action that immediately hides a list item), the same focus-on-delete guidance from Q28 applies: if the removed item held focus, move it to a logical neighbor immediately, and if the optimistic removal is later rolled back, restore the item and consider whether focus should return to it or stay wherever the user has since moved.

Code Example

function AddToCartButton({ item, addToCart }) {
  const [status, setStatus] = React.useState("idle");
  async function handleClick() {
    setStatus("added"); // optimistic
    try {
      await addToCart(item);
    } catch {
      setStatus("failed"); // rollback — focus never left this button, nothing is stranded
    }
  }
  return (
    <button onClick={handleClick} aria-describedby="cart-status">
      Add to cart
      <span id="cart-status" aria-live="polite" className="sr-only">
        {status === "added" && "Added to cart."}
        {status === "failed" && "Couldn't add to cart. Please try again."}
      </span>
    </button>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The senior insight: optimistic UI's accessibility risk isn't the optimism itself, it's the rollback path — teams thoroughly test the happy optimistic case but rarely test what happens to focus and announcements when the server rejects it. Naming that asymmetry unprompted is a strong signal.

Q74. How do you handle accessibility for skeleton loaders and Suspense boundaries?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

A skeleton loader should be announced as a loading state (e.g. role="status" wrapping it, or an aria-live region announcing "Loading dashboard…") rather than silently presenting a screen reader user with a wall of unlabeled decorative shapes. When the real content replaces the skeleton, a second announcement ("Dashboard loaded") — or simply the content's own heading being naturally encountered — closes the loop. Suspense boundaries in React need the same treatment: the fallback isn't just a visual placeholder, it needs to be announced as a loading state too.

Detailed Answer

A skeleton's shimmering boxes are purely visual with zero inherent semantics — without aria-hidden="true" on the decorative shapes themselves (so they don't get announced as meaningless empty elements) plus a real text-based status announcement somewhere, a screen reader user gets either silence (not knowing anything is loading) or a confusing stream of unlabeled elements. The standard fix: wrap the whole skeleton in role="status" with a visually-hidden text label ("Loading dashboard…"), and hide the decorative skeleton shapes themselves from the accessibility tree.

For React's <Suspense fallback={...}>, this means the fallback prop's content should itself follow this pattern — many teams forget the fallback needs the same accessibility treatment as any other loading UI, since it's easy to think of it as "just a placeholder."

Code Example

function DashboardSkeleton() {
  return (
    <div role="status">
      <span className="sr-only">Loading dashboard…</span>
      <div aria-hidden="true" className="skeleton-box" />   {/* decorative, hidden from AT */}
      <div aria-hidden="true" className="skeleton-box" />
    </div>
  );
}

<React.Suspense fallback={<DashboardSkeleton />}>
  <Dashboard />
</React.Suspense>

Senior Engineer Perspective

Most teams style skeleton loaders carefully but never think about their accessibility tree presence at all — pointing out that the shimmer boxes need aria-hidden while the wrapper needs a real status announcement is a specific, checkable detail that shows this has actually been implemented and tested before, not just theorized.

Q75. How do you make a toast/snackbar queue accessible when multiple messages arrive quickly?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

Queue toasts and announce them one at a time through a single live region rather than dumping several onto the screen reader simultaneously — a burst of 5 near-instant aria-live updates typically gets partially dropped or garbled by screen readers, which don't queue announcements the way a toast UI visually stacks them. Space announcements out (e.g. a small delay between each) or collapse related rapid-fire updates into a single summary ("3 files uploaded successfully") when that's a truthful simplification.

Detailed Answer

Screen readers generally handle live-region updates as "announce the latest change," not "queue and read every change in order" — so if your toast system pushes three messages into the live region within 200ms of each other, a user might only hear the last one, or hear a jumbled interruption. Two workable fixes: (1) a small debounce/throttle on the live region's text updates so rapid bursts settle before announcing, or (2) explicitly batch related messages into one combined announcement when the individual detail isn't critical (uploading 10 files one by one → one final "10 files uploaded" announcement rather than 10 separate ones).

Code Example

function useThrottledAnnouncement(delay = 500) {
  const [message, setMessage] = React.useState("");
  const queueRef = React.useRef([]);
  const timerRef = React.useRef(null);

  function announce(text) {
    queueRef.current.push(text);
    if (timerRef.current) return;
    timerRef.current = setTimeout(() => {
      setMessage(queueRef.current.join(". "));  // combine what queued up during the delay
      queueRef.current = [];
      timerRef.current = null;
    }, delay);
  }
  return [message, announce];
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The nuance interviewers want: screen readers don't queue live-region announcements the way a visual toast stack queues on screen — that mismatch is exactly why a burst of rapid toasts needs its own throttling/batching strategy distinct from the purely visual stacking logic.

Q76. What accessibility considerations apply to virtualized/windowed lists (e.g. react-window)?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

Virtualized lists only render DOM nodes for visible (plus a small buffer of) rows, which breaks two assumptions screen readers rely on: knowing the true total count of items, and letting users jump via Ctrl+F/find or by scrolling with a screen reader's own navigation (since off-screen rows literally don't exist in the DOM yet). Mitigate with aria-setsize/aria-posinset on rendered rows to announce true position ("item 42 of 1000") even though only a handful of DOM nodes exist, and consider whether virtualization is even necessary for lists small enough that full rendering wouldn't hurt performance.

Detailed Answer

The deeper problem: a screen reader building its accessibility tree from the DOM has no way to know "there are 1000 items total, you're currently seeing items 40-55" unless you tell it explicitly — from the tree's perspective, the list might as well only have 15 items. Setting role="listbox"/role="list" with aria-setsize (total count) and aria-posinset (this item's 1-based position) on each rendered row patches this gap, letting the screen reader announce true position within the full, unvirtualized conceptual list.

The other real cost: virtualization defeats browser find-in-page and screen-reader "search/browse" features for off-screen content, since that content simply doesn't exist in the DOM until scrolled into range. For content where searchability matters more than raw render performance (a moderately-sized settings list, for instance), it's often worth skipping virtualization entirely rather than fighting these tradeoffs — reserve virtualization for genuinely huge datasets where the performance win clearly outweighs the accessibility cost.

Code Example

function Row({ index, style, data }) {
  return (
    <div
      style={style}
      role="option"
      aria-setsize={data.totalCount}
      aria-posinset={index + 1}
    >
      {data.items[index].label}
    </div>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

The most senior framing: "virtualization is a performance optimization with a real accessibility cost — I'd only reach for it once the list is genuinely too large to render in full, and I'd patch the gap with aria-setsize/posinset rather than assuming it's a purely performance-neutral decision." That cost-benefit framing, not just the ARIA attribute names, is what shows real judgment.

Q77. How do you handle focus when a component conditionally unmounts the currently focused element (e.g., a permission check hides a button mid-interaction)?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

This is the general form of the Q28/Q73 problem: whenever React conditionally stops rendering an element based on state that can change independently of direct user action (a permission flag flips, a feature flag disables mid-session, a websocket update removes a row), you must check whether the currently-focused element is about to disappear and proactively move focus to a stable fallback before the unmount commits, not after focus has already silently landed on <body>.

Detailed Answer

The pattern generalizes across all the "vanishing focused element" cases in this chapter: capture a ref to the currently-focused element (or its identifying key/id) before a state change you know might remove it, compare after the state update in a useEffect, and if document.activeElement === document.body (the telltale sign focus was stranded), redirect it to a sensible, still-existing landmark or heading. This is exactly why a reusable useEffect-based "focus guard" utility, applied consistently across a codebase, is more valuable than fixing each individual case ad hoc.

Code Example

function useFocusGuard(fallbackRef) {
  React.useEffect(() => {
    // Run after every render; if focus got silently dumped on body, redirect it
    if (document.activeElement === document.body) {
      fallbackRef.current?.focus();
    }
  });
}

function Toolbar({ canDelete, mainHeadingRef }) {
  useFocusGuard(mainHeadingRef);
  return (
    <div>
      {canDelete && <button>Delete</button>}   {/* if this vanishes while focused, guard catches it */}
    </div>
  );
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

Recognizing this as one general pattern (rather than several unrelated bugs) and proposing a shared, reusable guard is exactly the kind of system-level thinking senior engineers are expected to bring — instead of writing bespoke, one-off focus-restoration code in every component that happens to have this risk.

Q78. How do you announce real-time collaborative updates (e.g., another user edited a field) without being disruptive?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

Route collaborative-presence updates (someone else edited a field, joined a document, changed a status) through a low-priority, polite live region — never assertive, since these are background events, not something the current user directly caused or urgently needs. Batch/throttle rapid successive updates (many teams editing simultaneously) the same way you would a toast burst (Q75), and never move focus in response to another user's action — the current user's cursor position/focus is theirs, and remote updates must never hijack it.

Detailed Answer

Real-time collaboration (think a shared document or a live dashboard) introduces update events the current user never initiated, which makes it easy to over-announce — every remote keystroke or cursor move triggering a screen-reader interruption would make the product unusable for assistive tech users. The right granularity is usually coarser than the raw event stream: announce "Alex is editing this document" once when presence changes, not every individual remote keystroke; announce a field's new value only after the remote edit settles (debounced), not mid-typing.

The absolute rule: remote/background events must never move the current user's focus or selection — if a remote edit happens to the exact field the current user has focused, prefer a non-disruptive visual/textual indicator ("Alex just edited this field") over silently overwriting what they're looking at or, worse, yanking their cursor.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The insight interviewers reward: collaborative features multiply the volume of "background" events dramatically, so the discipline of "polite, never assertive, never focus-stealing, and coarsely batched" for anything the current user didn't directly initiate becomes essential at a completely different scale than a typical single-user app.

Q79. What's different about accessibility in a single-page app using optimistic navigation transitions (React 19 transitions, the View Transitions API)?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

These APIs animate the visual transition between two DOM states, but they don't automatically solve the underlying SPA focus/announcement problem from Q10 — you still explicitly own moving focus to the new page's heading and updating the document title once the transition settles. The specific new risk: an in-flight, animated transition can make focus/announcement timing ambiguous (should it happen at transition-start or transition-end?) — the safest choice is generally to defer focus movement until the new content's transition completes, matching what a sighted user visually perceives as "the new page has arrived."

Detailed Answer

React 19's useTransition/Actions and the browser's View Transitions API both let old and new content coexist visually during an animated swap, which is a UX win for sighted users but adds a genuine question mark for assistive tech: firing a focus change or a live-region announcement the instant the transition starts (before the visual/DOM swap is complete) can be premature and confusing, while waiting until it fully finishes matches the sighted user's actual perceived timing. Most current guidance is to hook focus/announcement logic to the transition's completion callback (or the finished promise the View Transitions API provides) rather than its start.

prefers-reduced-motion also applies squarely here — a "page transition" animation is exactly the kind of large-scale visual motion that criterion is meant to allow users to opt out of, so the transition itself should degrade to an instant swap under that preference, independent of the focus-management question.

Code Example

async function navigate(path) {
  if (document.startViewTransition && !prefersReducedMotion()) {
    const transition = document.startViewTransition(() => render(path));
    await transition.finished;      // wait for the animation to actually complete
  } else {
    render(path);                   // instant swap: no transition, or user opted out of motion
  }
  document.title = getTitleFor(path);
  headingRef.current?.focus();      // focus moves only once the new page has "arrived"
}

Senior Engineer Perspective

This is a genuinely current (2026-era) question — the honest answer acknowledges the pattern is still settling across the industry, and the safest default is "tie focus/ announcement to transition completion, and always provide a reduced-motion escape hatch that also simplifies the timing question entirely." Showing awareness that this is an open, evolving area (rather than reciting a single settled rule) is itself a mark of genuine expertise here.

SECTION 13 - LEGAL & COMPLIANCE CONTEXT

Q80. What are the practical differences between ADA Title III, Section 508, and EN 301 549?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

ADA Title III (US) covers "places of public accommodation" — commercial websites and apps generally — enforced through private lawsuits and demand letters, without codifying a specific technical standard (courts consistently point to WCAG 2.x AA as the de facto bar). Section 508 (US) is a specific federal regulation requiring US federal agencies (and their contractors/vendors) to meet defined technical standards, which now directly incorporate WCAG 2.0 AA. EN 301 549 is the EU/UK technical standard (also built on WCAG) required for public-sector bodies and, increasingly, private-sector products under the European Accessibility Act.

Detailed Answer

FrameworkJurisdictionWho it bindsEnforcement
ADA Title IIIUnited States"Places of public accommodation" (broadly interpreted to include commercial websites/apps)Private lawsuits, demand letters — no government pre-certification
Section 508United States (federal)Federal agencies and their vendors/contractorsProcurement requirements; VPATs required (see Q82)
EN 301 549EU / UKPublic-sector bodies; increasingly private-sector under the EAAGovernment procurement + EAA enforcement mechanisms per member state

Meeting WCAG 2.1/2.2 AA is therefore the single highest-leverage engineering target regardless of which specific legal framework applies to a given product.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The framing that reassures a hiring manager: "I don't need to be a lawyer, but I know that whichever legal framework applies, the actual engineering target converges on WCAG 2.1/2.2 AA — so building to that standard consistently is the right default regardless of which specific law a given client or product falls under."

Q81. What does "WCAG 2.1 AA" mean as a contractual/legal target, and what's typically out of scope?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

Contracts and demand letters citing "WCAG 2.1 AA" mean all Level A and Level AA success criteria in that version must be met — not AAA (rarely contractually required site-wide), and typically scoped to the product's core, user-facing functionality rather than every edge case (third-party embedded widgets you don't control, legacy archived content, or PDFs are common, explicitly negotiated exclusions).

Detailed Answer

Practical scoping questions an engineering team should get answered explicitly rather than assume: does the commitment cover third-party embeds (payment widgets, chat plugins, analytics popups) that render inside your product but that you don't control the code for? Does it cover downloadable documents (PDFs, which have their own separate accessibility remediation burden distinct from the web app)? Does it apply retroactively to old, low-traffic archived content, or only to actively-maintained pages going forward? These scoping boundaries matter enormously for estimating remediation effort and should be explicit in any conformance statement or contract, not left implicit.

Also worth knowing: "conformance" under WCAG technically requires meeting every applicable success criterion for a claimed level on a defined set of pages — a partial pass ("most of AA") isn't technically "AA conformant," which is why honest conformance statements (Q82/Q85) usually describe a specific, bounded scope rather than claiming blanket site-wide conformance.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The senior move here is asking the scoping questions explicitly rather than assuming — "does this commitment include third-party embeds and legacy content, or just our actively-maintained surfaces?" — because that scope directly drives what's realistically achievable on a given timeline, and getting it wrong creates both legal and planning risk.

Q82. What is a VPAT (Voluntary Product Accessibility Template), and when would you need to produce one?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

A VPAT is a standardized document where a vendor self-reports, criterion by criterion, how their product conforms to WCAG/Section 508/EN 301 549 requirements — using categories like "Supports," "Partially Supports," "Does Not Support," and "Not Applicable." It's most commonly required when selling software to US federal agencies (or their contractors) under Section 508 procurement rules, and increasingly requested by any large enterprise buyer doing accessibility due diligence before purchase.

Detailed Answer

Engineering's role in producing a VPAT is usually supplying the underlying, honest, criterion-level assessment — walking through the actual WCAG success criteria against the real product (informed by both automated scans and manual/screen-reader testing) rather than rubber-stamping "Supports" across the board. A VPAT that overstates conformance is a real liability: it becomes a documented, citable claim a customer or plaintiff can later point to as contradicted by the product's actual behavior — accuracy protects the company more than optimism does.

VPATs are typically refreshed periodically (e.g. with major releases) rather than being a one-time document, since the underlying product keeps changing.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The line that lands: "a VPAT is only valuable if it's honest — engineering's job is to supply an accurate, tested assessment, because an inflated VPAT is a liability that outlives the sales cycle that motivated it." This ties directly into the honest-scoping theme from Q81 and Q85.

Q83. How does the European Accessibility Act (EAA) affect product teams outside the EU?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

The EAA (binding across EU member states since mid-2025) requires many private-sector digital products and services — e-commerce, banking, e-books, some transport services — sold into the EU market to meet accessibility requirements (built on EN 301 549/WCAG), regardless of where the company selling them is headquartered. A US or other non-EU company selling a covered product/service to EU consumers is generally in scope if it falls under a covered category, which is exactly why "we're not an EU company" doesn't exempt a product team from EAA relevance.

Detailed Answer

This mirrors the extraterritorial reach many engineers are already familiar with from GDPR — the EAA applies based on where the product is sold/offered, not where the vendor is incorporated. For a product team, the practical implication is that "we only have US customers today" is not a durable reason to deprioritize accessibility if EU market expansion is even a plausible future plan — retrofitting accessibility later is dramatically more expensive than building it in from the start, so EAA exposure is a good argument for treating WCAG AA as a baseline default rather than a market-specific afterthought.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The comparison to GDPR is the fastest way to make this land with an interviewer or a skeptical product manager — "extraterritorial reach based on where you sell, not where you're incorporated" is a familiar mental model most engineers already have, just applied to a different EU regulation.

Q84. What's the engineering team's realistic role versus legal/compliance's role in an accessibility lawsuit or demand-letter response?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

Legal owns the response strategy, settlement negotiations, and any public statements. Engineering's role is to quickly and honestly assess the specific claims — is this violation real, how widespread is it, what's the realistic remediation timeline — and then execute a prioritized fix plan. Engineering should never independently make legal commitments about conformance timelines or scope without legal's involvement, but should absolutely push back with facts if a claim is inaccurate or a proposed remediation timeline is unrealistic.

Detailed Answer

A typical demand letter cites specific alleged WCAG violations (often generated from an automated scan by the plaintiff's counsel, which means some claims may be false positives or already fixed). Engineering's most valuable early contribution is verifying each claim against the current, live product — confirming what's genuinely broken, what's already resolved, and what's a tooling false positive — which directly informs legal's negotiating position. From there, engineering typically commits to (and then must actually deliver) a remediation plan with realistic milestones, since a settlement's remediation timeline becomes a commitment the company is legally bound to meet.

This is also the point at which the triage framework from Q71 becomes directly relevant — a demand letter often names symptoms; engineering's job is tracing them to root causes and communicating a genuinely achievable timeline rather than an optimistic one that later becomes a second, worse legal problem for missing a settlement commitment.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The framing that shows maturity: engineering's job in this moment is accurate, verified facts and a realistic plan — not legal strategy, and not blind agreement with whatever the letter claims. Pushing back with evidence when a claim is wrong (or already fixed) is just as valuable to legal as confirming what's genuinely broken.

Q85. What accessibility conformance claims are risky to make, and how do you scope them honestly?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

Blanket claims like "our website is fully WCAG 2.1 AA compliant" or "100% accessible" are risky because they're almost never literally true across every page, state, and third-party component, and they become a specific, citable claim in litigation. Safer, more honest framing: name the specific standard and version targeted, the specific scope it covers (core flows, not necessarily every archived page or third-party embed), and frame it as an ongoing commitment with periodic re-assessment rather than a one-time, permanent certification.

Detailed Answer

The legal risk asymmetry is real: an accurate, modest statement ("we are working toward WCAG 2.1 AA conformance across our core product; we welcome accessibility feedback at [contact]") protects the company far better than an inflated one, because an overstatement is itself evidence of a misrepresentation claim if any violation is later found — which, given how large modern web apps are, is nearly inevitable somewhere. This is exactly the same principle from the VPAT discussion (Q82) generalized to any public accessibility statement.

A well-scoped public accessibility statement (see also Q89) typically names: the standard and version targeted, what's explicitly in and out of scope, how users can report an issue, and a rough cadence for re-evaluation — turning a legal liability into a demonstrable, good-faith effort that actually helps in a dispute rather than being used against the company.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The senior instinct: push back on marketing/legal language like "fully accessible" or "100% WCAG compliant" whenever you see it drafted, and advocate for scoped, honest, contact-inviting language instead — engineers are often the first and only people in the room who actually understand why an absolute claim is both technically inaccurate and legally unwise.

Q86. What's the difference between "accessible" and "WCAG conformant," and why does that distinction matter legally?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Low

Short Answer

"WCAG conformant" is a precise, testable claim (every applicable success criterion at a given level is met, for a defined scope) — it's a technical, binary-per-criterion standard. "Accessible" is a broader, more subjective usability claim about the actual experience of real disabled users, which can diverge from strict conformance in both directions: a page can technically pass every WCAG criterion and still be a genuinely poor experience (Q39/Q67's core theme), or a product might be highly usable in practice while technically failing a narrow, edge-case criterion.

Detailed Answer

This distinction matters legally because plaintiffs and demand letters typically cite the precise, testable standard (WCAG conformance) since it's objectively verifiable by an automated scan or expert review, while a company's marketing or support language often uses the vaguer word "accessible" without meaning to make a formal conformance claim at all. Being precise about which word you're using — and to whom — avoids accidentally making an implicit legal commitment ("accessible") when you only intended a general aspiration.

Senior Engineer Perspective

A subtle but real point: internally, an engineering team should chase genuine usability for disabled users (the "accessible" goal) even beyond the letter of WCAG, while externally/ legally, being precise about conformance claims specifically (the "WCAG AA conformant" claim) protects the company from overpromising on a word that sounds similar but means something legally distinct.

Q87. How do procurement/RFP accessibility requirements typically get enforced on vendors, and what does that mean for an engineering team building a product other companies buy?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Low

Short Answer

Large enterprise and government buyers routinely require a VPAT (Q82) and/or a signed contractual commitment to a specific WCAG/Section 508/EN 301 549 level as a condition of purchase — accessibility becomes a procurement gate, not just a legal-risk topic. For a B2B or B2G product team, this means accessibility conformance can directly determine whether a deal closes at all, which typically elevates it from "nice to have" to a sales-blocking requirement that product/engineering leadership tracks explicitly.

Detailed Answer

In practice, a sales team pursuing a large enterprise or government contract will often be asked directly for a current VPAT and evidence of an accessibility remediation process before a deal can proceed — turning an engineering-quality concern into a revenue-blocking dependency with its own timeline pressure. This is a strong argument for building accessibility testing (automated + periodic manual passes) into the standard development workflow proactively (see Section 14) rather than scrambling to produce evidence reactively when a big deal suddenly depends on it.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The business-fluent framing that resonates with non-engineering stakeholders: "accessibility debt isn't just a legal-risk line item — for a B2B/B2G product it can directly block revenue, since procurement teams increasingly require a current, honest VPAT before a deal closes." That reframing often unlocks budget and prioritization faster than a purely ethical or legal-risk argument alone.

SECTION 14 - BUILDING AN ACCESSIBILITY CULTURE

Q88. How do you run an accessibility audit on an existing codebase from scratch?

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Start broad and automated (axe/Lighthouse across every major page, plus eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y across the codebase) to get a fast, wide baseline. Then narrow to manual testing on the highest-traffic and highest-risk flows (signup, checkout, core workflows) — keyboard-only pass, then a real screen reader pass. Finally, group findings by root cause (Q71) and produce a prioritized, actionable backlog rather than a raw dump of hundreds of individual violations.

Detailed Answer

A concrete audit sequence:

  1. Automated baseline — run axe/Lighthouse across every distinct page template (not literally every URL — templates repeat), and run the ESLint a11y plugin across the whole repo to catch component-level issues even on pages you haven't manually visited yet.
  2. Manual keyboard pass — unplug the mouse, walk every critical user flow end to end, noting any trap, missing focus indicator, illogical tab order, or unreachable control.
  3. Screen reader pass — repeat the critical flows with NVDA or VoiceOver, focusing on whether the experience is coherent, not just technically compliant (Q35/Q67).
  4. Zoom/contrast/motion pass — 200%/400% zoom, forced-colors mode, prefers-reduced-motion, and a visual contrast spot-check on the design system's core colors.
  5. Synthesize — group everything by root cause, rank by severity and traffic (Q71), and produce a scoped, honest report rather than a raw violations list.

Where possible, involve an actual assistive-technology user (internal or via a specialized accessibility consultancy) for at least the highest-stakes flows — lived experience surfaces problems that no engineer simulating a screen reader for the first time will reliably catch.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The strongest closing point: recommend bringing in a real assistive-technology user for at least the critical flows, rather than relying purely on engineers testing with tools they're not fluent in. That single recommendation signals genuine respect for the difference between "I tried NVDA for an hour" and "someone who uses NVDA every day of their life reviewed this."

Q89. How do you write a public accessibility statement, and what should it actually contain?

ROI: ★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

A good accessibility statement names the specific standard and version targeted (e.g. "WCAG 2.1 Level AA"), states the scope honestly (which properties/flows it covers, and any known exceptions like third-party embeds), provides a clear contact channel for reporting issues, and commits to a review cadence — it's a living, honest document, not a marketing claim of perfection (tying back to Q85's "avoid 100% compliant" guidance).

Detailed Answer

A well-structured statement typically includes:

  • Commitment — a brief statement of the organization's commitment to accessibility and why (values-based, not just legal-compliance framing).
  • Standard and scope — the specific standard/version and what parts of the product it covers (and honestly, what it doesn't yet).
  • Known limitations — a short, honest list of known gaps and, ideally, a rough timeline for addressing them — this is the section most companies are tempted to omit, but it's the part that most credibly demonstrates good faith.
  • Feedback mechanism — a real, monitored contact (email/form) specifically for accessibility issues, distinct from general support, so reports don't get lost.
  • Last reviewed date — showing it's actively maintained, not a one-time artifact.

Engineering's role is usually supplying the honest technical content (scope, known limitations, testing methodology) while legal/comms typically own the final wording — but engineering should insist on accuracy over polish in that collaboration.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The counter-intuitive but correct advice: including a "known limitations" section, rather than omitting it to look better, is what actually makes the statement credible and legally defensible — a company that publicly acknowledges specific gaps and a plan to fix them reads as trustworthy in a way a suspiciously perfect claim never does.

Q90. How do you embed accessibility checks into a team's day-to-day workflow — PR checklist, Definition of Done?

ROI: ★★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Bake automated checks into CI so they're non-optional (lint + jest-axe failing blocks merge, same as any other test), add a short, concrete accessibility checklist to the PR template for anything touching UI (keyboard-tested? labels present? contrast checked?), and add "accessible" as an explicit, non-negotiable criterion in the team's Definition of Done for any user-facing feature — the same way "has tests" or "handles errors" already is.

Detailed Answer

The goal is making accessibility a default part of the existing process rather than a separate, optional review step that gets skipped under deadline pressure. Concrete mechanisms that work well together:

  • CI gates — eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y and jest-axe as required checks, same severity as any other failing test.
  • PR template checklist — a short, specific list (not a vague "is this accessible?"): "Tested with keyboard only?" "New interactive elements have accessible names?" "Color isn't the only signal for state?"
  • Definition of Done — "accessible per WCAG AA" listed alongside "has tests," "handles loading/error states" as a standard acceptance criterion, not an afterthought bolted onto a separate a11y-specific ticket.
  • Design handoff — designers annotate contrast and focus-order expectations in Figma/handoff docs before engineering even starts, catching issues before a single line of code is written.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The framing that resonates most with engineering leadership: "the goal isn't a separate accessibility review — it's making accessibility invisible-by-default, the same way linting or type-checking became invisible-by-default once they were wired into CI rather than left as a manual, skippable step."

Q91. How do you get buy-in from product and design for accessibility work when it's not the "squeaky wheel"?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Translate accessibility into language each stakeholder already optimizes for: for product, frame it as addressable market size (15-20% of users have a disability) and legal risk reduction; for design, frame it as a forcing function for better, clearer design overall (the curb-cut effect from Q1); for leadership, frame it as procurement/revenue risk in B2B/B2G contexts (Q87). Pair the framing with concrete, visible wins — fixing a handful of high-impact issues and showing the before/after — rather than an abstract, hard-to-visualize ask.

Detailed Answer

Accessibility competes for prioritization against visible, revenue-attributable feature work, so the pitch needs to connect to metrics stakeholders already track. Effective tactics seen in practice: running a quick, cheap audit (Q88) and presenting a small number of concrete, relatable findings ("here's what a screen reader user hears trying to check out on our site right now") rather than a spreadsheet of hundreds of WCAG codes; tying specific fixes to specific business risk (the procurement-gate argument from Q87 for B2B products, the legal exposure argument from Section 13 for consumer products); and securing a small, protected percentage of each sprint for accessibility debt so it doesn't have to win a prioritization fight from zero every single sprint.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The strongest tactic to name: a short, visceral demo — playing a recording of (or live demonstrating) a real screen reader failing on your own product's checkout flow — tends to move stakeholders far more than any WCAG citation ever will. Concrete, felt experience beats abstract compliance language nearly every time.

Q92. How would you train a team of React engineers who have never thought about accessibility?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: Medium

Short Answer

Start with a short, hands-on session rather than a lecture: have each engineer unplug their mouse and try to complete a real task in your own product using only the keyboard, then a screen reader — direct, felt friction teaches faster than any slide deck. Follow with a small set of durable principles (semantic HTML first, the first rule of ARIA, focus management basics) rather than an exhaustive WCAG checklist, and back it with the CI/PR-checklist guardrails from Q90 so the learning sticks into daily habit rather than fading after one workshop.

Detailed Answer

A workable rollout sequence: (1) a 60-90 minute hands-on workshop where engineers experience their own product's friction firsthand — this is consistently the highest-impact, most memorable part; (2) a short internal reference (not the full WCAG spec — a condensed "top 10 things we actually see go wrong here" doc tailored to your codebase's real patterns); (3) pairing new hires or less-experienced engineers with whoever on the team already has accessibility depth, on real PRs, not synthetic exercises; (4) the automated guardrails (Q90) that catch what people forget, since even well-trained engineers slip under deadline pressure — tooling should be the safety net, not the primary teaching method.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The key insight: people remember friction they personally experienced far better than rules they were told. A single hands-on session where an engineer genuinely struggles to use their own feature by keyboard alone typically produces more lasting behavior change than months of passive documentation ever will.

Q93. How do you prioritize an accessibility backlog when you can't fix everything at once?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Score each item on a small number of axes — severity/blocking-ness (does it fully prevent task completion, or is it friction?), traffic/business-criticality of the affected flow, and fix cost (a shared-component fix with multiplicative payoff versus a one-off page tweak) — and let root-cause fixes in shared components jump the queue, since they resolve many individual symptoms in a single unit of work (echoing Q71's grouping approach applied to ongoing backlog management, not just a one-time audit).

Detailed Answer

A practical scoring approach many teams use: multiply (or otherwise combine) severity × traffic, then subtract for fix cost, and specifically boost anything that's a shared component or pattern (since one fix propagates everywhere it's used). This naturally surfaces a small set of high-leverage items — a broken date-picker component used on 40 pages usually outranks a contrast tweak on one rarely-visited settings page, even if the settings-page issue was reported first or feels more urgent because a specific customer complained about it.

It's also worth explicitly protecting a fixed percentage of ongoing capacity for accessibility debt (rather than treating it purely as a backlog competing item by item against feature work every planning cycle), so genuine progress happens continuously instead of only during periodic crisis-driven pushes.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The senior signal: resisting the urge to prioritize purely by "who complained loudest" or recency, and instead using a consistent, explainable scoring framework — it produces both better outcomes and a defensible answer when a stakeholder asks "why isn't my reported issue fixed yet?"

Q95. How do you prevent accessibility regressions after a big redesign or a rewrite to a new component library?

ROI: ★★★★ Frequency: High

Short Answer

Treat the migration itself as an accessibility-testing checkpoint, not just a visual one: re-run the full automated suite (axe/Lighthouse/jest-axe) against the new implementation before rollout, re-do a manual keyboard and screen-reader pass on the highest-traffic flows specifically (since new components can silently regress focus order, ARIA wiring, or keyboard patterns that the old ones had correctly solved), and if adopting a new component library, verify its accessibility posture up front — reputable headless libraries (Radix, React Aria) are a strong signal precisely because they've already solved this, but don't assume it without checking.

Detailed Answer

Redesigns are a disproportionately common source of accessibility regressions precisely because visual QA (does it look right?) is usually thorough while accessibility QA is easy to skip under launch pressure — a new visual design can look identical or better while quietly losing focus-visible styles, breaking a previously-correct tab order, or swapping a real <button> for a styled <div> in the process. The practical safeguard: add the redesign/migration to the same CI gates as any other change (Q90) so it can't merge with new violations, and explicitly schedule a manual re-test pass on critical flows as a launch-blocking checklist item, not an optional nice-to-have squeezed in if time allows.

When adopting a new component library specifically, a quick due-diligence check — does it publish accessibility documentation, does it have a track record of NVDA/VoiceOver testing, does its own test suite include accessibility assertions — is worth doing before committing to a large-scale migration, since inheriting a library's accessibility debt is far more expensive to unwind later than choosing carefully up front.

Senior Engineer Perspective

The insight that closes out this whole chapter: accessibility isn't a property you build once and keep forever — it's a property of the current code, and any large-scale change (redesign, library migration, framework upgrade) is a genuine risk point that deserves the same rigor as the original build, not an assumption that "it looked fine in the demo."
