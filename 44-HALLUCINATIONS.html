CHAPTER 44 — HALLUCINATIONS

Volume 1 — AI Foundations · Chapter 17 of 22

The Sampling chapter showed you that every token an LLM emits is drawn from a probability distribution rather than looked up as a fact — that randomness is a feature, not a bug, when the goal is fluent, varied text. But that same mechanism has a dark side: nothing in the sampling process distinguishes "the next token that is true" from "the next token that merely sounds right." This chapter names that failure mode precisely — hallucination — and gives you the engineering vocabulary and countermeasures you will need for every retrieval, agent, and guardrail chapter that follows.

1. MOTIVATION

If there is one word that determines whether your first production LLM feature survives contact with real users, it is this one. Every other topic in this book — retrieval, tool calling, memory, evaluation, guardrails — exists at least partly because of the phenomenon this chapter names. You can build a beautifully engineered Spring Boot service around a model, with clean ChatClient code, proper retries, and elegant prompt templates, and still ship something that quietly invents a refund policy, cites a court case that does not exist, or confidently states a wrong version number for a library — and it will say all of this in the same fluent, well-punctuated voice it uses when it is completely correct.

This is not a rare edge case you can patch away with a better prompt. It is a structural consequence of how these models are trained and how they generate text, which you already understand from the Transformers, Training, and Sampling chapters. Understanding hallucination deeply — not just "models sometimes lie" but precisely which failure modes are structural, why they happen mechanically, and where in a production pipeline you can intervene — is the single highest-leverage piece of knowledge separating engineers who ship demos from engineers who ship systems that survive an enterprise's legal, compliance, and support teams.

It also happens to be one of the most reliably asked interview topics for any AI Engineering role above junior level, because it is the fastest way for an interviewer to test whether a candidate actually understands the model they are wrapping, or is just gluing an SDK to a UI.

2. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

The word "hallucination" as an AI term of art did not begin with chatbots. It was borrowed from computer vision and image captioning research in the mid-2010s, where researchers observed that neural image-captioning systems would describe objects that were not in the image at all — a model shown a photo of an empty table might confidently caption it "a cat sitting on a table," because "cat" and "table" frequently co-occurred in its training captions. The model was not consulting the pixels for that detail; it was pattern-completing based on what usually goes together. The term stuck because it captured something visceral: the system was perceiving something that was never there, the way a person hallucinates a sensory experience with no external stimulus behind it.

Before that, the underlying problem already had a long history in Natural Language Generation (NLG) research, under less evocative names: faithfulness and fidelity. Automatic summarization systems in the 2000s and early 2010s (long before Transformers) were known to produce summaries that were fluent and grammatical but factually inconsistent with the source document — a summarizer might merge two separate facts from an article into one incorrect combined claim, simply because the sentence "sounded" like a reasonable summary sentence. Statistical machine translation systems had a parallel problem: translations that read naturally in the target language but subtly changed the meaning of the source, a phenomenon translators call "fluent but unfaithful."

Rule-based and retrieval-based chatbots of the pre-Transformer era (from ELIZA in the 1960s through early-2010s scripted assistants) rarely "hallucinated" in this sense at all — not because they were smarter, but because they were incapable of generating novel factual claims in the first place. ELIZA reflected your own words back at you; a scripted customer-service bot selected from a fixed library of canned answers. You cannot hallucinate a fact you are structurally incapable of inventing. The moment the field moved to generative sequence models — first RNN-based encoder-decoders for translation and summarization, then Transformer-based large language models trained on internet-scale text — the systems gained the generative freedom to produce genuinely novel sentences, and with that freedom came the ability to produce fluent, novel, false sentences. Hallucination is, in a very real sense, the shadow cast by generative capability. You cannot have one without the risk of the other.

The public inflection point was the wave of general-purpose conversational LLMs beginning in late 2022. Because these models could hold open-ended conversations and answer nearly any question with confident, well-formatted prose, hallucination stopped being an obscure NLG-research metric and became front-page news: fabricated legal citations submitted in real court filings, invented academic references in generated literature reviews, confidently wrong biographical claims about real people. The research community responded with a wave of benchmarks (TruthfulQA, HaluEval, and others) specifically designed to measure how often and in what circumstances models hallucinate — work that directly feeds the evaluation techniques covered later in this series.

3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

Define, detect, and reduce the rate at which a language model generates fluent, plausible-sounding content that is not true, not supported by the provided context, or not verifiable — while preserving the model's genuine usefulness at open-ended generation.

Notice the phrase "while preserving genuine usefulness." This is what makes hallucination a genuinely hard engineering problem rather than a simple bug to patch. The exact same generative mechanism that lets a model write an original cover letter, brainstorm five marketing taglines, or summarize a document in a style you specify is the mechanism that lets it fabricate a citation. You cannot flip a switch that turns off "generate content not literally copied from training data" without also destroying the model's usefulness for every creative and synthesizing task. The engineering problem, precisely, is: how do you keep the generative capability for tasks that want it (drafting, brainstorming, rephrasing) while constraining or verifying the model where factual grounding matters (citing a policy, stating a number, naming an API)?

This immediately implies that "solve hallucination" is the wrong framing for any production system. The correct framing, which this chapter builds toward, is: for each feature you ship, classify how much factual grounding it requires, then apply the matching combination of grounding, verification, and guardrail techniques — because no single technique eliminates hallucination outright.

4. THEORY

4.1 A Working Definition

Hallucination is generated content that is fluent and internally coherent but is either (a) factually false relative to the real world, or (b) unsupported by / inconsistent with a source the model was explicitly given (a retrieved document, a system prompt, a conversation history). Crucially, hallucination is defined by a mismatch with ground truth or provided context, not by how the text reads. A hallucinated sentence is often grammatically perfect, well-organized, and stated with exactly the same confident tone as a correct sentence — this is precisely what makes it dangerous. As the Sampling chapter established, the model has no internal notion of "confidence" in the human sense attached to its output tone; a token's fluency and a token's truth are two entirely separate axes that happen to be correlated most of the time and catastrophically uncorrelated some of the time.

4.2 The Core Taxonomy: Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic

The research literature on faithfulness (inherited from summarization research, Section 2) gives you the cleanest taxonomy, and it maps directly onto production failure categories:

TypeDefinitionExampleRoot Cause
Intrinsic hallucinationThe output directly contradicts information present in the given source/contextA RAG system is given a document stating "the warranty is 12 months" and the model answers "the warranty is 24 months"The model failed to faithfully attend to and use the provided context
Extrinsic hallucinationThe output adds information that cannot be verified from the given source, whether or not it happens to be trueA RAG system given only a warranty document has the model add "and this warranty is transferable to a new owner" — a plausible-sounding claim the source never madeThe model filled a gap using its parametric (training-time) knowledge or pure pattern completion, instead of admitting the source doesn't say
Factuality hallucination (closed-book)With no external source at all, the model's claim about the real world is simply false"The Eiffel Tower was completed in 1901" (it was 1889)Parametric knowledge was never learned correctly, was learned then "forgotten"/interfered with, or the fact is rare/long-tail and under-represented in training data

Notice that "extrinsic" and "closed-book factuality" hallucinations are really the same underlying mechanism (the model drawing on its trained-in statistical knowledge instead of an authoritative source) viewed from two different production contexts (with vs. without retrieval). This single distinction — faithfulness to a given context versus correctness about the real world — is the axis every mitigation strategy in this chapter is organized around, and it is also the axis on which most engineers new to this field get confused, conflating "the model is wrong" (factuality) with "the model ignored what I told it" (faithfulness). They require different fixes.

4.3 The Mental Model: A Brilliant, Overconfident Improviser

Build your intuition with this analogy, extending the one from earlier in this volume: imagine an extraordinarily well-read improv actor who has memorized the general shape and cadence of legal briefs, medical charts, API documentation, and product manuals, but who has never actually practiced law, medicine, or software, and has no live access to today's newspaper. Put this actor on stage and ask them to "continue this legal brief" or "cite the relevant case law," and they will do so instantly, fluently, and in exactly the right register — because they are not recalling a specific memorized fact from a database, they are improvising in the style and shape of the genre. Most of the time, because the genre's shape correlates strongly with genre's typical content, the improvisation happens to be true. But an improviser under pressure to produce a citation, a name, or a number, and unwilling to break character and say "I don't actually know," will generate a syntactically perfect, contextually appropriate, entirely fabricated case number. That is hallucination, exactly. The model was never trained with an "I refuse to guess" reflex baked in by default — it was trained to keep producing the next plausible token, and a well-formed fake citation is, token by token, extremely plausible.

4.4 Why Confidence Language Is Not a Signal

A critical sub-point, because it trips up experienced engineers: phrases like "I am confident that," "studies clearly show," or "it is well established that" are themselves just tokens the model learned are common in confident-sounding prose. They carry zero calibrated relationship to the model's actual internal uncertainty (which, as covered in the Sampling chapter, does exist in the token probability distribution, but is rarely surfaced to you as text). A model can be simultaneously highly "uncertain" in its underlying probability distribution over the next token (many candidate tokens with similar probability mass) and phrase its answer in maximally confident prose, because confident phrasing is simply the more common style in its training data for the type of question being asked.

5. INTERNAL WORKING

To reason about hallucination the way a senior engineer does — able to predict where in a new pipeline it will strike — you need to trace the mechanism step by step, connecting back to the Transformer architecture and training process covered earlier in this volume.

5.1 Step 1 — Knowledge Is Stored as Statistical Association, Not as Facts in a Database

During training, the model's parameters are nudged, via the predict-measure-adjust loop from the Training chapter, to make the training corpus more predictable. This process does not create discrete, addressable "facts" the way a row in a relational table is a discrete, addressable fact. Instead, it distributes statistical regularities across billions of parameters. A fact that appeared thousands of times, consistently and unambiguously, in training data (e.g., "Paris is the capital of France") gets encoded as an extremely strong, well-reinforced association, and the model will reproduce it reliably. A fact that appeared rarely, inconsistently, or was entangled with many similar-sounding but different facts (a mid-tier company's founding year, a specific version number in a fast-changing API, an obscure legal statute) gets encoded weakly, ambiguously, or not distinguishably from similar alternatives. There is no mechanism inside the model that flags "this one is weak, hedge your answer" at generation time by default — the generation procedure treats a weakly-supported association and a strongly-supported one identically: sample from whatever distribution the parameters produce.

5.2 Step 2 — Generation Never Consults an External Ground Truth Unless You Force It To

Left to itself (what the industry calls "closed-book" generation — no retrieval, no tools, no supplied context), the model has exactly one source of information at inference time: the frozen parameters set during training, plus whatever is in the current context window. If the true answer is not well-represented in either, the model does not have a "return null" or "throw NotFoundException" behavior built in by default. The next-token prediction objective it was trained on has no concept of "no answer" as a first-class outcome unless the training data specifically taught it, through examples, that saying "I don't know" is itself a valid and rewarded completion in cases of genuine uncertainty (a behavior modern providers now deliberately train for via reinforcement learning from human feedback, covered later in this series, with only partial success).

5.3 Step 3 — Autoregressive Generation Compounds Small Errors

Recall from the Transformers and Sampling chapters that generation is autoregressive: each new token is generated conditioned on all previous tokens, including the ones the model itself just generated. This creates a subtle but important amplifying effect. If, at token 40 of an answer, the model samples a token that introduces a small factual drift (say, naming the wrong year), every subsequent token is now generated conditioned on that wrong year being part of the "established" context. The model does not go back and revise; it continues building a fluent, internally consistent narrative on top of the error, because internal consistency (not correctness) is what its training objective rewards. This is why hallucinations often "snowball" — a single wrong premise early in a long answer gets elaborated into an increasingly detailed and increasingly wrong structure, each additional sentence sounding more authoritative because it is consistent with what came immediately before it, even though what came before it was already false.

5.4 Step 4 — Context Competes for Attention, and Grounding Can Lose

Even when you do the right thing and supply grounding context (a retrieved document, a system-prompt fact, a tool result), the model's attention mechanism must still choose, token by token, how much weight to give that supplied context versus its own parametric priors and versus other parts of the prompt. In long contexts, supplied facts can be "attended away from" — a phenomenon sometimes called "lost in the middle," where information placed in the middle of a long prompt is statistically less likely to be faithfully used than information near the beginning or end. This is precisely why intrinsic hallucination (contradicting a source you literally handed the model) still happens even in well-built RAG systems: providing the correct document does not guarantee the model faithfully uses it, it only raises the probability that it will.

5.5 Step 5 — Sampling Adds a Final Layer of Variance

Finally, whatever decoding strategy you use — greedy, top-k, nucleus/top-p, with some temperature (all covered in the Sampling chapter) — introduces its own variance on top of everything above. A prompt that would have produced a faithful answer under greedy decoding can, at higher temperature, sample a lower-probability-but-still-plausible token that steers the rest of the generation toward a hallucinated path. This is why the same prompt, run multiple times, can hallucinate on some runs and not others — hallucination is not a fixed property of a prompt, it is a probabilistic outcome of a prompt.

6. MATHEMATICS

This topic does not need heavy formalism to reason about correctly, but two light quantitative ideas sharpen your intuition and appear directly in evaluation and guardrail tooling later in this series.

6.1 Entropy of the Next-Token Distribution as a Proxy for Uncertainty

Recall from the Sampling chapter that at every generation step, the model produces a full probability distribution over its entire vocabulary before a token is sampled. The entropy of that distribution is a single number capturing how "spread out" it is: 

Low entropy (model is "sure")            High entropy (model is "unsure")

Token:  Paris   London  Berlin           Token:   1997   2001   2003   1994  ...
Prob:   0.94    0.03    0.02             Prob:    0.14   0.13   0.12   0.11  ...

  ▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇                       ▃▃  ▃▃  ▃▂  ▃▂  ▃▂  ▃▁  ▃▁ ...
  ▁▁                                       (probability mass spread thin
  (one token dominates)                     across many near-equal candidates)

High entropy at a token position where a specific fact (a date, a name, a number) is being generated is a statistical red flag: it means the model's training did not leave behind one dominant, well-reinforced association for that position, and it is effectively picking among several similarly-plausible candidates — exactly the situation where a hallucinated value is likely to be sampled. This is the mathematical basis for practical uncertainty-estimation and hallucination-detection techniques (such as measuring token-level or sequence-level log-probabilities, or running the same prompt multiple times and checking for disagreement between samples, an approach sometimes called self-consistency checking).

6.2 Self-Consistency as a Cheap, Practical Signal

A simple, engineer-friendly way to operationalize this: generate the same answer N times (varying the random seed / temperature slightly) and measure agreement between the samples. If a factual claim (a number, a name, a date) is stable across all N samples, it is drawing on a strong, well-reinforced association — low hallucination risk. If it varies across samples, the model is effectively "guessing" at that token position — a strong hallucination signal you can act on (flag for human review, suppress, or trigger a retrieval/verification step) without needing access to the model's raw internal logits at all, which matters because many hosted model APIs do not expose full token probabilities.

Formally, if you generate samples y₁, y₂, ..., y_N for the same prompt, a simple consistency score for a specific claim c extracted from the answer is the fraction of samples in which a semantically equivalent claim appears. Low scores correlate strongly, in practice, with hallucinated content — this is the mechanism behind several production hallucination-detection tools built on top of hosted, logit-opaque APIs.

8. PRODUCTION ARCHITECTURE

No single component "fixes" hallucination. Production systems reduce its rate and, more importantly, reduce its blast radius through layered defenses, each catching what the previous layer missed.

Notice the shape of this pipeline: it is defense in depth, exactly like a security architecture. No single layer is trusted to be sufficient on its own, because — as Section 5 established mechanically — hallucination is a probabilistic property of generation, not a deterministic bug with one root cause to patch. A mature AI Engineering team budgets for all six layers on any feature where a wrong answer has real cost (a support bot answering pricing questions, a legal-document assistant, a medical-information tool), and consciously relaxes some layers (skipping layer 4 and 5, for instance) for lower-stakes, creative-generation features (a marketing copy brainstorming tool) where over-constraining would destroy the feature's value.

A concrete, realistic sketch of a grounded, self-checking call using Spring AI idioms: 

@Service
public class PolicyQaService {

    private final ChatClient chatClient;
    private final VectorStore vectorStore;

    public PolicyQaService(ChatClient.Builder builder, VectorStore vectorStore) {
        this.vectorStore = vectorStore;
        this.chatClient = builder
                .defaultAdvisors(new QuestionAnswerAdvisor(vectorStore))
                .defaultSystem("""
                        You are a company policy assistant.
                        Answer ONLY using the CONTEXT documents provided.
                        If the answer is not present in the context,
                        respond exactly: "This is not covered in our documented policy."
                        Never state a specific number, date, or dollar amount
                        that does not literally appear in the context.
                        """)
                .build();
    }

    public PolicyAnswer answer(String question) {
        // 1. Grounded, structured generation
        PolicyAnswer draft = chatClient.prompt()
                .user(question)
                .call()
                .entity(PolicyAnswer.class);

        // 2. Cheap self-consistency check: re-ask, compare key fields
        PolicyAnswer check = chatClient.prompt()
                .user(question)
                .call()
                .entity(PolicyAnswer.class);

        if (!draft.keyFact().equals(check.keyFact())) {
            draft = draft.toBuilder()
                    .needsHumanReview(true)
                    .build();
        }
        return draft;
    }
}

public record PolicyAnswer(
        String keyFact,
        String citedSourcePassage,
        boolean needsHumanReview) {}

Nothing here is exotic — it is ChatClient, an Advisor, structured output binding, and a plain Java equality check, applied with the specific goal of catching the failure modes Section 5 walked through mechanically. This is the throughline of this entire book series: Spring AI does not remove the need to understand hallucination, it gives you the composable primitives to engineer around it the same disciplined way you already engineer around any unreliable dependency.

10. BEST PRACTICES

  • Classify every feature by grounding requirement before writing a prompt. A brainstorming tool tolerates a high hallucination rate; a feature that states a price, a legal obligation, or a medical fact does not. Decide this up front and size your defenses (Section 8) accordingly — do not apply the same guardrail budget uniformly everywhere.
  • Prefer retrieval and tool calls over asking the model to "remember" precise facts. Anything with a canonical, changeable source of truth (prices, inventory, policy text, today's date) belongs in a database lookup or tool call, not in the model's parametric memory, per Section 5.2.
  • Explicitly instruct the model that "I don't know" / "not found in context" is an acceptable, rewarded answer. Left unstated, the default generative pressure is to always produce a plausible-sounding completion; you must counteract this in the prompt.
  • Require citations to specific retrieved passages, and verify the citation actually contains the claim. A model asked to cite is more likely to stay faithful to the source (attention is drawn back to the passage), and a citation that does not verify is a strong, cheap hallucination signal you can check programmatically.
  • Use self-consistency sampling for high-stakes numeric or factual claims. As Section 6.2 showed, disagreement across repeated samples is a practical, logit-free uncertainty signal you can act on even against hosted APIs that don't expose raw probabilities.
  • Build and continuously grow a real evaluation set from production hallucination cases (user corrections, support escalations, thumbs-down feedback) rather than relying only on generic public benchmarks — your domain's long-tail facts are exactly where generic benchmarks won't help you.

11. COMMON MISTAKES

  • Believing RAG "solves" hallucination. Retrieval reduces factuality hallucination by supplying current, authoritative context, but it does nothing to guarantee faithfulness — intrinsic hallucination (contradicting the very document supplied) is a separate failure mode with a separate cause (attention dilution, "lost in the middle"), not fixed by retrieval alone.
  • Treating a model's confident tone as a correctness signal in code or in UX copy. As Section 4.4 explained, confidence language is a stylistic artifact of training data, not a calibrated probability. Never let a feature's error handling implicitly trust "the model sounded sure."
  • Testing hallucination mitigations with a handful of hand-picked happy-path questions. This mirrors the generalization mistake from earlier chapters — a mitigation that works on five easy test questions tells you almost nothing about the long tail of real user phrasing where hallucination actually strikes hardest.
  • Stuffing more and more context into the prompt "to be safe." Beyond a point, this increases the "lost in the middle" risk described in Section 5.4 rather than reducing hallucination — more context is not strictly safer context; relevance and placement matter more than volume.
  • Assuming a bigger, more capable model hallucinates proportionally less on your specific domain. Larger general-purpose models are typically more fluent and more broadly knowledgeable, but on narrow, fast-changing, or proprietary domain facts (your company's own policies, your own product's API), a smaller model with good retrieval will usually out-perform a larger model with none, because the fact was never going to be in either model's training data regardless of size.
  • Ignoring hallucination in structured/JSON output because "it's just a schema." Forcing output into a valid schema constrains the shape of the answer, not its truth — a perfectly valid JSON object can still contain a fabricated field value.

12. PERFORMANCE CONSIDERATIONS

Every hallucination mitigation from Section 8 has a real latency, throughput, and cost cost, and a senior engineer's job is to spend that budget only where the stakes justify it.

  • Retrieval adds a round trip. A vector search plus document formatting typically adds tens to low-hundreds of milliseconds before the model call even starts — usually worth it for grounding, but it is not free, and it compounds with the model's own latency.
  • Self-consistency checking multiplies model calls linearly. Sampling N times to check agreement (Section 6.2) means Nx the token cost and, if run sequentially, roughly Nx the latency for that step — in practice, teams run this selectively (only on claims flagged as high-stakes or high-entropy) rather than on every single response, and run the extra samples concurrently to bound latency.
  • A separate "judge" verification call doubles cost on the verified path. Using a second, often smaller/cheaper model to check faithfulness against retrieved sources is a common pattern, precisely because a smaller judge model is usually sufficient for a narrower verification task and costs far less than re-running the full generation model.
  • Guardrail/policy layers add a filtering pass. Rules-based or classifier-based filters on high-stakes categories are typically cheap (milliseconds) compared to any additional model call, and should usually be your first line of defense before reaching for a more expensive verification model call.
  • The real cost trade-off is asymmetric. The cost of an extra verification pass is measured in cents and milliseconds; the cost of a hallucinated legal, medical, or financial claim reaching a user is measured in incident response, reputational damage, and in some domains, regulatory exposure. Size your mitigation spend against the cost of the failure, not against a flat "keep latency under X ms" target applied uniformly to every feature.

13. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

Hallucination has a real, if sometimes underappreciated, security surface — and unlike some purely conceptual topics, this one has a documented, concrete attack pattern worth knowing by name: slopsquatting. When developers ask a code-generation model to suggest a library or package to import, the model can hallucinate a plausible-sounding but non-existent package name (following the same "fluent completion of a familiar pattern" mechanism from Section 5.1). Attackers have registered exactly these hallucinated package names on public package registries, so a developer who trusts the model's suggestion and installs the "recommended" package pulls down attacker-controlled code. This is a direct, real-world consequence of treating fluent model output as verified fact in a security-sensitive action (installing a dependency) without the verification layer from Section 8.

More broadly, hallucination interacts with prompt injection (introduced conceptually in the Symbolic AI discussion earlier in this volume and covered in depth in Volume 5): an attacker who can influence retrieved context or tool output can deliberately induce a targeted hallucination — for example, injecting text into a document that a RAG pipeline will retrieve, worded to make the model "confidently" state a false instruction, a false price, or a false authorization decision. In this sense, hallucination is not only an accuracy problem but also an amplifier for any injected content: if the model faithfully (or unfaithfully) reproduces manipulated context in a confident tone, downstream systems or humans acting on that output inherit the manipulation with no obvious red flag in the surface text.

Beyond these two concrete patterns, this topic's security surface is genuinely narrower than topics like authentication or injection — hallucination on its own does not grant an attacker code execution or data access. Its danger is downstream: whatever automated or human action is taken because a hallucinated claim was trusted. This is exactly why Section 8's verification and guardrail layers matter most precisely at the boundary where a model's output triggers a real-world action (an automated refund, a merged pull request, an installed dependency) rather than merely being displayed as advisory text to a human who can sanity-check it.

14. INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

JUNIORQ1. In one sentence, what is an LLM hallucination?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

A hallucination is fluent, confident-sounding output generated by a language model that is factually false or unsupported by any source/context provided to it — the model generates plausible text, not verified fact.

JUNIORQ2. Why can't you just tell the model "don't hallucinate" in the prompt and trust that it won't?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Instructing the model not to hallucinate can somewhat reduce the rate (especially by making "I don't know" a more acceptable completion), but it cannot eliminate it, because hallucination is a structural consequence of how the model generates text — sampling from a learned probability distribution with no built-in fact-checking step — not a switch the model can consciously flip off on command. The instruction competes for attention with everything else in the prompt and does not change the underlying generation mechanism.

MIDQ3. What is the difference between an "intrinsic" and an "extrinsic" hallucination, and why does that distinction matter for RAG systems?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

An intrinsic hallucination directly contradicts information present in the supplied source/context (a faithfulness failure — the model was given the right answer and still got it wrong). An extrinsic hallucination adds information that cannot be verified from the supplied source, drawing instead on parametric training knowledge or pure pattern completion. It matters for RAG because retrieval only reduces the second category by supplying better source material — it does nothing to guarantee the model faithfully uses that source, so a well-built RAG system can still intrinsically hallucinate by ignoring or misreading the very document it retrieved.

MIDQ4. Why does a model's confident tone not indicate that its answer is correct?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Confident-sounding phrasing ("studies show," "it is well established that") is learned as a common stylistic pattern in fluent, authoritative-register training text, not as a calibrated readout of the model's internal uncertainty. The model's actual uncertainty exists in the probability distribution over candidate tokens (measurable via entropy or log-probabilities) but is a completely separate signal from the surface-level tone of the generated text, which is optimized for fluency, not calibration.

SENIORQ5. Design a hallucination-mitigation strategy for a customer-facing support assistant that answers questions from a knowledge base. Walk through your layers and justify each one.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Very High at senior level

Start with a grounding layer: retrieve the most relevant knowledge-base articles via a vector store and inject them into context (reduces factuality hallucination from stale/absent parametric knowledge). Add explicit prompting instructions requiring the model to answer only from supplied context and to say "not found" otherwise (reduces both hallucination types by making abstention an acceptable output). Force any precise, high-stakes value — account balance, order status, exact pricing — through a tool/function call to the actual backend system rather than free-text generation, since these are exactly the values a model is most likely to guess incorrectly. Require the model to cite the specific source passage for each claim, and programmatically verify the cited passage actually contains the claim before returning the answer (a lightweight faithfulness check). For a defined set of high-stakes categories (refunds, legal disclaimers, safety issues), route to human review regardless of model confidence. Finally, log every answer with its sources and any user feedback (thumbs down, follow-up correction) to build a growing, domain-specific evaluation set that measures the real hallucination rate over time, not just a generic benchmark score.

Interview Gold Point: Explicitly say that no single layer is trusted alone — call it "defense in depth," draw the parallel to security architecture, and note that you would size the investment in each layer by the actual cost of a wrong answer in that category, not apply the same guardrail budget uniformly to every feature. Also mention that RAG alone does not solve faithfulness — interviewers specifically listen for whether a candidate conflates "we added retrieval" with "we solved hallucination."
SENIORQ6. How would you measure hallucination rate in a production system where you don't have access to the model's raw token probabilities (a hosted API)?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Use self-consistency sampling: re-run the same prompt (or a paraphrase of it) multiple times with slightly varied sampling parameters, extract the key factual claims from each response, and measure agreement across the samples — low agreement on a specific claim is a strong signal that the model is effectively guessing at that point rather than drawing on a strong, well-reinforced association, without needing access to internal logits. Combine this with an automated "judge" model that checks each answer's claims against the retrieved source documents for faithfulness, and with a slower but higher-quality feedback loop from real user corrections and support escalations to build a labeled evaluation set over time. Track both a faithfulness metric (did the answer stick to the provided source) and a correctness metric (is it actually true) separately, since a system can score well on one and poorly on the other.

SENIORQ7. What is "lost in the middle," and why does it matter for hallucination even when the correct information is present in the prompt?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at senior level

"Lost in the middle" refers to the empirically observed tendency for information placed in the middle of a long context window to be used less reliably by the model than information near the beginning or end, because the attention mechanism does not weight all positions in the context equally in practice. This matters for hallucination because simply retrieving and including the correct document does not guarantee the model faithfully uses it — if the relevant fact is buried in the middle of a large context (for example, several long retrieved documents concatenated together), the model may still intrinsically hallucinate by ignoring it or blending it with less relevant nearby text, even though, in principle, the correct answer was available in the prompt the whole time.

STAFFQ8. A product leader wants to ship a legal-document Q&A feature and says "let's just use the biggest, most capable model so it doesn't hallucinate." How do you respond, and what would you actually recommend?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Model scale improves general fluency and broad-domain factuality but does not specifically address the two failure modes that matter most for a legal-document feature: intrinsic hallucination (misreading or contradicting the specific contract/document supplied) and extrinsic hallucination on domain-specific, low-frequency facts (this client's specific clause numbering, a jurisdiction-specific statute) that no general-purpose model, regardless of size, was trained deeply on. A bigger model without grounding can still confidently fabricate a clause. The staff-level recommendation is architectural, not "buy a bigger model": ground every answer in the actual supplied document via retrieval or direct document inclusion with citation requirements, require the model to quote the exact source passage for every substantive claim, add an automated faithfulness-verification pass comparing claims against the source, route anything touching binding legal interpretation to mandatory human attorney review regardless of model confidence, and evaluate the system against a legal-domain-specific test set built from real documents and known-correct answers rather than trusting a generic capability benchmark. Model choice becomes a secondary optimization (cost/latency for a given faithfulness bar) once the grounding and verification architecture is in place, not the primary hallucination defense.

STAFFQ9. Explain "slopsquatting" and what it reveals about how hallucination becomes a security incident, not just an accuracy problem.

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

Slopsquatting is the practice of attackers registering package names that language models are known to hallucinate when asked to suggest a library for a given task — because the fabricated name follows the same plausible naming conventions as real packages in that ecosystem, and models tend to hallucinate the same fake names repeatedly for the same prompts. A developer who trusts a code-generation model's suggested import and installs it without verification pulls down attacker-controlled code. It reveals the general lesson that hallucination stops being merely an accuracy metric the moment its output triggers a real-world action with no verification step in between — the security exposure lives not in the model itself but at the boundary where an unverified, confidently-stated claim is allowed to cause a side effect (installing a dependency, executing a transaction, merging code). The mitigation is architectural, not model-side: never let generated identifiers (package names, API endpoints, file paths) be acted upon without checking them against an authoritative registry or allowlist first.

16. SUMMARY

  • Hallucination is fluent, confident-sounding content that is factually false or unsupported by supplied context — a structural consequence of autoregressive, probability-based generation, not a rare bug.
  • The core taxonomy — intrinsic (contradicts supplied context) vs. extrinsic/factuality (unsupported or false relative to the real world) — determines which mitigation actually applies; retrieval fixes one category far more than the other.
  • Mechanically, hallucination arises from knowledge stored as statistical association rather than discrete fact, no built-in "I don't know" default, compounding errors across autoregressive steps, attention competing across long contexts, and sampling variance layered on top of all of it.
  • No single technique eliminates hallucination; production systems use defense in depth — grounding, prompting, structured/tool-forced output, verification, guardrails, and observability — sized to the real cost of a wrong answer for each specific feature.
  • Spring AI gives you concrete primitives (QuestionAnswerAdvisor, tool calling, structured output binding, custom Advisor verification) for exactly these layers, extending the same disciplined-dependency mindset you already use for unreliable REST APIs.
  • Hallucination's security surface is narrower than classic vulnerabilities but real and concrete — slopsquatting and injection-amplification both show that the danger lives at the boundary where unverified model output triggers a real-world action.
