CHAPTER 10 — DESIGNING A URL SHORTENER

Volume 5 — Case Studies · Chapter 10 of 12

Every system design interview guide in existence opens its case-study section with a URL shortener, and for good reason: it is the smallest possible problem that still forces you through the entire discipline this track has been building toward — scoping (Chapter 01), capacity estimation (Chapter 01), an encoding decision with real trade-offs, a data model choice that is correct specifically because it resists over-engineering, and a read path dominated by caching (Chapter 04). Nothing in this chapter introduces a new building block. Everything in it is you, for the first time, assembling eight chapters of prior material into one coherent design under a single, deceptively simple prompt: "design bit.ly." If you can do this chapter cleanly, in order, with real numbers, you have demonstrated the exact skill the rest of this volume's case studies will keep demanding at higher difficulty.

1. MOTIVATION

A URL shortener looks almost insultingly simple the first time you hear the prompt: take a long URL, hand back a short one, and when someone visits the short one, send them to the long one. A junior engineer can describe that behavior in one sentence and a single database table. The reason interviewers still reach for this problem, year after year, is that the one-sentence description is a trap — it describes the functional requirement perfectly and tells you nothing about the actual engineering problem, which only appears once you ask how many times a day this happens and at what ratio of reads to writes.

This chapter exists to show you exactly where that trap springs. The naive instinct is to reach immediately for a big, sophisticated, heavily-partitioned system because "it has to handle web scale." The correct instinct, and the one this chapter trains, is the one Chapter 01 insisted on: scope the problem, estimate the load with real arithmetic, and only then decide whether you need a simple key-value table behind a cache (you almost always do) or something heavier (you almost never do, at the scale this product realistically reaches). A URL shortener is, in a very literal sense, the chapter that tests whether you actually internalized Chapter 01's warning about over-engineering for a scale nobody asked for.

There is also a direct career incentive here. Because this exact problem is so commonly asked, interviewers have heard every shallow answer imaginable — "we'll hash the URL with MD5" delivered with no discussion of collisions, or "we'll shard the database" delivered with no math showing why sharding is even necessary at this scale. Doing this one well, with the specific trade-off reasoning this chapter walks through, is one of the highest-leverage forty-five minutes you can prepare for, precisely because so many candidates treat it as too simple to prepare for at all.

2. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

2.1 Why Short URLs Existed Before "URL Shorteners" Did

URL shortening is not a cloud-era invention. It predates Twitter, predates the smartphone, and even predates widespread broadband. In the early web, URLs generated by dynamic, database-backed sites were often extremely long — query strings carrying session IDs, tracking parameters, and nested resource identifiers could run to hundreds of characters. Pasting one of those into an email client or, worse, a printed document or a early-2000s forum post with a character limit, was genuinely painful. The earliest shortening services (TinyURL, launched in 2002, is the most cited example) solved a purely cosmetic and practical problem: making an unwieldy URL shareable in a constrained text context.

2.2 The Twitter-Driven Inflection Point (2006–2010)

The service's importance jumped an order of magnitude with Twitter's original 140-character limit. A single long URL could consume the entire character budget of a tweet, leaving no room for commentary. This created sudden, massive demand for shortening at a scale the original cosmetic-use-case services were never built for, and it is the direct reason bit.ly (launched 2008) grew from a minor utility into core social-media infrastructure almost overnight — Twitter itself briefly made bit.ly its default shortener. This period is also what turned "design a URL shortener" from an obscure trivia question into the canonical system design interview prompt: it was, for a few years, one of the highest-QPS, highest-stakes pieces of infrastructure a mid-sized engineering team could be asked to operate, and its requirements (huge read:write skew, must never be down, redirect latency directly affects a user's experience of an unrelated product) are unusually clean and teachable.

2.3 From Convenience to Infrastructure: Analytics and Branding (2010s)

As shortened links became ubiquitous, the service's value proposition expanded beyond mere compression. Click analytics (who clicked, when, from where) turned a shortener into a marketing and attribution tool, and custom branded domains and aliases (a company's own short domain, a memorable custom slug) turned it into a product feature rather than a utility. This is precisely why Section 3's functional requirements below include custom aliases and why Section 5 treats analytics as a first-class, if optional, extension rather than an afterthought — the historical product actually grew in that direction, and interviewers often probe exactly this extension as a follow-up.

2.4 The Interview Artifact That Survived the Product's Decline

Ironically, dedicated URL-shortening products have become less central to the consumer web than they were at their 2009–2012 peak — social platforms now handle link previews and character limits natively, and the specific business of "generic third-party shortener" consolidated to a handful of players. What survived, and arguably grew, is the system design interview's use of the problem, because the underlying engineering shape — extreme read-write skew, a trivial data model, a decision between several equally defensible encoding strategies, and a caching-dominated read path — remains one of the cleanest teaching vehicles for the exact skills Chapters 01, 04, and 05 of this track cover. You are not learning a dying product; you are learning the cleanest available exercise in applying live, foundational distributed-systems skills.

3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

Design a service that converts a long URL into a short, unique alias, redirects visitors from that alias to the original URL with minimal added latency, and remains highly available and consistent under a workload where reads vastly outnumber writes.

Applying Chapter 01's scoping discipline before any architecture, the functional requirements this chapter commits to are:

  • Shorten: given a long URL, return a short code (e.g., short.ly/aZ9kLp) that redirects to it.
  • Redirect: given a short code, issue an HTTP redirect to the original long URL, as fast as possible.
  • Custom alias (optional, user-specified): a user may request a specific human-readable code (e.g., short.ly/our-sale) instead of a generated one, subject to uniqueness.
  • Expiry: a short URL may optionally expire after a set date or after a set number of clicks, after which it should no longer redirect.

The non-functional requirements are where this problem gets its actual shape, and they are deliberately extreme on one axis and relaxed on another:

  • Read-heavy, by a huge margin. A link is created once and clicked many times — a 100:1 read-to-write ratio is a reasonable starting assumption, and some real services see ratios far higher than that. Every architectural decision in Section 8 follows from this single fact.
  • Low-latency redirects. The redirect sits directly on another product's critical path (a tweet, an email, an ad) — a slow redirect is a visibly broken experience attributable to this service specifically.
  • High availability over strict consistency. A short link that resolves to the wrong URL is a serious bug; a short link that takes a few extra seconds to become resolvable after creation is an acceptable, even unnoticeable, trade — this service can tolerate eventual consistency on the write side far more easily than it can tolerate downtime on the read side.
  • Uniqueness, not confidentiality. Short codes must not collide with each other, but (unless a product requirement says otherwise) they do not need to be unguessable or cryptographically secure — this distinction matters directly in Section 4's encoding discussion.

What this chapter deliberately does not over-scope: it does not assume global multi-region active-active writes, it does not assume the data volume requires sharding from day one, and it does not assume analytics must be synchronous. Section 6's arithmetic is what earns the right to make each of those calls explicitly rather than by reflex — exactly the discipline Chapter 01 demanded and Section 11 of that chapter warned you not to skip.

4. THEORY

4.1 The Central Design Decision: How Do You Generate the Short Code?

Everything else in this chapter is relatively mechanical once this one decision is made correctly, because it determines your data model, your collision-handling strategy, and whether your write path needs coordination across servers at all. There are three real candidates.

4.2 Option A — Base62 Encoding of an Auto-Incrementing ID

Assign every new long URL a globally unique, monotonically increasing integer ID (from a database auto-increment column, or a dedicated ID-generation service), then encode that integer in base62 (the 62 characters [a-z][A-Z][0-9]) to produce the short code. Base62 is chosen specifically because it is URL-safe with no escaping needed and because its 62-character alphabet packs more information per character than base16 or base36, which keeps codes short: 62^6 is already over 56 billion distinct codes at just six characters.

This approach has one decisive advantage: it has no collisions, by construction. Two different long URLs can never be assigned the same ID, so they can never produce the same short code, and there is no retry loop or uniqueness check needed on the write path. The cost is that it requires a source of unique, ordered IDs — trivial on a single database, and a genuinely interesting distributed-systems problem (a dedicated ID-generation service, or a range-partitioned allocator) only once a single database can no longer hand out IDs fast enough, which Section 6's arithmetic will show is not this chapter's regime.

4.3 Option B — Hashing the Long URL

Run the long URL through a hash function (commonly MD5 or SHA-256, truncated to the first 6–8 characters of the resulting digest, base62-encoded), producing a short code derived from the URL's own content rather than from an incrementing counter. This has a pleasant property Option A lacks: the same long URL hashed twice produces the same short code, so a service can deduplicate — if a URL has already been shortened, return the existing code rather than minting a new one.

Its real cost is unavoidable: truncating any hash to a short code introduces collisions — two different long URLs can map to the same truncated hash, and at billions of stored records this stops being a theoretical concern. A collision requires explicit handling (check if the code is already taken by a different URL; if so, append a disambiguating character or re-hash with a salt and retry), which reintroduces exactly the kind of write-path uniqueness check and retry loop Option A avoided for free. Hashing is a reasonable choice specifically when content-based deduplication is a product requirement worth paying that retry complexity for; it is not obviously superior to Option A otherwise.

4.4 Option C — A Pre-Generated Key Service

Run an offline process that continuously generates random, unique 6–8 character codes ahead of time and stores them in a "key pool" table marked unused. When a shorten request arrives, the service simply claims one pre-generated key and marks it used — the hot write path never has to generate anything or check for collisions at request time, because uniqueness was already guaranteed when the pool was populated. This is the approach real high-scale systems (including bit.ly's own publicly described architecture) favor, because it moves all collision-avoidance and generation cost off the user-facing request path entirely, at the cost of operating a second, standalone key-generation service and pool.

Recommendation for this chapter's scope: Base62 encoding of an auto-incrementing (or distributed-sequence) ID. It is collision-free by construction, requires no retry logic on the hot write path, needs no additional standing service, and — as Section 6 shows numerically — the write volume this product realistically sees (tens to low hundreds of writes per second) never approaches the point where a single ID source becomes a bottleneck. The pre-generated key pool is the right answer if an interviewer explicitly pushes the write volume up by several orders of magnitude; naming that escalation path unprompted, the way Chapter 01's Section 10 recommends naming your own design's weak point, is a strong signal.

4.5 Custom Alias Handling

A user-requested custom alias bypasses the generated-code path entirely and instead requires an explicit uniqueness check against the data store before the record is written — this is the one place in the design where a collision check on the write path is unavoidable regardless of which generation strategy you chose, because the user, not your generator, is choosing the key. The check-then-insert must be done with a database-level uniqueness constraint on the short-code column, not merely an application-level check-then-write, to avoid a race between two users requesting the same alias simultaneously.

5. INTERNAL WORKING

5.1 The Write Path: Shortening a URL

  1. Validate the submitted long URL (well-formed, not already on a blocklist for abuse/phishing).
  2. Determine the short code. If the user supplied a custom alias, attempt an insert relying on a uniqueness constraint and return a clear conflict error if it's taken. Otherwise, allocate the next ID from the chosen ID source (Section 4.4's recommendation) and base62-encode it.
  3. Persist a record mapping the short code to the long URL, the owner, creation time, and optional expiry metadata, in the data store described in Section 5.3.
  4. Return the full short URL to the client. Nothing in this path touches the cache yet — the cache is populated lazily on first read (cache-aside), exactly as Chapter 04 describes, rather than pre-warmed on write, since most newly created links are not read often enough in their first moments to justify the extra write-path cost of a cache-fill on every create.

5.2 The Read Path: Resolving a Redirect

This is the path that matters most, given the read:write ratio from Section 3, and it should be read as a direct application of Chapter 04's cache-aside mechanics rather than a new mechanism:

  1. A request arrives for short.ly/{code}.
  2. Check expiry/click-limit metadata first if those are cached alongside the mapping; an expired code should fail fast without a wasted redirect.
  3. Check the cache (Redis or an equivalent in-memory store) for code → long_url. On a hit — which, given this workload's skew, should be the overwhelming majority of requests — return the redirect immediately without touching the database at all.
  4. On a cache miss, read the mapping from the database, populate the cache with a reasonable TTL, and then issue the redirect. Chapter 04's point about a cache being an optimization, not the system of record, applies exactly here: if the cache is flushed entirely, the service is slower but still correct, because the database remains the ground truth.
  5. Issue an HTTP 301 (permanent) or 302 (temporary) redirect depending on product requirements — a 301 lets browsers and intermediate proxies cache the redirect themselves, reducing load on this service even further, but makes per-click analytics (Section 5.4) harder to capture reliably, since a browser that has cached a 301 may never hit the server again for subsequent clicks. A 302 guarantees every click is observed by the server at the cost of forgoing browser-level caching. This is a real trade-off worth naming explicitly, not an arbitrary implementation detail.

Beyond the application-level cache, a CDN or edge-cache layer in front of the redirect endpoint matters for exactly the same reason Chapter 04 gives for static assets: a redirect is tiny, immutable (until expiry) response content, and serving it from an edge location close to the requester shaves meaningful latency off a path that, per Section 3, directly affects another product's user experience. Treat the redirect endpoint as cacheable content at the edge, not just at the application's own in-memory layer.

5.3 The Data Model and Why It Stays Simple

The core table needs remarkably few columns: 

short_code   VARCHAR(8)   PRIMARY KEY
long_url     TEXT         NOT NULL
owner_id     BIGINT       NULL
created_at   TIMESTAMP    NOT NULL
expires_at   TIMESTAMP    NULL
click_limit  INT          NULL
is_active    BOOLEAN      NOT NULL DEFAULT TRUE

A single index — the primary key on short_code — serves the entire read path's lookup pattern, because every read is a point lookup by exact short code, never a range scan or a complex join. This is precisely why a plain relational table or a simple key-value store (DynamoDB, Cassandra, or even Postgres) is sufficient: the access pattern is about as simple as access patterns get. This is explicitly not a sharding-heavy system at realistic scale — Section 6's math shows a few terabytes of data over five years and low-thousands of peak QPS on the read path, both of which a single well-provisioned database (with read replicas, per Chapter 05, to further spread the already-cache-absorbed read load) handles comfortably. Per Chapter 05's own framing, sharding earns its place only once a single node can no longer hold or serve the data — reaching for it here, before that threshold is demonstrated with numbers, is exactly the over-engineering Chapter 01 warned against.

The one scale regime where this changes: if this product were operated at the scale of a dominant global link shortener used by every major social platform simultaneously — write volume in the thousands per second sustained, not tens — Chapter 05's sharding and Chapter 06's consistent hashing become genuinely necessary, sharding the mapping table by a hash of the short code so that any given code's lookup can be routed deterministically to the shard that owns it. Name this threshold explicitly in an interview rather than either prematurely sharding or refusing to discuss it at all.

5.4 Click Analytics as an Optional, Asynchronous Extension

Recording who clicked a link, when, and from where is a common follow-up requirement, and the correct instinct is to keep it entirely off the latency-critical redirect path. The redirect handler should fire an event — short code, timestamp, coarse request metadata — onto a message queue and return the redirect to the user immediately, without waiting for that event to be durably processed. This is a direct, unmodified application of Chapter 07's event-driven patterns: a producer (the redirect handler) that does not block on a consumer (a background analytics aggregation job) it does not need to wait for. Re-deriving queue mechanics here would be redundant with Chapter 07; the only chapter-specific point worth adding is that this producer sits on an extremely hot path, so the publish call itself must be fire-and-forget (or at most a cheap, non-blocking enqueue) — any synchronous round trip to the queue broker on every single redirect would undo the latency work the rest of Section 5 just did.

6. MATHEMATICS

Applying Chapter 01's estimation method concretely, with the same style of worked arithmetic:

6.1 Assumptions

100 million monthly active users. Each user shortens 1 URL per month on average (the write path). The read:write ratio is 100:1, reflecting Section 3's requirement. Links are retained for 5 years. Each stored record (short code, long URL, owner, timestamps, flags) averages roughly 500 bytes.

6.2 Write Throughput

100,000,000 users x 1 write/month      = 100,000,000 writes/month
100,000,000 / 30 days                   ≈ 3,333,333 writes/day
3,333,333 / 86,400 seconds               ≈ 39 writes/second (average)
Peak write QPS (x3 peak factor)          ≈ 117 writes/second

6.3 Read Throughput

Read-to-write ratio 100:1  →  100 x 39   ≈ 3,900 reads/second (average)
Peak read QPS (x3 peak factor)           ≈ 11,700 reads/second

6.4 Storage Over Five Years

3,333,333 writes/day x 365 x 5 years     ≈ 6.08 billion records
6.08 billion x 500 bytes                  ≈ 3.04 TB total storage over 5 years

6.5 Short Code Length Required

With base62 encoding, the number of distinct codes available at length n is 62^n. Checking against the 6.08 billion records estimated above: 

62^5  ≈ 916 million        — not enough headroom for 6+ billion records
62^6  ≈ 56.8 billion       — comfortably covers 6.08 billion records with room to spare
62^7  ≈ 3.5 trillion       — far more than needed; only relevant if you expect to outlive this estimate by orders of magnitude

A 6-character base62 code is the right choice at this estimated scale — it is the shortest length that comfortably outlives the 5-year storage projection without needing a length migration later, which is exactly the kind of concrete, numbers-driven decision Chapter 01 asks you to make instead of guessing "6 or 7 characters, doesn't really matter."

6.6 What the Arithmetic Actually Decides

Three conclusions fall directly out of these numbers, each one a design decision made elsewhere in this chapter: first, the read path (thousands of QPS) outweighs the write path (low hundreds of QPS) by roughly two orders of magnitude, which is the entire justification for Section 5's cache-first read path and for treating cache-aside as the dominant performance lever rather than, say, optimizing the insert statement. Second, 3 TB over five years and under 150 peak writes/second are both trivially served by a single well-provisioned database with read replicas — this is the numeric backing for Section 5.3's claim that sharding is not warranted at this scale. Third, none of these numbers come close to the regime (thousands of sustained writes/second, tens of terabytes per year) where Section 5.3's sharding escalation path would actually need to be exercised — which is itself a useful, explicitly statable conclusion in an interview, not a gap in the design.

Three deliberate choices in this diagram are worth calling out. First, there is no sharding layer and no consistent-hashing ring drawn anywhere — not an oversight, but the direct consequence of Section 6's arithmetic, and the diagram would gain a sharded database tier plus a Chapter 06 routing layer only once an interviewer or a real requirement pushes the write volume into the thousands-per-second regime named in Section 5.3. Second, the cache sits only in front of the read path; the write path talks to the primary database directly, since writes are both rare (Section 6.2) and must be durable immediately, whereas reads benefit enormously from being absorbed before they reach the database at all. Third, the message queue and analytics workers form an entirely separate, decoupled subsystem from the core shorten/redirect service — if the analytics pipeline falls behind or fails outright, the shortening and redirecting functionality (the actual product) is completely unaffected, which is the architectural payoff of treating analytics as optional per Section 5.4.

9. JAVA / SPRING PERSPECTIVE

A minimal Spring Boot implementation of this design maps almost one-to-one onto Sections 5 and 8. The controller exposes exactly the two endpoints Section 3 scoped: 

@RestController
@RequestMapping("/")
class UrlShortenerController {

    private final UrlShortenerService service;

    UrlShortenerController(UrlShortenerService service) {
        this.service = service;
    }

    @PostMapping("/shorten")
    ResponseEntity<ShortenResponse> shorten(@RequestBody ShortenRequest request) {
        String code = service.createShortUrl(request.longUrl(), request.customAlias());
        return ResponseEntity.ok(new ShortenResponse("https://short.ly/" + code));
    }

    @GetMapping("/{code}")
    ResponseEntity<Void> redirect(@PathVariable String code) {
        String longUrl = service.resolve(code); // cache-aside lookup, Section 5.2
        return ResponseEntity.status(HttpStatus.FOUND)
            .location(URI.create(longUrl))
            .build();
    }
}

The base62 encoder is a small, pure, easily unit-tested utility — exactly the kind of component Section 4.2 described conceptually: 

final class Base62Encoder {

    private static final String ALPHABET =
        "0123456789abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyzABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ";

    static String encode(long id) {
        StringBuilder sb = new StringBuilder();
        if (id == 0) return String.valueOf(ALPHABET.charAt(0));
        while (id > 0) {
            sb.append(ALPHABET.charAt((int) (id % 62)));
            id /= 62;
        }
        return sb.reverse().toString();
    }
}

The service layer wires together ID allocation, persistence via Spring Data JDBC (a plain repository over the table from Section 5.3 — no ORM complexity is warranted for a single-table, no-join access pattern), and a Spring Cache-backed lookup for the hot redirect path: 

@Service
class UrlShortenerService {

    private final UrlMappingRepository repository; // Spring Data JDBC

    UrlShortenerService(UrlMappingRepository repository) {
        this.repository = repository;
    }

    String createShortUrl(String longUrl, String customAlias) {
        String code = (customAlias != null)
            ? claimCustomAlias(customAlias, longUrl)     // Section 4.5: DB-level uniqueness constraint
            : Base62Encoder.encode(repository.nextId());  // Section 4.4: Base62(auto-increment)
        repository.save(new UrlMapping(code, longUrl, Instant.now()));
        return code;
    }

    @Cacheable(value = "urlMappings", key = "#code")
    String resolve(String code) {
        // Spring Cache intercepts here on a hit — this method body only
        // runs on a cache miss, exactly matching Section 5.2's flow.
        return repository.findByShortCode(code)
            .filter(UrlMapping::isActive)
            .map(UrlMapping::longUrl)
            .orElseThrow(() -> new NotFoundException(code));
    }
}

Two details in that service deserve emphasis because they are exactly where a casual implementation drifts from the design. The @Cacheable annotation, backed by a Redis CacheManager rather than Spring's default in-memory map, is what makes this cache shared across every horizontally-scaled instance of this stateless service — an in-memory-only cache would silently reintroduce the per-instance state problem Chapter 01's Section 4.4 warned about, since a redirect cached on one instance would be invisible to a request load-balanced onto another. And claimCustomAlias must rely on a database-level unique constraint on short_code, surfacing a constraint-violation exception as a clean 409 Conflict to the client, rather than a check-then-insert race in application code — precisely the concurrency hazard Section 4.5 flagged.

10. BEST PRACTICES

  • Let the read:write ratio drive every architectural choice — cache-first reads, a lightweight write path, and no sharding until the numbers in Section 6 actually demand it.
  • Prefer base62(auto-increment ID) over hashing unless content-based deduplication is an explicit product requirement — it avoids collision-handling complexity on the hottest path entirely.
  • Enforce custom-alias uniqueness at the database level, with a real constraint, never a check-then-insert in application code alone.
  • Treat the cache as an optimization, never the system of record — a cache flush should degrade latency, not correctness, exactly as Chapter 04 insists.
  • Keep analytics fully asynchronous and decoupled from the redirect path, per Chapter 07's event-driven patterns, so an analytics outage can never take down the core redirect functionality.
  • Name your own design's scaling threshold explicitly — state the write-QPS or data-volume point at which you would introduce sharding (Chapter 05) or a pre-generated key pool, rather than either over-building for it up front or refusing to discuss it.

11. COMMON MISTAKES

  • Reaching for a sharded, heavily-distributed data store on day one "because it needs to scale," without doing Section 6's arithmetic first — the numbers almost always show a single database with replicas is sufficient, and proposing otherwise unprompted reads as a lack of judgment, not thoroughness, exactly as Chapter 01 warns generally.
  • Using a raw hash truncated to a few characters with no collision-handling plan — a candidate who says "we'll just MD5 the URL and take the first 6 characters" and stops there has not actually solved the encoding problem, since collisions at that truncation length are a certainty, not an edge case, at billions of records.
  • Forgetting the custom-alias race condition — allowing a check-then-insert in application code for user-requested aliases, which two concurrent requests for the same alias can both pass before either has written, producing a duplicate-key failure or, worse, a silently overwritten mapping.
  • Putting click-tracking on the synchronous redirect path — making the user's browser wait on an analytics write (or a blocking call to a queue broker) before issuing the redirect, directly undermining the low-latency requirement from Section 3 for a feature that was supposed to be optional.
  • Choosing 301 redirects without considering the analytics trade-off, or vice versa — picking a redirect status code without naming that it trades browser-level caching against per-click observability, discussed in Section 5.2, is a missed opportunity to show real trade-off reasoning.
  • Treating the cache and the database as needing to agree on write-through semantics for a workload this read-skewed — a mandatory write-through cache adds write-path latency and complexity to solve a staleness problem that, per Section 3's relaxed consistency requirement, barely exists in practice for a mapping that essentially never changes after creation.

12. PERFORMANCE CONSIDERATIONS

Because the read path so thoroughly dominates this workload (Section 6.6), almost every performance lever available to this design concentrates there.

  • Maximize cache hit rate on the redirect path — a reasonably long TTL is safe here because mappings essentially never change after creation (expiry aside), unlike a typical cache-invalidation problem where staleness is a real risk; a near-100% hit rate is a realistic, achievable target.
  • Push redirects to the edge — a CDN or edge-cache layer in front of the redirect endpoint removes round trips to the origin entirely for already-warm codes, which matters because the redirect sits on another product's critical path, per Section 3.
  • Keep the write path cheap, not fast — since writes are two orders of magnitude rarer than reads (Section 6), it is acceptable for a shorten request to take a few extra milliseconds for a uniqueness check or ID allocation; optimizing it further yields negligible system-wide benefit compared to the same engineering effort spent on the read path.
  • Use read replicas (Chapter 05) to absorb cache-miss traffic, not the primary, so that write-path durability on the primary is never contended by read-path cache misses during a cold-cache event (a deploy, a cache eviction storm).
  • Batch and buffer click-analytics writes rather than writing one row per click synchronously in the background consumer — Chapter 07's event-driven batching patterns apply directly, since analytics data has no latency requirement anywhere near the redirect path's.

13. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

A URL shortener's security surface is smaller than a chat platform's or an auth service's, but it has one property that makes it a uniquely attractive abuse target: it is, by design, a service that makes an arbitrary destination look short, trustworthy, and opaque.

  • Screen submitted URLs against phishing/malware blocklists at creation time, and ideally re-check periodically, since a URL that was benign when shortened can later be compromised or repurposed by its original owner.
  • Treat short codes as non-confidential identifiers, not secrets — unless a product explicitly requires unguessable codes (e.g., for private, unlisted content), don't rely on code length or randomness as an access-control mechanism; apply real authorization if a link is meant to be access-restricted.
  • Rate-limit the shorten endpoint per account/IP to prevent the write path (Section 6.2's low, predictable baseline) from being used as a mass-abuse or spam-link-generation vector, which is a very different threat profile from the read path's expected huge volume.
  • Protect against open-redirect abuse — validate that the destination is a well-formed, intended target and consider surfacing an interstitial warning page for destinations flagged as suspicious, since the redirect itself is attacker-exploitable infrastructure if left unchecked (spammers routinely abuse trusted shortener domains to bypass link-reputation filters).
  • Log and cap custom-alias claiming to prevent alias squatting at scale, where an abuser programmatically claims large numbers of short, desirable aliases.

14. INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

JUNIORQ1. An interviewer says only "design a URL shortener" and stops talking. Which one or two unstated requirements are most likely to send you down the wrong architecture if you guess wrong, and why those specifically rather than, say, exact QPS numbers?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

The read:write ratio and whether custom aliases are in scope matter more than the exact scale numbers, because they change the shape of the design, not just its sizing. Getting the ratio wrong means building a write-optimized system for a workload that's actually 100:1 read-heavy, or vice versa. Missing that custom aliases exist means discovering mid-design that your collision-free ID-based encoding (Section 4.2) needs a bolted-on uniqueness check anyway (Section 4.5), which is a design revision, not a sizing tweak. Exact QPS numbers, by contrast, just move a dial — a 10x miss on QPS rarely changes which architecture is correct at this problem's realistic scale, per Section 6.

JUNIORQ2. Why is the read-to-write ratio the single most important non-functional requirement for this system?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Because a link is created once but clicked many times, often a 100:1 ratio or higher, which means almost every architectural decision — caching strategy, which path gets optimized for latency, whether sharding is warranted — should be made in service of the read path, not the write path. Optimizing the write path at the expense of read-path simplicity would be solving the wrong problem.

MIDQ3. Walk through your back-of-the-envelope capacity estimate for a URL shortener with 100 million monthly active users.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Assuming 1 shorten per user per month: 100M/30 days ≈ 3.33M writes/day ≈ 39 writes/second average, roughly 117/second at a 3x peak factor. At a 100:1 read:write ratio, that's roughly 3,900 reads/second average and 11,700/second at peak. Over 5 years of retention at roughly 500 bytes per record, storage comes to about 6.08 billion records and roughly 3 TB total. These numbers justify a single well-provisioned database with read replicas and a caching layer, not a heavily sharded architecture.

MIDQ4. Compare base62-encoding an auto-incrementing ID versus hashing the long URL to generate a short code.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Base62(auto-increment ID) is collision-free by construction — two different URLs can never receive the same ID, so there's no retry logic needed on the write path, at the cost of needing a source of unique, ordered IDs. Hashing the long URL (e.g., truncated MD5) allows deduplication, since the same URL always hashes the same way, but truncating any hash to a short, URL-friendly length introduces real collisions at scale, requiring an explicit check-and-retry or salting strategy. For most products, base62 of an auto-increment ID is the simpler, more defensible default; hashing is justified specifically when content-based deduplication is a real requirement worth the added collision-handling complexity.

MIDQ5. How many characters does your short code need to be, and how did you arrive at that number?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Derive it from the storage estimate, not a guess: with roughly 6.08 billion records expected over 5 years, 62^5 (≈916 million) is insufficient, while 62^6 (≈56.8 billion) comfortably covers that volume with room to spare. Six characters is the shortest length that doesn't require a length migration within the planning horizon.

SENIORQ6. Why doesn't this system need database sharding at the scale you've estimated, and at what point would it?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

At roughly 117 peak writes/second, 11,700 peak reads/second (most of which the cache absorbs before they ever reach the database), and about 3 TB of data over five years, a single well-provisioned database with read replicas handles this comfortably — the access pattern is a simple point lookup by primary key, with no joins or range scans that would benefit from a more complex topology. Sharding, per Chapter 05, earns its place once a single node can no longer hold or serve the data — for this system that would mean sustained write volume in the thousands per second or data volume an order of magnitude or more higher than estimated, at which point sharding the mapping table by a hash of the short code, using consistent hashing from Chapter 06 to route lookups deterministically, becomes the right next step.

Interview Gold Point: Say the sharding threshold out loud, with a number attached, even though you're not building it — naming the specific point where your own design's assumption would break is a much stronger signal than either silently ignoring scale or defensively over-building for a scale nobody asked for.
SENIORQ7. Describe the read path for a redirect in detail, including what happens on a cache miss.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

The request first checks a Redis (or equivalent) cache keyed by short code. On a hit, which should be the vast majority of traffic given this workload's skew, the mapping is returned and the redirect issued immediately with no database round trip. On a miss, the service reads the mapping from the database, populates the cache with an appropriate TTL, and then issues the redirect — the standard cache-aside pattern. Critically, the cache is never the sole source of truth: if it's lost entirely, the system is slower (more database reads) but remains correct, because the database retains the authoritative mapping.

STAFFQ8. A single short link unexpectedly goes viral and starts receiving tens of thousands of redirect requests per second — far above your provisioned average. How does your design hold up, and what would you change?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

This is actually the scenario this design handles best, precisely because the hot key is also the easiest key to cache: once the mapping is in Redis, a single viral code generates effectively identical repeated reads that a cache (and, even better, an edge/CDN cache per Section 5.2, especially under a 301 redirect that browsers and intermediate proxies can cache themselves) absorbs almost entirely — the load does not meaningfully reach the database at all. The actual risk is a thundering-herd cache-miss moment the instant the link starts trending, before it's warm; mitigating that calls for either pre-warming known-promoted links, or a short-lived request-coalescing layer in front of the cache so concurrent misses for the same code collapse into a single database read rather than stampeding it. I'd also watch application-tier connection and thread-pool saturation under that request rate, independent of the database, since even a cache-hit path still costs CPU and network per request at tens of thousands of QPS.

STAFFQ9. How would you extend this design to support per-link click analytics without compromising redirect latency?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Treat analytics as a fully decoupled, asynchronous subsystem, not a feature of the redirect path itself. The redirect handler fires a lightweight event — short code, timestamp, coarse request metadata — onto a message queue via a non-blocking, fire-and-forget publish, then returns the redirect to the user without waiting on that publish to be acknowledged by any downstream consumer. A separate background worker pool consumes those events, batches them, and writes aggregated click counts and dimensions into a store optimized for analytical queries rather than point lookups. This is a direct application of Chapter 07's event-driven producer/consumer decoupling — the key chapter-specific caveat is that even the enqueue call itself must be cheap and non-blocking, since it now sits on what was previously a pure cache-hit path; any added synchronous latency there undermines the entire point of Section 5's read-path optimization.

Interview Gold Point: Call out explicitly that the enqueue call itself, not just the downstream consumer, must stay non-blocking — candidates who correctly decouple the consumer but still awkwardly await a synchronous publish-acknowledgment on the redirect path have only solved half the latency problem, and interviewers notice.
MIDQ10. How do you handle a user requesting a custom alias that's already taken, including under concurrent requests?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Return a clear conflict response (HTTP 409) to the user rather than silently generating a different code. To make this correct under concurrency, the uniqueness check cannot be a separate "look it up, then insert if absent" pair of operations in application code — two simultaneous requests for the same alias could both pass the lookup before either writes. Instead, rely on a database-level unique constraint on the short-code column and attempt the insert directly, catching the constraint-violation error as the signal that the alias is taken.

MIDQ11. Should a redirect use HTTP 301 or 302, and why does it matter?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A 301 (permanent redirect) lets browsers and intermediate proxies cache the redirect themselves, which reduces load on the shortener service for repeat visits — but it also means subsequent clicks from that browser may never reach the server again, making reliable per-click analytics harder to capture. A 302 (temporary redirect) guarantees every click is observed server-side, at the cost of forgoing browser-level caching. The right choice depends on whether accurate click counting or maximal caching efficiency matters more for this product — naming this trade-off explicitly is more valuable than picking one without justification.

SENIORQ12. How would you implement link expiry — by date and by click count — without adding overhead to every redirect?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Date-based expiry is cheap to check inline: store an expires_at timestamp alongside the mapping (cached together with the long URL, so the check costs nothing extra on a cache hit) and compare it against the current time before issuing the redirect. Click-count-based expiry is trickier because it requires a counter that increments on every single redirect, which is exactly the kind of write-amplification you don't want on your hottest path; the practical approach is to increment the counter asynchronously (the same event used for analytics, Section 5.4, can drive this) rather than synchronously on every redirect, accepting that the link may serve a small number of requests slightly past its exact click limit rather than paying a synchronous write cost on every single read.

SENIORQ13. Your cache layer goes down entirely. What happens to the system, and what's your mitigation?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Correctness is unaffected — the database remains the authoritative source, so every redirect still resolves correctly, just slower, since every request now falls through to a database read. The real risk is the sudden load spike on the database and read replicas as 100% of previously cache-absorbed traffic lands on them at once. Mitigation includes provisioning replicas with enough headroom to survive a full cache outage temporarily, a circuit breaker or graceful degradation path if replica load spikes dangerously, and a cache warm-up or restore strategy (rehydrating from a recent snapshot, or simply letting cache-aside repopulate naturally while replicas absorb the interim load) to shorten the exposure window.

JUNIORQ14. Why is a relational table with a single index enough for this system's data model?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Because the only access pattern that matters is a point lookup by exact short code — there are no joins, range scans, or complex queries in the hot path. A primary-key index on the short-code column serves that lookup directly and efficiently; adding more structure or a more complex data store would add operational cost without serving any access pattern this product actually has.

MIDQ15. How would you prevent someone from using your service to shorten links to known phishing or malware sites?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium

Check submitted long URLs against a reputation/blocklist service at creation time, and consider periodic re-checking afterward, since a URL that was safe when shortened can be compromised or repurposed later by its owner. Rate-limiting the shorten endpoint per account/IP also raises the cost of using the service to mass-generate malicious links. This matters more for a URL shortener than many services, because short links deliberately obscure the real destination — exactly the property an attacker wants.

STAFFQ16. How would this design change if the product required strong, immediate consistency — a shortened link must be resolvable by every server, instantly, the moment it's created, with zero staleness tolerated?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

This is a rare, tighter requirement than Section 3 assumed, and it mainly bites in a multi-region or multi-replica setup, since a single-primary database with synchronous local reads already gives you this for free. The change needed is to ensure the write is acknowledged by any replica or region before confirming success to the user, which likely means synchronous (not asynchronous) replication for this table specifically, or routing reads for very recently created codes to the primary rather than a replica until a short propagation window has passed. I'd push back and ask whether this is really required, though — a few seconds of propagation delay before a brand-new link is globally resolvable is unlikely to be noticeable to a real user, and demanding synchronous replication trades away availability and write latency broadly to solve a problem that, per Section 3's original relaxed-consistency framing, usually isn't actually there.

Interview Gold Point: Pushing back on an interviewer-stated requirement, with a specific reason tied to the user-visible impact, is itself a strong signal — it shows you treat "requirements" as negotiable inputs to be stress-tested, not commandments to be satisfied at any architectural cost.
SENIORQ17. How would you support multiple short domains (branded domains for enterprise customers) without redesigning the core system?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Add a domain identifier as part of the mapping key rather than assuming short codes are globally unique across all domains — the natural model is a composite key of (domain, short_code), since two different branded domains should be allowed to mint the same code independently (e.g., both acme.sh/promo and beta.sh/promo). The cache key and database lookup both extend to include the domain, and the redirect handler resolves the domain from the incoming request's Host header. This is a schema and routing change, not a rearchitecture — the read-path, write-path, and caching mechanics from Sections 5 and 8 are unaffected.

MIDQ18. What happens if two shorten requests race to allocate the same auto-incrementing ID — is that a real risk?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Low-Medium

No — this is precisely the advantage of base62-encoding an auto-increment ID over hashing. A proper ID source (a database sequence, or an auto-increment column with correct isolation) guarantees every allocated ID is unique by construction; the database itself serializes concurrent allocation requests so no two callers ever receive the same ID. There is no race to handle here, unlike the custom-alias path in Section 4.5, which is exactly why this approach was recommended as the default.

STAFFQ19. At what point would a single auto-increment ID source become a bottleneck, and what would you replace it with?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

A single database sequence can typically sustain far more than the roughly 117 peak writes/second this chapter's estimate requires, so at this chapter's scale it is not a bottleneck at all. It becomes one only at write volumes several orders of magnitude higher, or in a multi-region active-active write setup where a single centralized sequence would force cross-region coordination on every write. At that point, the right replacement is a pre-generated key-pool service (Section 4.4) — an offline process that mints and stores batches of unique codes ahead of time so the hot write path only has to claim one, or a distributed ID scheme that partitions ID ranges per region/node (similar in spirit to Twitter's Snowflake) so no single coordinator is on the critical path.

SENIORQ20. How would you design the system to gracefully handle a short code that was deleted or deactivated, versus one that never existed?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

These should be distinguishable in the response, not collapsed into a generic 404. A code that never existed is a plain not-found. A code that existed but was deactivated (expired, deleted by its owner, or taken down for a policy violation) should ideally return a distinct response — a "this link has expired or been removed" page rather than a bare 404 — which requires soft-deleting (an is_active flag, as in Section 5.3's schema) rather than hard-deleting rows, so the distinction remains queryable. The cache must also be invalidated or updated on deactivation, not just the database, or a recently-deactivated link could keep resolving from a stale cache entry until its TTL expires.

Interview Gold Point: Flag the cache-invalidation-on-deactivation detail unprompted — it's the kind of consistency edge case that separates a candidate who drew a cache box from one who has actually operated a cache-backed system in production.
MIDQ21. Why would you choose Spring Cache with a Redis-backed CacheManager instead of a plain in-memory HashMap for the redirect lookup?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Because the API service is horizontally scaled and stateless, per Chapter 01's Section 4.4 — an in-memory HashMap cache would be local to whichever instance populated it, invisible to every other instance behind the load balancer, producing inconsistent cache hit behavior and defeating the purpose of caching shared, frequently-accessed mappings. A Redis-backed cache is shared across every instance, so a mapping warmed by one instance's cache-miss benefits every other instance's subsequent requests for the same code.

STAFFQ22. How would you test and validate your capacity estimates once this system is actually live, rather than trusting the original back-of-the-envelope numbers forever?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

Instrument the real system with the same quantities the estimate used — actual writes/second, actual reads/second, actual cache hit rate, actual average record size — via standard application metrics (Spring Boot Actuator's metrics endpoint, as covered in Chapter 01's Java/Spring perspective) rather than trusting the launch-time assumptions indefinitely. The cache hit rate in particular is worth watching closely: if it's meaningfully lower than expected, that's a strong signal either the TTL is too short, the workload's actual read skew differs from the 100:1 assumption, or there's a hot-key/thundering-herd problem the design didn't anticipate. Treat the original estimate as a starting hypothesis to be confirmed or corrected by production data, not a one-time calculation you never revisit.

SENIORQ23. Why is it important that the short code generation strategy not leak information about volume or timing — for example, sequential codes revealing how many links have been created?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Low-Medium

A raw, unmodified sequential ID encoded directly (link #1, #2, #3…) leaks business-sensitive information — a competitor or researcher could infer total link volume and creation rate over time just by observing a handful of codes, and sequential codes are trivially enumerable, letting someone scrape every link the service has ever created. The practical mitigation is to offset, shuffle-permute, or otherwise obscure the mapping from raw ID to encoded output (e.g., applying a reversible bit-mixing or permutation step before base62-encoding) so codes don't look obviously sequential, without reintroducing the collision risk Section 4.3 associated with hashing — the mapping must still be a deterministic, unique, reversible function of the ID, just not a visibly incrementing one.

MIDQ24. What's the difference in how you'd design this system if custom aliases didn't exist at all versus being a core feature?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Low-Medium

Without custom aliases, the entire write path becomes collision-free and coordination-free by construction (Section 4.2), with no uniqueness check or constraint-violation handling needed anywhere. The moment custom aliases are supported, every write request has to account for the possibility of a user-supplied key, which reintroduces a database-level uniqueness constraint and conflict-handling path (Section 4.5) that the generated-code path otherwise avoids entirely. It's a good example of how a seemingly small feature addition changes the correctness requirements of a code path that would otherwise need none.

STAFFQ25. How would you redesign the read path if this product needed to support not just HTTP redirects but also QR-code generation and link previews (showing the destination's title/image before redirecting)?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

Keep the core redirect path exactly as designed — it must stay the fastest, simplest path, since it's the one on another product's critical path. QR-code generation is embarrassingly cacheable and stateless (the QR image is a pure function of the short URL string), so it can be generated on first request and cached indefinitely, either at the application layer or offloaded to the CDN as a static asset per code. Link previews are a different story: they require fetching and parsing metadata from the destination URL, which is slow, unreliable (the destination could be down or rate-limiting you), and must never block the redirect itself — this is best implemented as a background job triggered at shorten-time that pre-fetches and caches preview metadata asynchronously, with the redirect and preview-serving endpoints kept entirely separate so a slow or failing destination can never degrade the core redirect latency.

SENIORQ26. Your monitoring shows p50 redirect latency is excellent but p99 latency is unacceptably high. How do you investigate?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

A large gap between p50 and p99 on a cache-dominated read path usually points to the tail being driven by cache misses, not typical requests — so I'd first segment latency by cache hit versus miss explicitly, since averaging them together hides exactly this signal. If misses are the cause, likely culprits are a too-short TTL causing unnecessary churn, a thundering-herd pattern where many concurrent requests for the same cold key all miss and hit the database simultaneously (addressed by request coalescing), or a database/replica under more load than expected. I'd also check whether specific individual codes are disproportionately represented in the slow tail (a few unusually hot or unusually cold keys) versus a uniform slowdown across all traffic, since the fix differs — hot-key handling versus general capacity.

JUNIORQ27. What's the simplest possible version of this system you could build, and what would you explicitly leave out?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

A single database table (short_code, long_url), an API with the two endpoints from Section 3, base62-encoding of an auto-increment ID, and no cache, no custom aliases, no expiry, and no analytics. This version would correctly satisfy the core functional requirement for a genuinely small deployment, and naming it explicitly — then layering in caching, custom aliases, and analytics as the scale and feature requirements actually demand them — demonstrates the "start simple, add complexity in response to a named need" discipline from Chapter 01 rather than designing the fully-featured version unprompted.

STAFFQ28. How would you detect and respond to an ongoing abuse campaign where a botnet is shortening millions of spam/phishing links through your service?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

Detection is mostly a write-path anomaly problem: a sudden, sustained spike in shorten requests from a small number of accounts or a narrow IP range, well outside this chapter's modeled baseline of roughly 39 writes/second average, is the clearest early signal, so write-path metrics need alerting thresholds independent of the (much larger, noisier) read-path metrics. Response layers in order of severity: per-account and per-IP rate limiting at the gateway (not just the application), CAPTCHA or proof-of-work challenges on anomalous creation patterns, synchronous blocklist/reputation checks on destination URLs at creation time rather than only after the fact, and the ability to bulk-deactivate a cohort of recently created links sharing a suspicious pattern (same destination domain, same creation window, same account cluster) without needing to identify and deactivate them one at a time.

Interview Gold Point: Noting that write-path alerting thresholds must be set independently of read-path metrics is a detail many candidates miss — a 100x spike in writes is often statistically invisible when averaged into a dashboard dominated by a 100:1 read-heavy baseline, so the two paths need separate monitoring from the start, not a shared one split after the fact.
MIDQ29. Why might you choose Spring Data JDBC over full JPA/Hibernate for this particular service?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Low-Medium

Because the data model (Section 5.3) is a single table with no relationships, no joins, and no need for an object-relational mapping layer's lazy-loading, entity-graph, or cascading features — all of which exist to manage complexity that simply isn't present here. Spring Data JDBC gives a thinner, more predictable mapping between a row and a Java object for exactly this kind of simple, single-aggregate access pattern, avoiding the overhead and occasional surprising behavior (the N+1 query problem, proxy objects) that full JPA is built to handle for genuinely relational models this system doesn't have.

SENIORQ30. How would you support "edit the destination of an existing short link" as a feature, and what does it break?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium

Mechanically it's a simple update to the long_url column plus an explicit cache invalidation (or a short, deliberately-bounded TTL instead of a long one) for that code, since otherwise clients would keep being redirected to the stale destination until the old cache entry expires. The bigger issue it introduces is semantic, not technical: it breaks the assumption, implicit everywhere else in this chapter, that a mapping is immutable once created — any analytics, audit logging, or caching-TTL reasoning built on "this record essentially never changes" needs to be revisited, and I'd want product confirmation that mutable destinations are really wanted, since they also make a previously-shared link's behavior unpredictable to whoever shared it originally.

STAFFQ31. If you had to deploy this system across multiple geographic regions for latency reasons, what would need to change?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

The read path localizes easily and is the one worth prioritizing: deploy read replicas (Chapter 05) and cache nodes per region, so the dominant 100:1 traffic is served locally everywhere, which is most of the latency win with the least complexity. The write path is the harder question — a single-region primary for writes is simplest and matches this chapter's relaxed-consistency requirement (Section 3) reasonably well, accepting that cross-region writes incur extra latency; true multi-region active-active writes would require either a globally coordinated ID-allocation scheme (partitioned ID ranges per region, avoiding a single sequence as a cross-region bottleneck, echoing Q19) or accepting a different consistency model for conflict resolution, which is a materially bigger undertaking I would not propose without the interviewer or requirement explicitly asking for active-active writes specifically.

MIDQ32. What's a reasonable cache TTL for the redirect lookup, and what does getting it wrong in either direction cost you?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Because mappings are essentially immutable after creation (barring expiry or the edit feature from Q30), a relatively long TTL — hours, not seconds — is safe and maximizes hit rate without meaningful staleness risk. Too short a TTL causes unnecessary cache churn and more database load than this read-skewed workload needs to generate; too long a TTL mainly matters once expiry or mutable destinations are introduced, where a stale cached entry could keep serving a deactivated or changed link past when it should stop. The TTL choice should track whichever mutability feature (expiry, editable destinations) the product actually supports, not be picked arbitrarily.

SENIORQ33. How would you prevent a malicious actor from enumerating all short codes to discover private or unlisted links?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

If codes are meant to be non-guessable for any links treated as private, don't rely on a small 6-character base62 space plus sequential or lightly-obscured generation (Q23) as the actual access control — a determined actor can brute-force or scrape a space that size over time. The correct fix is to treat unlisted-ness as a real authorization concern: require authentication to resolve certain links, or generate a materially longer, cryptographically random code specifically for links flagged private, separate from the default short, convenient code used for public links. Rate-limiting the redirect endpoint itself also raises the cost of brute-force enumeration regardless.

STAFFQ34. Design the schema and flow changes needed to support link ownership transfer between user accounts.

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Low at staff level

Mechanically straightforward given Section 5.3's schema — update the owner_id column on the mapping row, likely behind an explicit transfer-confirmation flow (both parties consenting, or an admin action) rather than a bare field update, and invalidate any owner-scoped cache entries (e.g., "list of links owned by user X") if such a view exists, since the redirect-path cache keyed purely by short code is unaffected by ownership changes. The more interesting design question is audit history: if analytics or billing ever attribute clicks to an owner, you need to decide whether historical clicks before the transfer stay attributed to the original owner or move with the link — a product decision, not a purely technical one, that should be made explicit rather than left to whatever the simplest implementation happens to do.

MIDQ35. Why is it acceptable for this system to use eventual consistency on writes but not on reads of the redirect mapping itself?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

These are different things being asked about: "eventual consistency on writes" here means a newly created link might take a brief moment to propagate to every replica/region before it's globally resolvable, which is unnoticeable to the user who just created it and hasn't shared it yet. "Consistency on reads of the mapping" means once a code is resolvable, it must always return the correct long URL, not an occasionally wrong one — a cache serving a stale-but-still-correct value from before an update is fine, but a cache or replica serving an outright incorrect mapping (pointing code A to URL B's destination) would be a serious correctness bug, not an acceptable trade-off, regardless of this chapter's relaxed consistency stance.

SENIORQ36. How would you add support for A/B testing — routing a percentage of clicks on one short code to two different destination URLs?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Low-Medium

Extend the mapping to support multiple weighted destinations per code rather than a single long_url, with the redirect handler choosing a destination probabilistically (or deterministically per-user, via a stable hash of a user/session identifier, if consistent assignment across visits matters for the experiment) before issuing the redirect. This needs to stay cheap — the weighted selection is a pure in-memory computation on an already-cached small set of destinations, so it doesn't reintroduce a database round trip on the hot path. It also changes the analytics requirement from Section 5.4 meaningfully: click events now need to record which variant was served, not just that a click happened, since that's the entire point of the feature.

STAFFQ37. Your team proposes storing the entire mapping table in a distributed in-memory data grid instead of a traditional database plus cache. How do you evaluate that proposal?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

I'd push back by returning to Section 6's numbers: at roughly 3 TB over five years and a few thousand peak reads/second, this workload does not need an all-in-memory architecture to hit its latency targets — a conventional database with read replicas plus a conventional cache (Chapter 04) already gets there, and an all-in-memory data grid trades a real increase in cost and operational complexity (durability design, cluster management, memory costs at that data volume) for latency headroom this system doesn't need. I'd ask what specific, measured latency problem the current cache-plus-database design fails to solve before accepting the added complexity — this is the same "don't add a bigger hammer than the numbers justify" judgment Chapter 01 and Section 11 of this chapter both warn about, just proposed from the infrastructure side instead of the database-sharding side.

Interview Gold Point: Responding to a "let's use fancier infrastructure" proposal by asking for the specific measured problem it solves — rather than either agreeing reflexively or rejecting it reflexively — is exactly the trade-off-reasoning behavior Chapter 01 identified as what interviewers are actually scoring.
MIDQ38. How would you version or migrate the short-code format if you later needed to move from 6-character to 7-character codes?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Low-Medium

Existing 6-character codes keep working unchanged — nothing about the redirect lookup cares about code length, since it's a simple string key lookup, not a fixed-width field. New codes are simply minted at 7 characters once the ID space allocated to 6-character codes is exhausted (or proactively, once Section 6.5's projection shows the 6-character space nearing capacity). The only things to double check are any client-side or documentation assumptions that hard-coded a fixed 6-character length, and ensuring the database column and any validation regex were defined with enough headroom (e.g., VARCHAR(8) as in Section 5.3) rather than tightly sized to exactly 6 characters.

SENIORQ39. What would you monitor and alert on for this system in production, beyond basic uptime?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Cache hit rate on the redirect path, since a sustained drop is the earliest signal of either a configuration problem or a shift in traffic pattern the design didn't anticipate; p50/p95/p99 redirect latency, segmented by cache hit versus miss as in Q26; write-path request rate and error rate, watched for the kind of abuse spike described in Q28; database replica lag, since stale replica reads would be a real correctness issue if read-path traffic is ever routed to replicas during a cache miss; and queue depth/consumer lag for the analytics pipeline, so a backlog there is caught before it either loses events or, worse, someone is tempted to make the redirect path wait on it.

STAFFQ40. How would you approach a request to guarantee "no two users ever see each other's custom alias conflict error leak information about link existence" — i.e., a privacy concern around the uniqueness check itself?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Low at staff level

This is a subtle information-disclosure question: a bare "alias already taken" error reveals that some specific alias exists, which could leak information if aliases are allowed to encode sensitive strings. If that's a genuine concern, the uniqueness-conflict response can be made intentionally generic ("alias unavailable," without distinguishing "taken" from "disallowed by policy"), and alias uniqueness checks should never be exposed as a separate, pre-submission "is this available" lookup endpoint, since that would turn the privacy concern into an even more direct enumeration oracle. I'd confirm with product/security stakeholders whether this threat model is actually in scope before adding this friction, since for most public-link use cases it's unnecessary complexity — but it's the right instinct to flag rather than assume away.

MIDQ41. Why does the write path in this design never need to touch the cache directly?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Because this design uses cache-aside (Chapter 04), not write-through — the cache is populated lazily, on the first read that misses it, rather than being proactively filled at creation time. This keeps the write path simple and fast (Section 6.6 already shows it doesn't need optimizing further) and avoids paying a cache-write cost for links that may never be clicked before their TTL would have expired anyway. The trade-off is that a link's very first read is always a cache miss, which is an acceptable, barely-noticeable cost given this workload's overall skew.

SENIORQ42. How would you handle the scenario where the message queue for click analytics (Section 5.4) is completely unavailable?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

The redirect path must degrade gracefully, not fail — the publish call should be wrapped so that a queue-unavailable error is caught and logged (and surfaced to monitoring, per Q39), never allowed to propagate up and block or fail the actual redirect response. This means accepting some click-analytics data loss during a queue outage as the correct trade-off, since the core product function (redirecting users) must never depend on the health of an explicitly optional subsystem, exactly as Section 5.4 scoped it.

STAFFQ43. If an interviewer told you to assume this needs to support 50,000 sustained writes per second instead of the ~117 you estimated, how would your design change?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

This crosses the threshold named in Section 5.3 and Q6, so several decisions flip. ID allocation moves from a single database sequence to the pre-generated key-pool service from Section 4.4, since even a well-tuned single sequence becomes a coordination bottleneck at that volume. The mapping table gets sharded (Chapter 05) by a hash of the short code, with Chapter 06's consistent hashing routing both writes and reads to the correct shard, so no single node is responsible for the full write volume. I'd also revisit the custom-alias uniqueness check, since a database-level constraint across a sharded table needs the shard key to include enough of the alias (or a separate, differently-sharded uniqueness-reservation table) to enforce global uniqueness without a cross-shard coordination step on every custom-alias write.

Interview Gold Point: Flagging the custom-alias uniqueness check as the one piece that doesn't shard cleanly is the detail that separates candidates who've internalized Section 4.5's point from those who only memorized "shard by hash of the key" as a generic answer — a generated code shards trivially; a user-chosen one needs global uniqueness, which sharding actively complicates.
MIDQ44. What's the risk of using UUIDs directly as short codes instead of base62-encoded integers?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Low-Medium

A UUID is globally unique and requires no coordinated ID source, which sounds appealing, but it's far longer than a base62 code needs to be (36 characters versus 6) once rendered as a string, defeating the entire purpose of a "short" URL. It also carries no natural ordering, which isn't strictly required here but does remove an option (ordered IDs are occasionally useful for debugging or range-based operational queries) for no real benefit. Base62-encoding a compact integer achieves the same uniqueness guarantee in far fewer characters, which is the actual design goal.

SENIORQ45. How would you approach deprecating or sunsetting this service gracefully if the company decided to shut it down, given that external content (tweets, emails, printed material) may still reference short links indefinitely?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Low

Because short links are frequently embedded in content the operator doesn't control and can't update (old tweets, printed material, archived emails, per Section 2's history), an abrupt shutdown breaks links indefinitely into the future in a way a typical service deprecation doesn't. The responsible approach is a long advance-notice window, an export/migration path letting customers redirect their own domains elsewhere, and ideally keeping the redirect-serving function alive in a minimal, low-cost read-only mode indefinitely (or for a long committed period) even after new link creation is disabled — since the read path's infrastructure cost, per this chapter's own numbers, is modest and mostly cache-absorbed, keeping it alive far outlasts the cost of the alternative: broken links scattered across the web with no recourse for whoever posted them.

16. SUMMARY

This chapter's content was never really about URL shortening — it was about proving you can run the full design process this track has been building toward, start to finish, on a problem small enough that nothing about its difficulty is hiding the process itself. You scoped functional and non-functional requirements explicitly (Section 3), ran real capacity arithmetic before choosing any technology (Section 6), weighed three genuinely different encoding strategies against each other and picked one with a stated reason (Section 4), and justified a deliberately simple data model and the absence of sharding by pointing at numbers, not instinct (Sections 5 and 6).

Just as important is what this chapter did not re-teach. The cache-aside mechanics in Section 5.2 are Chapter 04's, applied rather than rederived. The sharding threshold discussion in Section 5.3 is Chapter 05's framework, invoked by name rather than reproduced. The analytics extension in Section 5.4 leans entirely on Chapter 07's event-driven patterns. That is the actual skill this chapter was built to exercise: synthesis. A URL shortener is "easy" only in the sense that none of its individual pieces are novel — what makes it a legitimate system design exercise, and the reason it remains the field's canonical first case study, is assembling those pieces correctly, in the right order, under real numbers.

Carry two things forward into the next two case studies in this volume. First, the sequence itself — scope, estimate, choose a core mechanism and justify it, design the data model to match the actual access pattern, then build the read and write paths around the dominant load direction — is reusable on any system design problem, not specific to short links. Second, the specific discipline of naming your own design's weak point and its scaling threshold, demonstrated repeatedly throughout this chapter's interview questions, is worth more in both an interview and a real design review than a design that looks complete but never admits where it would actually break.
