CHAPTER 11 — DESIGNING A CHAT & MESSAGING SYSTEM

Volume 5 — Case Studies · Chapter 11 of 12

Chapter 10 walked you through a URL shortener — a case study chosen precisely because it is almost entirely stateless: a client asks a question, a server answers it, and the connection is gone a few milliseconds later. This chapter throws that assumption away. A chat and messaging system — the WhatsApp/Slack/iMessage shape of product — is built around a client that stays connected for hours, a server that must push data to that client without being asked, and a message that must reach a specific person regardless of which of your thousands of stateless app servers they happen to be attached to right now. It is also, alongside the URL shortener, one of the two most commonly asked system-design interview questions in the industry, precisely because it forces a candidate to synthesize load balancing (Chapter 03), sharding (Chapter 05), and message queues (Chapter 07) into one coherent design rather than reciting any of them in isolation.

1. MOTIVATION

Every system you have designed so far in this track has shared one quiet assumption: the client asks, the server answers, and the interaction is over. A load balancer (Chapter 03) routes a request to any healthy backend because no backend needs to "remember" anything about the client between requests. A cache (Chapter 04) and a sharded database (Chapter 05) both work because each request is self-contained — it carries everything needed to find and return the right answer. Chat breaks this assumption at the root. A chat system's defining property is that the server must be able to reach the client without the client asking first — a message arrives, and it has to be pushed to a phone or browser that is just sitting there, not polling in a tight loop burning battery and bandwidth. That one requirement — unsolicited, low-latency server-to-client delivery — is the seed every other design decision in this chapter grows out of.

The second reason this deserves full case-study treatment is how much of the track it actually exercises. Chapter 03's load balancing has to be rethought the moment connections become long-lived instead of one-shot (Section 4.1). Chapter 05's sharding strategy has to pick a partition key that matches how chat data is actually read (Section 4.3). Chapter 07's message-queue patterns — at-least-once delivery, idempotent consumers, dead-letter handling — turn out to be exactly the right abstraction for delivering a message to a user who is currently offline (Section 4.4 and Section 5). None of these earlier chapters is being re-taught here; they are being applied, under new constraints, to a single concrete product.

Third, and most practically: "design WhatsApp," "design Slack," and "design a messenger app" are among the most frequently asked system-design interview prompts at every level from new-grad to staff, because the problem scales cleanly with seniority — a junior candidate can reasonably describe sending and storing a message, while a staff candidate is expected to reason about connection-routing at hundreds of millions of concurrent users, fan-out cost for a 10,000-person broadcast channel, and the trade-offs of end-to-end encryption on searchability and spam detection. This chapter is written to make you competent across that entire range.

2. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

2.1 Centralized IM: IRC and the Walled Gardens (1988–2003)

Internet Relay Chat (IRC, 1988) established the core vocabulary still used today — channels, nicknames, a server that relays a message from one connected client to every other client subscribed to the same channel — but it was built for an era when "online" meant a single, long-lived terminal session, not a phone that sleeps, loses signal, and switches networks constantly. Through the 1990s, proprietary walled gardens (ICQ, AOL Instant Messenger, MSN Messenger) added the first mainstream "buddy list" and presence indicator (online/away/offline), but each ran its own incompatible protocol and server fleet — a user on AIM could not message a user on ICQ, a limitation that shaped the next era's central ambition.

2.2 Open Standards: XMPP / Jabber (1999–2010s)

XMPP (originally Jabber, 1999) tried to do for instant messaging what SMTP had done for email: define an open, federated, XML-based protocol so that independently-run servers could exchange messages between users on different providers, the same way two different email providers already could. XMPP introduced the architecture most chat systems still conceptually borrow — a client holds one persistent connection to its home server, and messages between users on different servers are routed server-to-server. Early Google Talk and large parts of the enterprise messaging world ran on XMPP, and its core lesson still matters directly to this chapter: a persistent, stateful connection per client, not a request/response call per message, is the right primitive for this problem — XMPP got that right two decades before "add a WebSocket" became the default answer.

2.3 Mobile-Native Messaging at Internet Scale: BlackBerry Messenger and WhatsApp (2005–2014)

BlackBerry Messenger (2005) was the first messaging product built around a device that was mobile and intermittently connected by default, not a desktop client assumed to be always-on — it had to solve store-and-forward delivery for a device that regularly dropped off a cellular network for minutes at a time, a problem XMPP's always-connected assumption had not centered. WhatsApp (founded 2009) took that same mobile-first instinct and scaled it further than almost any system before it, eventually serving over a billion simultaneously-connected users with a famously small engineering team — a result achieved specifically by keeping the server side stateless and horizontally scalable per connection (Erlang's lightweight-process model let one machine hold millions of idle connections cheaply) rather than by any single clever algorithm. WhatsApp's 2016 rollout of end-to-end encryption by default, built on the Signal Protocol, also permanently changed what a production chat system is expected to guarantee, and directly constrains several decisions in Section 13.

2.4 Team & Workspace Chat and the WebSocket-Native Era (2013–present)

Slack (launched 2013) popularized a different shape of the same underlying problem: not one-to-one personal messaging, but persistent, named group channels inside an organization, with message history that is expected to be fully searchable and durable rather than ephemeral. Slack's architecture is explicitly built on the modern, standardized WebSocket protocol (RFC 6455, finalized 2011) rather than on a bespoke always-on TCP protocol the way XMPP and BBM were, which is also why WebSockets — alongside long-polling and Server-Sent Events as fallbacks — are this chapter's default transport (Section 4.1). The current era is defined by this WebSocket-native approach combined with managed real-time infrastructure (Pusher, Ably, Firebase Realtime Database/Firestore) that lets smaller teams rent the connection-routing problem (Section 4.2) instead of building it, while the largest platforms (WhatsApp, Messenger, Slack, Discord) continue to run custom infrastructure purpose-built for their specific scale and fan-out shape.

Why this history matters: every era solved the same underlying problem — how does the server reach a client it did not just hear from — with the tools available at the time (federation, store-and-forward, lightweight connection handling, WebSockets). None of these approaches became obsolete; a modern production system usually combines several of them (a WebSocket for the live connection, a store-and-forward queue for offline delivery, and a federation-like separation of concerns between a connection layer and a message layer), which is exactly the architecture Sections 5 and 8 build.

3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

Design a system that lets users exchange messages in real time, one-to-one and in groups, delivering each message reliably and in order to every recipient regardless of which of many stateless servers they are connected to or whether they are online right now, while supporting presence, read receipts, and media attachments, at a scale of hundreds of millions of concurrently connected users.

Clarify requirements the way an interview actually expects before designing anything:

  • Functional: 1:1 messaging; group messaging (a conversation with up to some bounded number of members, commonly capped well below social-feed follower counts — hundreds, not millions); message delivery to online recipients in near-real time; store-and-forward delivery to offline recipients; conversation/message history, retrievable on login or on a new device; media attachments (images, video, files); read receipts and typing indicators as explicit stretch goals, not core requirements.
  • Non-functional: low end-to-end latency for online-to-online delivery (low hundreds of milliseconds, not seconds); message ordering preserved within a single conversation; high availability (a chat outage is highly visible and immediately noticed by users); durability — a message that was accepted by the system must never simply vanish; horizontal scalability of connection count independent of message volume.
  • Explicitly out of scope for the core design (worth saying out loud in an interview): voice/video calling signaling, full end-to-end encryption key-exchange protocol details, and spam/abuse ML — each is a legitimate deep-dive the interviewer can steer you into, but none is the default scope.

Unpacked, the hard sub-problems are: (1) connection routing — a client can be connected to any of many stateless app servers, so "deliver this message to user X" requires knowing, and reaching, whichever server X is actually attached to right now; (2) data modeling — messages must be stored so that "give me the last 50 messages in this conversation, in order" is cheap, at a write volume of billions of messages per day for a large product; (3) offline delivery — a recipient who is not connected must still receive the message the moment they come back online, without the sender's request blocking or failing; (4) group fan-out — a message to a 200-person group must reach all 200 members, and the strategy for doing that cheaply is a direct, smaller-scale cousin of the social-feed fan-out problem Chapter 12 covers at a much larger scale; (5) presence — "online now" / "last seen 5 minutes ago" at a scale where naively writing a database row on every heartbeat from every connected user is its own capacity problem.

4. THEORY

4.1 Why Long-Lived Connections, and Which Transport

The foundational theoretical shift from everything earlier in this track: a chat client cannot simply poll "do I have new messages?" on a fixed interval and call it real time, because any polling interval short enough to feel instant (sub-second) is also short enough to be a denial-of-service attack against your own infrastructure at scale, and any interval long enough to be affordable feels broken to the user. The system needs the server to be able to push data to the client the moment it exists. Three transports solve this, with real trade-offs:

TransportMechanismTrade-off
Long pollingClient sends a request; server holds it open until a message exists or a timeout elapses, then responds, and the client immediately re-requestsWorks through any HTTP infrastructure with zero special support; wastes a connection/thread per idle client and adds a request-overhead tax on every cycle
Server-Sent Events (SSE)A single long-lived HTTP response the server keeps writing to; one-directional, server-to-client onlySimple, rides on plain HTTP/CDN/proxy infrastructure; the client still needs an ordinary HTTP request to send its own messages, so it is a half-duplex solution to a naturally full-duplex problem
WebSocketA single TCP connection upgraded from HTTP into a persistent, full-duplex channel — either side can push data at any timeThe natural fit for chat (true bidirectional push with low per-message overhead); costs more operationally — connection-aware load balancing, more server-side state per connection, and firewalls/proxies that handle long-lived non-HTTP-looking connections less gracefully than plain HTTP

Production chat systems default to WebSockets (or a comparable persistent binary protocol) precisely because the interaction is genuinely bidirectional — a client both sends messages and receives pushed ones over the same session — and the per-message overhead savings compound enormously once a user sends and receives thousands of small messages over a long-lived session. Long polling remains relevant as a fallback for networks or clients that cannot sustain a WebSocket (legacy proxies, restrictive corporate firewalls).

4.2 The Connection-Routing Problem

Here is the idea that most distinguishes this chapter from everything built on top of Chapter 03's load balancer: a load balancer picks any healthy backend for a stateless HTTP request, because any backend can serve it. A chat gateway server, once it accepts a client's WebSocket connection, becomes the one specific machine that holds that client's live connection — no other server in the fleet can push to that client directly. This means "deliver a message to user X" is no longer "route a request to any healthy server"; it is "find out which specific gateway server, out of potentially thousands, currently holds user X's connection, and get the message to that exact machine." This is solved with a connection-routing / presence registry — typically a fast key-value store (Redis is the common default) mapping user_id → gateway_server_id (and, for multi-device support, user_id → {device_id: gateway_server_id}), updated on every connect and disconnect. When message service A needs to deliver to a user connected to gateway server B, it looks up the mapping, then forwards the message to server B specifically — usually over an internal pub/sub channel (Redis Pub/Sub or Kafka; Section 9 and Chapter 07) rather than a direct service-to-service call, so the message service does not need to track every gateway server's network address itself.

Chapter 03's load balancing still matters here, just at a different layer: it distributes new incoming connections across the gateway fleet (so no one gateway server is overloaded with connections), and — critically — it must support sticky-enough routing for the life of a connection: once a WebSocket handshake completes against a specific gateway instance, that TCP connection has to keep talking to that exact instance for its entire duration; a load balancer cannot transparently move a live WebSocket to a different backend mid-session the way it can route independent HTTP requests freely. This is why chat gateway layers are typically "stateless" only in the sense that any instance can accept a *new* connection — once a connection exists, that specific instance holds real, non-negotiable state (the live socket) that the rest of the system must route around via the presence registry, not through the load balancer.

4.3 Data Model: Partitioning by Conversation

The single highest-leverage schema decision is choosing conversation_id as the partition key for message storage, not user_id and not a global auto-increment ID. Almost every read a chat application performs is "give me the most recent messages in this conversation" — never "give me every message ever sent by this user across all their conversations." Partitioning by conversation_id means that query touches exactly one shard (Chapter 05's sharding theory applied directly: pick the partition key that matches your actual read pattern), while a user_id-based partition would scatter a single conversation's messages across shards for every participant.

Within a conversation's partition, messages need a composite key of (conversation_id, sequence_or_timestamp) so they come back already sorted for the common "most recent N messages" query. This is exactly the shape a wide-column / NoSQL store (Cassandra, DynamoDB, Bigtable) is built for: a partition key plus a clustering/sort key that keeps rows for the same partition physically co-located and pre-sorted, avoiding an expensive sort at read time. A relational database can do this too (with a composite index), but the wide-column model's native support for "unbounded rows per partition, pre-sorted by a clustering key, cheap sequential writes" is why it is the common default at scale, the same way Chapter 05 discussed when a specialized store beats a general-purpose relational one for a specific access pattern.

Sequence numbers versus timestamps for ordering is a real and commonly-tested trade-off. A client-generated or server-generated wall-clock timestamp is simple but unreliable as a true ordering key — clock skew between servers (or between a client's clock and the server's) can make two messages sent moments apart compare in the wrong order, and two messages can legitimately share the same millisecond under load. A monotonically increasing sequence number scoped to the conversation (assigned by a single authoritative point — commonly the server handling the write, using an atomic increment or a coordination primitive) guarantees a strict, gap-free total order within that conversation regardless of clock behavior, at the cost of needing a serialization point for sequence assignment per conversation, which is rarely a bottleneck since no single conversation (even a large group) approaches the throughput of the system as a whole.

4.4 Offline Delivery and Fan-Out Strategy

When the recipient is offline, the system cannot push anything — there is no live connection to push to. The correct model, directly inherited from Chapter 07's messaging patterns, is store-and-forward via a per-user inbox: the message is durably written (both to the conversation's message history and to a lightweight per-recipient "pending delivery" queue or marker), and when that user's client reconnects, it fetches everything it missed since its last known sequence number/cursor — the same "catch up from a cursor" pattern Chapter 07 covers for consumer offset tracking. For a currently-offline mobile client, a push notification (via APNs/FCM) is additionally dispatched so the user is alerted without needing an open connection at all; the notification payload is typically a lightweight "you have a new message" signal, with the client fetching the actual content over its own authenticated channel once it wakes up or reopens the app, rather than trusting message content to an external push provider.

Group chat forces an explicit choice between two fan-out strategies, structurally the same decision Chapter 12 will revisit for social feeds, but with dramatically different numbers because chat groups are small (the common cap is in the hundreds of members) compared to a social feed's potentially millions of followers:

StrategyHow It WorksTrade-off at Chat's Scale
Fan-out-on-write (push model)The moment a message is sent, it is immediately written/delivered to every member's inbox/connectionAt chat-group sizes (tens to low hundreds of members), the write amplification is small and entirely affordable — this is why it is the overwhelmingly common default for chat, unlike for a social feed with millions of followers
Fan-out-on-read (pull model)The message is written once to the conversation; each member's client queries the conversation for new messages when it checks inAvoids write amplification entirely, but adds read-time cost and complicates "push me new messages the instant they arrive" — directly undermining the real-time requirement, so it is rarely used alone for chat

Because group sizes in chat are orders of magnitude smaller than a celebrity social-media account's follower count, the "hot key" problem that forces large platforms toward fan-out-on-read for their biggest producers (covered in depth in Chapter 12) essentially does not arise in chat — fan-out-on-write to a few hundred members is cheap enough that nearly every production chat system uses it uniformly, with no special-cased hybrid needed the way a feed system requires one.

4.5 Presence and Why "Last Seen" Is Eventually Consistent

Presence status ("online," "away," "last seen 3 minutes ago") looks like a trivial boolean flag but is, in reality, one of the better illustrations of eventual consistency in this entire track. A client sends a periodic heartbeat over its open connection; the gateway (or a dedicated presence service) updates a TTL-based record (commonly in Redis, keyed by user ID, with a short expiry — tens of seconds) each time a heartbeat arrives. If no heartbeat arrives before the TTL expires, the user is considered offline — not because anyone was explicitly told the user disconnected (a dropped mobile connection due to a tunnel or a dead battery often provides no clean disconnect signal at all), but because the record silently expired. This means presence is never a real-time, strongly-consistent fact — it is always a best-effort inference with a built-in staleness window equal to the heartbeat interval and TTL, and every chat product's "last seen X minutes ago" is really "X minutes since the last successfully received heartbeat," not a guarantee the user was actually active that recently. Writing a fresh database row on every heartbeat from every connected user at scale would itself be a punishing write-amplification problem, which is exactly why a TTL-expiry model in a fast in-memory store, not a durable write-heavy table, is the standard approach.

5. INTERNAL WORKING

Trace one 1:1 message and one group message end to end, with both the online and offline recipient cases made explicit.

5.1 One-to-One Message, Recipient Online

  1. Client A sends a message over its open WebSocket connection to Gateway Server 1, addressed to User B within Conversation C.
  2. Gateway Server 1 forwards the message to the Message Service, which assigns the next sequence number for Conversation C and writes the message durably to the conversation's partition (Section 4.3).
  3. The Message Service looks up User B's current connection location in the presence/connection registry (Section 4.2). The lookup returns "User B is connected to Gateway Server 7."
  4. The Message Service publishes the message onto an internal channel scoped to Gateway Server 7 (Redis Pub/Sub channel or a Kafka topic/partition keyed by gateway ID — Section 9 shows both).
  5. Gateway Server 7, subscribed to its own channel, receives the message and pushes it down User B's live WebSocket connection — User B sees the message arrive in real time.
  6. Independently, the Message Service writes a delivery acknowledgment path: once User B's client confirms receipt (and later, read status), those events flow back the same way in reverse, updating the message's status (sent → delivered → read) that Client A's UI reflects.

5.2 One-to-One Message, Recipient Offline

  1. Steps 1–2 are identical — the message is still durably written and sequenced first, regardless of recipient status. This ordering matters: durability never depends on whether delivery succeeds immediately.
  2. The connection-registry lookup for User B returns no active gateway — User B is offline.
  3. The Message Service records a pending-delivery marker for User B (conceptually, Chapter 07's per-consumer queue/offset pattern applied to a single human recipient instead of a service) and dispatches a push notification via APNs/FCM so User B's device surfaces an alert even with the app closed.
  4. When User B's client later reconnects, it authenticates and sends its last-known sequence number (or a per-conversation cursor) to the Message Service, which returns everything the client missed, exactly as a lagging Kafka consumer group catches up from its last committed offset.

5.3 Group Message Fan-Out

  1. Client sends a message to Conversation G (a 150-member group). The Message Service sequences and persists it once, against the group conversation's partition — one write, regardless of group size.
  2. The Message Service fetches Conversation G's member list and, per Section 4.4's fan-out-on-write default, immediately issues a delivery attempt to each of the 150 members: an online-member lookup against the connection registry for each, pushed through their respective gateway servers, and a pending-delivery marker plus push notification for any member currently offline.
  3. This fan-out is done asynchronously relative to the sender — the sender's own UI shows the message as "sent" the moment the single durable write in step 1 completes, not after all 150 deliveries finish, which would otherwise make the sender's perceived latency scale with group size.

Edge cases a naive design misses, each directly inherited from a pattern covered earlier in this track:

  • A user has the same account open on two devices simultaneously (phone and laptop) — the connection registry must map to a set of gateway connections per user, not a single one, and a sent message must be delivered to, and marked read consistently across, all of that user's active sessions.
  • A gateway server crashes while holding thousands of live connections — every one of those connections drops, and every affected client must detect the drop and reconnect (typically to a different gateway instance via the load balancer), re-registering itself in the connection registry and re-fetching anything it missed via its last-known sequence number, the same recovery path as any offline-reconnect case.
  • Two messages are sent to the same conversation at nearly the same instant from different senders — the single-point sequence assignment described in Section 4.3 is exactly what prevents this from producing an ambiguous or racing order.
  • A group member is removed mid-conversation — fan-out must consult a current, not cached-forever, membership list, or a removed member can keep receiving messages they should no longer see.

6. MATHEMATICS

6.1 Connection Capacity per Server (the C10K / C10M Problem)

A single gateway server's ceiling is not CPU or raw bandwidth under normal chat traffic — it is how many idle, mostly-silent connections it can hold open simultaneously, since each connection consumes file descriptors and some baseline memory even when no messages are flowing. The oft-cited "C10K problem" (handling 10,000 concurrent connections on one machine) was solved by event-driven, non-blocking I/O models over a decade ago; modern async runtimes (Netty on the JVM, Erlang's BEAM VM, Node's event loop) comfortably push a single well-tuned machine toward the "C10M" range — millions of lightly-active concurrent connections — which is precisely why a chat gateway layer scales primarily by adding more machines to spread connection count, not by making any one machine handle more raw message throughput. 

required_gateway_servers ≈ target_concurrent_connections / max_connections_per_server

Example: 50,000,000 concurrently connected users, 500,000 sustainable
connections per well-tuned gateway instance
  → 50,000,000 / 500,000 = 100 gateway server instances (before redundancy headroom)

6.2 Fan-Out Cost for Group Messages

fan_out_write_cost(group) = member_count × per_delivery_cost

Example: a 150-member group, per-delivery cost ≈ 1 lookup + 1 push ≈ small, fixed
  → one message send triggers ~150 delivery operations — trivial at this scale

Compare to a social-feed fan-out (Chapter 12) for a producer with 5,000,000
followers: 5,000,000 × per_delivery_cost — many orders of magnitude larger,
which is exactly why chat's small, bounded group sizes make fan-out-on-write
the uncontested default (Section 4.4), where a feed system needs a hybrid.

6.3 Message Storage Growth

daily_storage_growth ≈ messages_per_day × avg_message_size_bytes

Example: 100,000,000 daily active users, averaging 40 messages sent per
user per day, at ~200 bytes per message row (text + metadata, excluding
media which is stored separately in object storage, not inline):

  100,000,000 × 40 × 200 bytes = 800,000,000,000 bytes ≈ 745 GB / day

This is why message bodies are typically stored separately from media
attachments (object storage, referenced by URL/ID from the message row,
never embedded) — media would otherwise dominate this calculation by
several orders of magnitude and dictate a completely different storage
tier and retention/compression strategy than the message-metadata table.

6.4 Little's Law Applied to the Offline-Delivery Queue

Section 4.4's per-user pending-delivery mechanism is still a queue, so Chapter 07's L = λ × W applies directly: if messages destined for currently-offline users arrive faster (λ) than the push-notification and catch-up pipeline can clear its backlog (driving up W, time spent pending), the number of undelivered pending messages (L) grows without bound — the same unambiguous signal Chapter 07 established that consumer capacity, not storage, needs attention, applied here to "how fast can we notify and let offline users catch up" rather than to a generic service queue.

Three choices in this diagram deserve explicit justification. First, the gateway layer is the one place in this whole system that is not fully stateless, which is a deliberate exception to the pattern every earlier chapter in this track assumed — it is why Section 4.2's connection registry exists at all, as the mechanism that lets the rest of the architecture stay stateless and route around that one stateful layer rather than being stateful themselves. Second, the message store and the connection/presence registry are different systems with different consistency needs, mirroring the same separation Chapter 07's chat-adjacent chapter drew between a durable conversation store and a fast, ephemeral rate-limit store: messages must never be lost, while a presence entry expiring a few seconds late is a non-issue. Third, media never flows through the message path inline — Section 6.3's math is exactly why: embedding media bytes in the message store would make its growth, and its replication cost under Chapter 05's sharding, dominated by attachment size rather than by message volume, which is the wrong thing to optimize for.

9. JAVA / SPRING PERSPECTIVE

Spring's WebSocket support gives you most of the plumbing this chapter has described conceptually, provided you still design the connection-routing and fan-out logic around it — Spring does not solve Section 4.2's cross-server-instance problem for you automatically.

9.1 STOMP over WebSocket with @EnableWebSocketMessageBroker

Spring's @EnableWebSocketMessageBroker wires up STOMP (a simple, frame-based messaging sub-protocol) on top of raw WebSockets, giving you destination-based routing (/topic/conversation.{id}, /user/{username}/queue/messages) instead of hand-parsing raw frames yourself. The built-in simple in-memory broker is fine for a single-instance demo, but it is exactly the trap Section 4.2 warns about at production scale: it only knows about sessions connected to that one JVM instance, so a message published on Instance A never reaches a recipient connected to Instance B. Production configurations swap this for an external broker relay (or, more commonly in a microservices setup, keep STOMP's routing model but push cross-instance fan-out through Redis Pub/Sub or Kafka yourself, as shown below) specifically so that "which JVM instance holds this user's socket" stops being Spring's problem and becomes an explicit part of your architecture, per Section 4.2. 

@Configuration
@EnableWebSocketMessageBroker
public class WebSocketConfig implements WebSocketMessageBrokerConfigurer {

    @Override
    public void registerStompEndpoints(StompEndpointRegistry registry) {
        registry.addEndpoint("/chat")
                .setAllowedOriginPatterns("*")
                .withSockJS(); // fallback transport, Section 4.1
    }

    @Override
    public void configureMessageBroker(MessageBrokerRegistry registry) {
        registry.setApplicationDestinationPrefixes("/app");
        registry.enableSimpleBroker("/topic", "/queue"); // dev-only; see note above
    }
}

9.2 Fanning Out Across Instances with Redis Pub/Sub

To make Section 4.2's cross-instance delivery real, each gateway instance subscribes to a Redis Pub/Sub channel keyed to itself (or to the conversations it currently has active members in), and the Message Service publishes to the channel owned by whichever instance the connection registry says holds the recipient — the mechanism Section 7.1's diagram shows abstractly. 

// Message Service: after persisting and sequencing the message,
// resolve the recipient's gateway instance and publish to its channel.

@Service
public class MessageFanoutService {

    private final StringRedisTemplate redis;
    private final ConnectionRegistry connectionRegistry; // wraps Redis lookups

    public void deliver(ChatMessage message, String recipientUserId) {
        Set<String> gatewayIds = connectionRegistry.findGatewaysFor(recipientUserId);

        if (gatewayIds.isEmpty()) {
            offlineDeliveryService.enqueueAndNotify(message, recipientUserId); // Section 5.2
            return;
        }
        for (String gatewayId : gatewayIds) { // multi-device, Section 5's edge case
            redis.convertAndSend("gateway." + gatewayId, serialize(message));
        }
    }
}

// Each Gateway instance, on startup, subscribes to its OWN channel:
@Component
public class GatewayFanoutListener {

    GatewayFanoutListener(RedisMessageListenerContainer container,
                           @Value("${gateway.instance.id}") String instanceId,
                           SimpMessagingTemplate stomp) {
        container.addMessageListener(
            (msg, pattern) -> {
                ChatMessage chatMessage = deserialize(msg.getBody());
                // push to the specific locally-held session, not a broadcast
                stomp.convertAndSendToUser(
                    chatMessage.recipientUserId(), "/queue/messages", chatMessage);
            },
            new ChannelTopic("gateway." + instanceId)
        );
    }
}

At higher sustained throughput, teams commonly replace the Redis Pub/Sub channel with a Kafka topic partitioned by gateway instance ID (or by conversation ID), trading Redis Pub/Sub's simplicity for Kafka's durability and replay (Chapter 07) — relevant if a gateway instance can restart and needs to recover in-flight fan-out messages rather than silently losing whatever was published while it was briefly down. Either way, the architectural point is the same: Spring's WebSocket/STOMP layer handles the "talk to a socket this JVM holds" half of the problem, and an external pub/sub or log system handles the "get the message to the JVM that actually holds the right socket" half — conflating the two, by relying on Spring's in-memory simple broker in production, is the single most common beginner mistake in a Spring-based chat implementation.

10. BEST PRACTICES

  • Persist and sequence a message before attempting delivery, never the reverse — durability must never depend on whether a recipient happens to be reachable at that instant (Section 5.1-5.2).
  • Treat the connection registry as ephemeral, fast-access state, and the message store as durable, slower-access state — never conflate the two into one storage system, the same lesson Chapter 07 drew between a conversation store and a rate-limit store.
  • Design the client to reconnect and resync from a cursor, always — every disconnect (network blip, app backgrounded, server restart) should be handled by the exact same "reconnect, send last-known sequence number, fetch the gap" path, rather than special-casing each disconnect cause.
  • Keep push-notification payloads thin — a "new message" signal, not the message content itself, both for security (push providers are a third party) and so the client always fetches authoritative, current content over your own authenticated channel.
  • Cap group size deliberately, and document the cap as a product and architecture decision together — it is precisely what keeps fan-out-on-write (Section 4.4) cheap enough to use unconditionally.
  • Make heartbeat interval and presence TTL an explicit, tuned trade-off — a shorter TTL gives fresher presence at the cost of more frequent writes to the presence store; pick a number and document why, rather than defaulting to an arbitrary value.

11. COMMON MISTAKES

  • Assuming a load balancer can route a message to "the right server" the way it routes an HTTP request — it cannot; only the connection-registry lookup (Section 4.2) knows which specific gateway instance currently holds a given user's socket.
  • Using a global auto-increment ID or client-supplied timestamp as the sole ordering key instead of a conversation-scoped sequence number, producing subtly wrong ordering under clock skew or concurrent sends (Section 4.3).
  • Partitioning message storage by user_id instead of conversation_id, which scatters a single conversation's history across many shards and turns the single most common read ("this conversation's recent messages") into a fan-out query across shards instead of a single-shard lookup.
  • Relying on Spring's (or any framework's) default in-memory message broker in a multi-instance production deployment and being surprised when messages only reach recipients connected to the same JVM instance as the sender (Section 9.1).
  • Writing a presence update to a durable, heavily-indexed database table on every heartbeat instead of a TTL-based entry in a fast store, turning "is this person online" into an accidental write-amplification problem at scale (Section 4.5).
  • Blocking the sender's "message sent" confirmation on full fan-out completion for a group message, making perceived send latency scale with group size instead of being effectively constant (Section 5.3).
  • Storing media attachments inline in the message row rather than in dedicated object storage referenced by ID, inflating message-store size and replication cost far beyond what message volume alone would require (Section 6.3).

12. PERFORMANCE CONSIDERATIONS

Three distinct metrics matter, and optimizing one does not automatically fix the others: connection capacity (how many idle sockets a gateway fleet can hold — Section 6.1), message delivery latency (end-to-end time from send to the recipient's screen, for an online recipient), and fan-out completion time (how long it takes a large group's entire membership to receive a message).

  • Use an async, event-driven I/O model for the gateway layer (Netty-based servers, Spring WebFlux, or a comparable non-blocking runtime) — a thread-per-connection model collapses well before reaching the connection counts Section 6.1 describes as achievable.
  • Keep the connection-registry lookup on the hot path extremely fast — it runs on every single message delivery attempt, so it belongs in an in-memory store (Redis) with sub-millisecond latency, never a query against the durable message store.
  • Batch and paginate conversation history reads with cursor-based pagination, the same pattern recommended in this series' earlier chat-adjacent material — fetching "everything since the client's last cursor" stays fast regardless of total conversation length.
  • Co-locate hot conversation data in cache (Redis in front of the message store) for actively-open conversations, since the same recent messages are re-read by every participant's client on reconnect or scroll-back.
  • Monitor fan-out completion time as its own alerted metric for large groups, not just overall message-send latency — a slow push-notification provider or a degraded gateway instance can silently delay delivery to a subset of a group's members without showing up in an aggregate average.

13. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

Chat systems carry an unusually sensitive combination of concerns: the content is often deeply private, the connection is long-lived (giving an attacker a longer window than a single request), and the architecture is distributed across many machines that must trust each other's internal messages.

  • Authenticate the WebSocket handshake, and re-validate for the life of the connection — a long-lived connection that only checks auth once at connect time can outlive a revoked token or an expired session if there is no re-validation or short-lived, rotating connection credential.
  • Enforce conversation membership on every read and write, exactly as the earlier chat-adjacent chapter in this series stressed for any persisted, per-user conversational state — a conversation ID alone is never proof of authorization.
  • Consider end-to-end encryption deliberately, and understand what it trades away — true E2E encryption (as WhatsApp and Signal use) means even the server operator cannot read message content, which is a strong privacy guarantee but also removes the server's ability to run server-side spam/abuse detection, full-text search across message content, or message previews in notifications; a system choosing E2E must design around that loss (client-side search indexes, on-device abuse heuristics) rather than silently losing those capabilities.
  • Rate-limit at the connection and message level, by identity — a single compromised or abusive account should not be able to flood a conversation or a group, and a connection sending an abnormal message rate is a strong, cheap-to-detect abuse signal.
  • Treat internal fan-out channels (Redis Pub/Sub, Kafka) as part of the trusted internal network, never exposed externally — a message published on an internal gateway-routing channel carries no end-user authentication of its own; it is trusted because only internal services can reach it, so that network boundary is itself a security control worth stating explicitly in a design review.

14. INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

JUNIORQ1. Why can't a chat system just have the client poll the server every second for new messages?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Polling at an interval short enough to feel real-time generates an enormous, mostly wasted request volume across millions of idle clients — most polls return "nothing new," which is pure overhead on both the client's battery/data and the server's capacity. Polling at an interval long enough to be affordable feels slow and broken to users expecting instant delivery. A persistent connection (WebSocket, or long-polling/SSE as fallbacks) lets the server push a message the instant it exists, with no wasted round trips when nothing is happening.

JUNIORQ2. Why does a chat product default to WebSockets over long polling or SSE for its core messaging transport, and what would actually degrade — not just "feel less elegant" — if you shipped the whole real-time path on long polling instead?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Chat is naturally bidirectional — a client both sends and receives over the same session — and WebSockets give you that over one persistent connection with low per-message overhead, which matters once a session exchanges thousands of small messages. Long polling can approximate real-time delivery, but it re-issues a full HTTP request/response cycle for every receive cycle, which multiplies per-message overhead and connection churn at scale, and it does nothing for the send direction, which still needs an ordinary request anyway. The concrete degradation isn't cosmetic: higher server-side connection/request churn, worse battery and data usage on mobile, and higher realistic latency per message under load, which is exactly why long polling survives only as a fallback for networks that can't sustain a WebSocket, not as a primary transport choice.

JUNIORQ3. Why store conversation metadata and individual messages as two separate entities instead of embedding a conversation's metadata into every message row? What specific operation gets measurably worse if you don't split them?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Conversation metadata (participants, type, title, last-activity timestamp) changes rarely relative to messages, which are written constantly. Denormalizing that metadata into every message row means every metadata update (say, renaming a group) has to fan out and rewrite every historical message row referencing it, which is both wasteful and a correctness hazard if any write fails partway through, leaving some messages with the old metadata and some with the new. Splitting them keeps metadata updates cheap and atomic against one small row, and keeps the high-volume message writes from ballooning in size for data that's redundant across thousands of rows in the same conversation.

JUNIORQ4. Why does a chat message need a sequence number instead of just relying on when it was saved to the database?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Relying purely on a database write timestamp (or a client-reported timestamp) is vulnerable to clock skew between machines, and two messages can be written close enough together that their timestamps tie or even appear reversed. A sequence number assigned by a single authoritative point per conversation gives a strict, unambiguous order regardless of clock behavior, which matters because message order is one of the few things users notice immediately if it's wrong.

JUNIORQ5. Beyond just persisting the message, what does the system still need to do for a currently-offline recipient, and why is dispatching a push notification alone not a sufficient guarantee that they'll actually receive it?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Two things, not one: record that delivery to this user is pending (so there's a durable fact the system can reconcile against later), and dispatch a push notification as a best-effort nudge. The push notification is not the guarantee — a provider outage, a disabled notification permission, or a dismissed alert the user never taps can all mean the push never actually surfaces anything. The real guarantee is that when the recipient's client eventually reconnects, it asks for everything since its last known point in the conversation and gets it — the push is just a way to shorten how long that "eventually" takes, not the delivery mechanism itself.

JUNIORQ6. Why might "last seen 5 minutes ago" not be perfectly accurate?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Presence is typically tracked via periodic heartbeats updating a short-lived (TTL-based) record; a user is marked offline only once their heartbeat record expires without being refreshed. This means "last seen" reflects the last successfully received heartbeat, not necessarily the exact last moment of activity, and a dropped connection with no clean disconnect signal (a dead battery, a lost signal) only becomes visible once the TTL lapses, not instantly.

JUNIORQ7. Should message attachments (images, videos) be stored directly in the same database row as the message text?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

No. Attachments should go to dedicated object storage (like S3), with the message row holding only a reference (an ID or URL) to the stored object. Embedding binary media inline would make the message table's size and replication cost dominated by media rather than message volume, and object storage is purpose-built for serving large binary content efficiently, which a row-oriented message store is not.

MIDQ8. Why can't a standard load balancer simply route "send a message to user X" to the right server the way it routes a normal HTTP request?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

A load balancer distributes requests across backends that are interchangeable because none of them hold state specific to the caller. A chat gateway server is different: once it accepts a user's WebSocket connection, it becomes the one specific machine physically holding that socket — no other server can push data down it. Delivering a message therefore requires first looking up which specific gateway instance currently holds the target user's connection (via a connection registry), then routing to that exact instance, not any healthy one.

MIDQ9. If you removed the connection/presence registry from this architecture entirely, what specifically breaks the first time you try to deliver a message?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

The Message Service would have no way to know which of potentially thousands of gateway instances currently holds a given recipient's live socket — it could only broadcast the message to every gateway instance and let each one check locally whether it happens to hold that user's connection, which works but turns every single delivery into a fleet-wide broadcast instead of a targeted lookup-and-forward, wasting bandwidth and CPU on every instance that doesn't hold the connection. The registry (commonly Redis, mapping user/device to gateway instance, updated on every connect/disconnect) is precisely what turns delivery into a cheap, targeted operation instead of an O(fleet size) broadcast on every message.

MIDQ10. Why is conversation_id the right choice of partition key for the message store, rather than user_id or a global message ID?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

The overwhelmingly dominant read pattern is "give me recent messages for this specific conversation," which should ideally touch exactly one shard. Partitioning by user_id would scatter a single conversation's messages across every participant's shard, and a global incrementing ID gives no locality at all. Partitioning by conversation_id, with a clustering key of sequence number or timestamp, keeps each conversation's history physically co-located and naturally sorted for the common query.

MIDQ11. Why is a wide-column/NoSQL store (Cassandra, DynamoDB, Bigtable) commonly recommended for chat message storage over a relational database?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

A wide-column store's native model — a partition key plus a clustering/sort key that keeps rows for the same partition physically co-located and pre-sorted — maps almost exactly onto "all messages for conversation X, sorted by sequence number." It also handles the write-heavy, append-mostly access pattern of chat (mostly inserts, rare updates, no complex joins) very efficiently at scale. A relational database can model the same thing with a composite index, but the wide-column store is purpose-built for exactly this shape and tends to scale write throughput more cheaply at very high message volumes.

MIDQ12. Why does fan-out-on-write work fine for chat groups, when Chapter 12's social feed problem needs a hybrid fan-out strategy?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Fan-out-on-write's cost scales with the number of recipients. Chat groups are bounded, typically to a few hundred members at most, so fanning a message out to every member immediately is cheap and uniform. A social feed producer can have millions of followers, so fanning out to all of them on every post becomes prohibitively expensive for high-follower accounts specifically — which is why feeds need a hybrid (fan-out-on-write for typical users, fan-out-on-read for very high-follower "hot" producers). Chat avoids that complexity entirely because its scale ceiling per conversation is architecturally much smaller.

MIDQ13. Why do production chat systems track "sent," "delivered," and "read" as three distinct status transitions instead of a single boolean, and what triggers each one under the hood?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Collapsing them into one flag would conflate three genuinely different facts the sender cares about separately: "sent" fires the instant the server durably persists the message, independent of the recipient entirely; "delivered" fires only once the message actually reached the recipient's device (pushed live, or fetched on reconnect); "read" fires only once the recipient's client confirms the user actually viewed it, an explicit event sent back once it's rendered on screen. Each transition is a separate status update routed back to the sender's client through the same connection-routing mechanism used for the original delivery — and keeping them distinct is what lets a client correctly show "sent but not yet delivered" during a recipient's temporary disconnect, instead of that state being indistinguishable from "read."

MIDQ14. A user is logged into the same chat account on both their phone and laptop. What does the connection registry need to support that it wouldn't need for a single-device system?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

It needs to map a user to a set of active gateway connections, not a single one — one entry per device/session, since the same user can be connected to different gateway instances simultaneously from different devices. A message to that user must fan out to every entry in that set, and read/delivery status needs to be reconciled consistently across devices (e.g., reading a message on the phone should typically update the unread state the laptop also sees).

MIDQ15. Why does Spring's default in-memory STOMP message broker fail in a multi-instance production deployment?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

The simple in-memory broker only knows about WebSocket sessions connected to that specific JVM instance. If a sender's request is handled by Instance A but the recipient's socket is held by Instance B, a message routed through Instance A's in-memory broker never reaches Instance B's connection at all. Production deployments need an external relay — a real STOMP broker, or more commonly, Redis Pub/Sub or Kafka used explicitly to carry messages across instances — so delivery doesn't silently depend on sender and recipient happening to share a server.

MIDQ16. What happens to the connection registry entries when a gateway server crashes unexpectedly?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium

Every connection that instance held drops at once, and the stale entries pointing to that now-dead instance must be cleaned up — typically via a TTL on each registry entry, refreshed by heartbeats, so a crashed gateway's entries expire naturally within one TTL window rather than needing an explicit cleanup step tied to detecting the crash. Affected clients detect the dropped socket and reconnect through the load balancer to a different healthy instance, re-registering themselves and catching up via their last-known sequence number, the same path used for any ordinary reconnect.

MIDQ17. Should typing indicators be persisted to the message store the way regular messages are?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

No — typing indicators are ephemeral, high-frequency, and have no lasting value once the moment passes, so persisting them to a durable store would be pure write amplification for no benefit. They're typically pushed directly through the same live connection-routing path as messages (so recipients see them in real time) but are never written to durable storage, and often aren't even sequenced, since losing one is harmless and showing a stale one briefly is a minor cosmetic issue, not a correctness bug.

MIDQ18. How would you implement cursor-based pagination for scrolling back through a long conversation's history?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Use the message's sequence number (or a timestamp paired with a tiebreaker) as the cursor: the client requests "N messages before sequence number X," and the server returns them from the conversation's partition, which is already naturally sorted by that key. This avoids offset-based pagination's problems — slow large offsets and inconsistent results if new messages are inserted mid-scroll — and stays fast and stable no matter how deep into history the user scrolls.

MIDQ19. Why is it important that the sender's message durably persists before any delivery attempt is made, rather than the reverse?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

If delivery were attempted first and persistence happened afterward (or not at all on failure), a crash or network issue during delivery could leave the message lost entirely — the sender believes it was sent, but it exists nowhere durable and the recipient never gets it, even later. Persisting first guarantees the message is never lost regardless of what happens during the delivery attempt; delivery can always be retried later against durable data, but data that was never durably written can't be recovered.

MIDQ20. How does end-to-end encryption change what the server is able to do with message content?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

With true end-to-end encryption, only the sender's and recipient's devices hold the keys to decrypt message content — the server stores and routes opaque ciphertext it cannot read. This means the server can no longer run server-side full-text search over message content, generate content previews for push notifications, or apply server-side spam/abuse content scanning the way it could with plaintext. Products that adopt E2E have to deliberately rebuild any of those capabilities client-side or give them up.

MIDQ21. Why shouldn't push notification payloads contain the full message content?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Push notifications are delivered through a third-party provider (APNs, FCM) outside your own infrastructure's trust boundary, so sending full content through it expands your data's exposure surface unnecessarily — especially relevant for an end-to-end encrypted product, where the server itself may not even be able to produce readable content for the notification. A thin "you have a new message" signal, with the client fetching the actual content over its own authenticated connection once notified, keeps the trust boundary where it belongs.

MIDQ22. What's the risk of writing a presence update to a durable relational table on every heartbeat from every connected user?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium

At scale, that's an enormous, continuous write volume against a durable, typically indexed table — a form of write amplification that dwarfs the actual message-sending traffic for data that doesn't need durability or strong consistency in the first place. A TTL-based entry in a fast in-memory store (Redis) achieves the same practical outcome (is this user currently reachable) far more cheaply, accepting the small staleness window as a reasonable trade-off.

SENIORQ23. Walk through, in detail, how a message gets from Sender A (connected to Gateway 1) to Recipient B (connected to Gateway 7), including every lookup and hop.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Client A sends the message over its socket to Gateway 1. Gateway 1 forwards it to the Message Service, which assigns the next sequence number for the conversation and durably persists the message to its conversation-partitioned store. The Message Service then looks up User B in the connection registry, which returns "Gateway 7." The Message Service publishes the message on an internal channel scoped to Gateway 7 (Redis Pub/Sub or a Kafka topic/partition keyed by gateway ID). Gateway 7, already subscribed to its own channel, receives it and pushes it down User B's live WebSocket. Delivery/read status events flow back along a mirrored path once User B's client acknowledges them.

SENIORQ24. How would you estimate the number of gateway servers needed to support 50 million concurrently connected users?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Start from a realistic sustainable-connections-per-instance figure for a well-tuned, event-driven gateway process — commonly in the hundreds of thousands given modern async I/O models — then divide target concurrent connections by that figure: 50,000,000 ÷ 500,000 ≈ 100 instances, before adding redundancy headroom for instance failure and rolling deploys, and before accounting for geographic distribution if connections need to be routed to the nearest region. The key insight to state explicitly is that this scales primarily by adding machines to spread connection count, not by increasing any single machine's message throughput.

SENIORQ25. Why is a chat gateway layer described as "stateless to deploy, but stateful at runtime" — isn't that a contradiction?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Medium-High

It isn't a contradiction, it's two different senses of "state": any gateway instance can be deployed and can accept a brand-new incoming connection, exactly like a stateless web server handling any new HTTP request — that's the "stateless to deploy" part. But once a specific instance accepts a specific client's WebSocket, that instance now holds real, non-transferable state — the live socket itself — that no other instance can act on. The architecture compensates by routing around that per-connection state via the connection registry rather than pretending the gateway layer is fully stateless the way a typical REST backend is.

Interview Gold Point: Explicitly distinguishing "stateless for routing new connections" from "stateful for the lifetime of an existing connection" is exactly the nuance that separates a candidate who has actually reasoned about this architecture from one reciting "stateless services scale horizontally" without noticing chat breaks that assumption at the connection layer.
SENIORQ26. How would you prevent two messages sent to the same conversation at nearly the same instant, from different servers handling different senders, from being assigned conflicting or out-of-order sequence numbers?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Sequence number assignment for a given conversation needs a single authoritative serialization point — commonly achieved via an atomic increment operation against a per-conversation counter in a fast store, or by routing all writes for a given conversation through a specific partition/shard owner that assigns sequence numbers strictly in the order it receives write requests. Because even a large group's message volume is tiny relative to total system throughput, this serialization point is rarely a bottleneck; the key design point is ensuring it's genuinely a single point of truth per conversation, not something two independent servers could each increment without coordination.

SENIORQ27. A client reconnects after being offline for several hours during a very active group conversation. How should the system handle catching it up?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

The client sends its last-known sequence number (or cursor) for that conversation; the server queries the conversation's partition for everything after that point and returns it, typically paginated if the gap is large rather than returning potentially thousands of messages in one response. The same mechanism works regardless of why the client was offline — network loss, app closed, device off — because it's driven entirely by "what's the highest sequence number I've already seen," not by tracking the specific outage.

SENIORQ28. How would you design rate limiting specifically for a chat system's message-sending path, distinct from a generic API rate limit?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Layer limits at the connection/identity level (messages per second/minute per user, enforced close to the gateway so abusive traffic is rejected before reaching the Message Service and the durable store) and consider per-conversation limits too, since a compromised account flooding one specific group is a different failure mode than flooding broadly across many conversations. A token-bucket counter in a fast store, keyed by user ID and optionally by conversation ID, smooths bursts better than a hard fixed-window reset, and catching abuse this early protects both the fan-out pipeline and the push-notification budget from being burned on spam.

SENIORQ29. What's the correct behavior when a member is removed from a group chat while a message is actively being sent to that group?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Fan-out must consult a current membership list at send time, not a cached-forever one, or a just-removed member could still receive a message sent moments after their removal. In practice this means the removal operation and the membership lookup used for fan-out need to be reasonably consistent with each other — either by invalidating any membership cache on removal, or by reading membership from the authoritative store on every fan-out rather than relying on long-lived cached copies, accepting a small window of eventual consistency rather than strict simultaneity as the practical trade-off.

SENIORQ30. How would you support "message editing" and "message deletion" in this architecture without breaking message ordering or client caches?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Rather than mutating the original row in place (which complicates any client that cached the old version, and loses the edit history), store edits as a new event referencing the original message's ID — an "edited" status flag plus the new content, or a small append-only edit-history list — and fan that event out the same way a new message is fanned out, so connected clients update their already-rendered view in place. Deletion is typically modeled as a tombstone (a flag marking the message deleted, with content cleared or replaced by a placeholder) rather than a true row removal, both to preserve the sequence number's role in ordering and because "deleted" is itself often a fact other participants' clients need to be told about explicitly.

SENIORQ31. Compare storing conversation data in a relational database with a composite index versus a purpose-built wide-column store, at the scale of billions of messages per day.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A relational database with a composite (conversation_id, sequence) index can absolutely serve the same query pattern correctly, and is a perfectly reasonable choice at moderate scale where its transactional guarantees and tooling maturity are worth more than raw write throughput. At billions-of-messages-per-day volume, the operational cost of scaling a relational database's write throughput (sharding it yourself, managing replication topology) tends to exceed a wide-column store's, since stores like Cassandra or DynamoDB are built from the ground up for exactly this append-heavy, partition-and-sort-key access pattern with horizontal write scaling as a first-class property rather than something bolted on.

SENIORQ32. How would you handle a "hot" group conversation — a company-wide channel with thousands of members, all highly active simultaneously — differently from a typical small group?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

At thousands of members, pure fan-out-on-write starts to resemble the social-feed hot-key problem this chapter said chat normally avoids — the write/push cost for that one conversation can spike disproportionately. Mitigations include batching deliveries rather than firing all of them as independent operations, caching the active membership list aggressively so fan-out doesn't re-fetch it on every message, and, for truly very large channels, considering a read-oriented model for less-active members (only push to currently-connected members in real time, let less-active members pull on next open) — effectively borrowing a hybrid fan-out idea from Chapter 12's feed design for this one specific high-member-count case, rather than applying it system-wide.

SENIORQ33. Why might a chat product choose Kafka instead of Redis Pub/Sub for cross-gateway-instance message fan-out?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium

Redis Pub/Sub is fire-and-forget — if a gateway instance is briefly down or disconnected when a message is published to its channel, that message is simply lost, with no replay. Kafka retains messages durably and lets a recovering gateway instance (or a redeployed one) resume consuming from where it left off, which matters if you need a stronger guarantee that fan-out is not silently dropped during a gateway restart. The trade-off is added operational complexity and latency versus Redis Pub/Sub's simplicity and speed — a reasonable team might start with Redis and move to Kafka once durability through gateway restarts becomes a real production requirement rather than a theoretical one.

SENIORQ34. How would you detect and handle a "poison" message — one that consistently fails to deliver to a specific recipient due to a bug or malformed payload?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

This is the same dead-letter-queue pattern covered for general message queues: after a bounded number of failed delivery attempts to a specific recipient, route that specific delivery attempt to a dead-letter path for manual inspection rather than retrying indefinitely, and — critically — make sure this one recipient's persistent failure doesn't block delivery to the conversation's other members, who should still receive the message normally.

SENIORQ35. What are the trade-offs of storing all of a user's conversations' metadata in one row versus a separate row per conversation per user?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Low-Medium

A single row per user holding a list of all their conversations is simple to read in one query (useful for the conversation list screen), but every update to any one conversation's metadata (last message preview, unread count) forces a read-modify-write of that entire row, and concurrent updates from different conversations collide on the same row. A separate row per (user, conversation) pair avoids that contention — each conversation's state updates independently — at the cost of needing a multi-row read (or a secondary index/cache) to assemble the full conversation list view. Most production systems choose the latter for exactly this contention reason.

SENIORQ36. How would you implement "mute notifications but still receive messages" for a specific conversation?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Low-Medium

Store a per-user, per-conversation preference flag, checked at the point where a push notification would otherwise be dispatched for an offline or backgrounded recipient — the message itself is still persisted and delivered normally over the live connection or on reconnect catch-up; only the external push-notification step is suppressed. This keeps "mute" a notification-layer decision, not a delivery-layer one, so the user's message history and unread counts remain fully correct even while notifications are silenced.

STAFFQ37. Design the full connection-routing and fan-out architecture for a global chat product with users in North America, Europe, and Asia, where most conversations are between users in the same region but some cross regions.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Route each client's WebSocket connection to the gateway region closest to them for lowest connection latency, with a regional connection registry tracking which gateway instance (within that region) holds each local connection. For same-region conversations, the entire flow — persist, look up recipient, fan out — stays within one region, which is the common case and should be optimized for. For cross-region conversations, the message still needs to be durably written somewhere authoritative (commonly the conversation's "home region," chosen at creation time, often based on where the conversation was started or where most participants live), and a cross-region replication or forwarding step gets the message to the recipient's local region's gateway for final delivery — accepting the added cross-region latency as the unavoidable cost for that minority of conversations, rather than forcing every conversation through a single global write path and paying that latency tax on the common case too.

Interview Gold Point: Explicitly separating "optimize the common case (same-region)" from "accept extra cost on the uncommon case (cross-region)" rather than designing one uniform global path for everything is the specific judgment staff-level interviewers are listening for here.
STAFFQ38. A product decision is made to add full end-to-end encryption to an existing chat product that currently supports server-side search and rich push notification previews. What architectural changes does this force, and what trade-offs would you present to leadership?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Once content is encrypted such that the server never sees plaintext, server-side full-text search over message content and content-rich push previews both become impossible in their current form, since the server literally cannot read what it's searching or previewing. The architectural replacements are client-side: an on-device search index built and maintained locally by each client over its own decrypted message history (at the cost of search only covering messages that specific device has already synced and indexed, not a true global server-side search), and notification previews either omitted entirely or decrypted client-side after the OS delivers a generic "new message" push — which on some platforms requires a notification-service-extension pattern to decrypt just before display. The trade-off to present plainly: a meaningful, genuine privacy and trust improvement, in exchange for real, permanent feature regressions in search comprehensiveness and notification richness that cannot be fully recovered without breaking the encryption guarantee itself — this is a product trade-off as much as an engineering one, and should be framed to leadership as such rather than as a pure engineering migration.

STAFFQ39. How would you evolve this chat architecture to support a feature where a very large public channel (100,000+ members, like a large Discord server) coexists with normal small group chats, without redesigning the whole system?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Keep the existing fan-out-on-write path as the default for the overwhelming majority of conversations, which remain small, and introduce a separate, explicitly different code path for channels above a defined member-count threshold — closer to the hybrid fan-out model Chapter 12 uses for social feeds: push in real time only to currently-connected, actively-viewing members (a small fraction of 100,000 at any instant), and let less-active members pull the latest messages on next open rather than receiving a live push. This requires the system to classify a conversation's fan-out strategy at write time based on its member count (and potentially re-classify it if it crosses the threshold), rather than assuming every conversation uses the same fan-out path — the architectural principle being that you only pay for a more complex delivery model where the simple one's cost actually breaks down, not everywhere uniformly.

STAFFQ40. What non-functional requirements would most change your design, and how would each one push the architecture in a different direction?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Target message-delivery latency (tens versus hundreds of milliseconds) drives how aggressively you need regional gateway placement and how much you can tolerate a cross-region hop on the common path. Expected maximum group size drives whether fan-out-on-write alone suffices or a hybrid model is needed from day one. Durability and compliance requirements (data residency, retention mandates) drive whether a single global message store is even legally viable versus region-pinned storage with its own cross-region complexity. And a requirement for true end-to-end encryption versus "encrypted at rest and in transit but server-readable" fundamentally changes what server-side features (search, spam detection, rich previews) are even achievable, as covered in Q38. Naming these up front, and showing how each pushes the design differently, is what separates a staff-level design conversation from reciting one fixed architecture regardless of the actual requirements given.

STAFFQ41. How would you design this system to support compliance requirements like data residency (a government requiring its citizens' message data to stay within national borders) and legal hold / e-discovery (preserving specific users' messages indefinitely for litigation, even past normal retention)?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at staff level

Data residency pushes toward region-pinned storage per the Q37 discussion, with conversation "home region" assignment needing to respect the residency requirements of the participants involved, not just latency optimization — which can create genuine conflicts (a conversation between users in two different residency-restricted jurisdictions) that need an explicit policy decision, not just an engineering default. Legal hold requires a mechanism to flag specific users or conversations as exempt from normal retention/deletion policies, which argues for retention and deletion being enforced by an explicit, auditable policy engine consulting a hold list, rather than a blanket TTL or deletion job that has no concept of exceptions — a detail that's easy to omit from the initial design and expensive to retrofit once the retention pipeline already exists without hooks for it.

STAFFQ42. If you had to cut this system's infrastructure cost by 40% without degrading the core 1:1 and small-group messaging experience, where would you look first?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at staff level

Start by measuring where cost actually concentrates rather than guessing: gateway connection count (often dominated by idle connections, where tuning keep-alive/heartbeat intervals and connection pooling parameters can reduce per-connection overhead), message-store storage and read/write capacity (where tiered retention — aggressively compressing or archiving old, rarely-accessed message history to cheaper storage — is usually a large, low-risk win since most reads concentrate on recent messages), and push-notification volume (where batching or deduplicating redundant notification triggers can meaningfully cut third-party provider costs). The things to explicitly avoid cutting are the aspects directly tied to perceived quality for the core experience — delivery latency for online recipients and message durability — since those are exactly what users notice immediately if degraded, unlike infrastructure headroom or long-tail history access speed.

STAFFQ43. What's the single biggest architectural mistake teams make the first time they build a chat system from scratch?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at staff level

Treating it as "a database plus a WebSocket" and only discovering under real scale and real network conditions that the hard problems live in connection routing across many server instances, store-and-forward delivery for offline users, and fan-out cost for groups — none of which a basic WebSocket-plus-database prototype naturally forces you to confront, since a demo with a handful of users and a single server instance never exercises any of them. Teams that design the connection registry, offline-delivery path, and fan-out strategy deliberately from the start, even at modest initial scale, avoid the expensive retrofit that teams who "just add Redis later" or "just add a second server later" tend to face once their single-instance assumption quietly breaks in production.

STAFFQ44. Summarize the handful of trade-offs that are genuinely specific to chat and messaging systems, as opposed to general distributed-systems trade-offs any backend faces.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High as a wrap-up/closing question

Four stand out. First, the connection-routing problem — needing to find and reach one specific stateful server instance out of many, which simply doesn't exist in a stateless request/response system and breaks the usual "any healthy backend can serve any request" assumption. Second, the fan-out cost asymmetry between small, bounded chat groups (where fan-out-on-write is uncontested) and the much larger, hot-key-prone fan-out problem a social feed faces, despite both being structurally "one write, many reads/recipients." Third, presence as a deliberately eventually-consistent, TTL-driven signal rather than a strongly consistent fact, which is a different flavor of eventual consistency than the replication-lag kind covered earlier in this track. Fourth, the direct tension end-to-end encryption creates between privacy and server-side product features (search, previews, abuse detection) that simply doesn't arise in systems without a plaintext-content trust boundary to begin with.

MIDQ45. How would you design read-receipt tracking for a group conversation, where "read" status needs to show which specific members have seen a message?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Store a per-message, per-member read marker (or, more efficiently at scale, a per-member "highest sequence number read" cursor for the conversation, rather than one row per message per member) updated when that member's client confirms viewing. A "read by" list for a given message is then computed by checking which members' cursors have advanced past that message's sequence number, which is far cheaper to store and update than an explicit read-receipt row per message per member, especially for large groups.

SENIORQ46. How would you prevent a single very chatty user from degrading the experience for everyone else connected to the same gateway instance?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium

Per-connection and per-user rate limiting enforced at the gateway itself, before a message even reaches the Message Service, protects both that gateway instance's local resources (so one user's burst doesn't starve other connections it's holding) and downstream capacity. This should be a hard, fast check close to the edge, distinct from any business-logic-level abuse detection that might run asynchronously afterward — the rate limit's job is purely to bound resource consumption per connection, not to make a judgment call about intent.

MIDQ47. Why is it generally a bad idea to let the message content size be effectively unbounded?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Low-Medium

An unbounded message size directly inflates worst-case storage per row, worst-case fan-out payload size (multiplied by however many recipients a group has), and worst-case bandwidth per push — none of which scale gracefully, and all of which a malicious or buggy client could exploit to disproportionately burden the system with very few messages. A reasonable hard cap on text length, with large content redirected to the media/attachment path instead, keeps the message-delivery hot path predictable and cheap regardless of what any individual client sends.

SENIORQ48. How would you handle the scenario where a push notification provider (APNs or FCM) is experiencing a partial outage?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Low-Medium

Treat push dispatch as a best-effort, retriable side effect decoupled from the core message-delivery guarantee, not a dependency the core system blocks on — the message itself remains durably stored and will still be delivered the moment the recipient's client reconnects or opens the app, independent of whether the push notification ever successfully fires. Retries with backoff and a bounded attempt count (with eventual drop, since a stale "you missed a message from 2 hours ago" push has diminishing value) are appropriate for the push step specifically, while the message-store write path should never be coupled to, or blocked by, the push provider's availability.

MIDQ49. Why can't "search within a conversation" just run as a query against the message store's existing partition-and-sequence index, and what has to be built alongside it instead (assuming no end-to-end encryption constraint)?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

The partition-and-sequence index is built for "give me messages in order," not "find messages containing this text" — it has no notion of token matching, relevance, or arbitrary substring lookup, so a text search against it would mean scanning every message in the partition linearly. What's needed instead is a dedicated full-text search engine (Elasticsearch, or a database's native full-text index) that message content is indexed into as messages are written, scoped so a query can search within one conversation or across every conversation a user participates in. That indexing step runs asynchronously off the write path, kept only eventually consistent with the message store, specifically so indexing latency never becomes a dependency of message-send latency.

STAFFQ50. How would you design this system to gracefully degrade — rather than fail outright — during a partial outage of the connection/presence registry (Redis) itself?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at staff level

Since the registry is on the hot path for every delivery, its outage is serious, but the degradation should be graceful rather than total: already-open WebSocket connections can often keep functioning for messages that don't require a fresh registry lookup (e.g., a gateway instance can still accept and persist incoming messages even if it temporarily can't resolve delivery routing), falling back to treating affected recipients as effectively "offline" and routing through the store-and-forward/push-notification path until the registry recovers, rather than dropping messages outright. This requires the offline-delivery path to be robust enough to absorb a temporary surge if it suddenly becomes the fallback for all online users too — a capacity assumption worth stating explicitly, since a registry outage turning every user "offline" at once is a very different load profile than the path was likely provisioned for under normal operation.

Interview Gold Point: Naming that the offline-delivery fallback path needs its own capacity headroom for the scenario where it suddenly serves everyone, not just the normal small trickle of actually-offline users, is the kind of second-order capacity reasoning that distinguishes a staff-level answer from one that stops at "fail over to the backup path."

16. SUMMARY

  • A chat system's defining architectural challenge is that the server must push to clients unsolicited, which requires long-lived connections (WebSockets, with long-polling/SSE as fallbacks) rather than the request/response model every earlier chapter in this track assumed.
  • Because a specific gateway server instance — not any interchangeable one — holds a given client's live connection, a connection-routing/presence registry is required to find and reach the right instance; Chapter 03's load balancer only distributes new connections, it cannot route an in-flight message to the right place.
  • Partitioning message storage by conversation_id, with a conversation-scoped sequence number as the ordering key, matches the dominant read pattern and avoids the ordering ambiguity that timestamps alone introduce under clock skew or concurrency.
  • Offline delivery is store-and-forward, directly reusing Chapter 07's at-least-once and cursor/offset-catch-up patterns applied to a single human recipient instead of a service consumer.
  • Group fan-out defaults to fan-out-on-write because chat group sizes are small and bounded, unlike a social feed's potentially massive follower counts (Chapter 12), which is exactly why chat avoids the hybrid fan-out complexity a feed system requires.
  • Presence ("last seen," "online now") is an inherently eventually-consistent, TTL-driven inference, not a strongly consistent real-time fact, and treating it as the latter leads to both wrong product expectations and unnecessary write-amplification in its implementation.
