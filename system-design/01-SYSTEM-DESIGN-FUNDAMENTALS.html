CHAPTER 01 — SYSTEM DESIGN FUNDAMENTALS

Volume 1 — Foundations · Chapter 1 of 12

Every chapter after this one assumes you already know how to think about a system, not just how to describe one component of it. System design interviews are notorious for feeling unlike any other interview format a backend engineer prepares for: there is no compiler to tell you that you're wrong, no single accepted solution to memorize, and the "correct" answer changes depending on numbers nobody told you until you asked for them. This chapter is the on-ramp for the rest of the track — load balancing, caching, database replication, consistent hashing, message queues, consensus, and the case-study chapters on a URL shortener, a chat system, and a news feed all assume you can already scope a problem, do back-of-the-envelope math, and talk fluently about the vertical-vs-horizontal and stateless-vs-stateful trade-offs covered here. Skimming this chapter is the single most common reason candidates who know every individual building block still fail the interview: they never learned how to assemble the blocks under uncertainty.

1. MOTIVATION

Ask a strong backend engineer to reverse a linked list and they will solve it in minutes. Ask the same engineer to "design Twitter" and many freeze — not because the problem is intellectually harder in a narrow sense, but because it is a different kind of problem, and nobody told them the rules changed. A coding interview has a correct answer: your function either reverses the list or it doesn't. A system design interview has no such target. The interviewer is not secretly holding an architecture diagram in a drawer, waiting for you to reproduce it. They are watching how you behave when the problem is underspecified, the scale is unknown, and every design choice trades one desirable property for another.

This is the first and most important mental shift this chapter asks you to make: a system design interview evaluates your reasoning process, not your final diagram. Specifically, interviewers are assessing a handful of concrete, observable behaviors — do you ask clarifying questions before committing to a design, or do you charge ahead on assumptions nobody validated? Do you quantify scale with real numbers before choosing a database, or do you reach for "we'll use a NoSQL database because it scales" as a reflex phrase with no math behind it? When two components trade off against each other — consistency versus availability, latency versus throughput, cost versus redundancy — can you name the trade-off explicitly and justify the side you picked for this problem's constraints, or do you present your first idea as though it were the only possible one?

There is a very practical reason this matters beyond passing an interview. Production systems fail, cost money, and get paged on at 3 a.m. almost never because an engineer wrote a buggy loop — they fail because of decisions made at the system level: a service assumed to be stateless was not, a cache was invalidated incorrectly under concurrent writes, a queue absorbed more load than its consumers could drain, a single database was asked to serve both the write-heavy and read-heavy halves of a workload with the same schema and the same hardware. The skills this track builds — scoping, estimation, and trade-off reasoning — are the actual job of a senior backend or platform engineer, independent of whether you ever sit another interview. The interview format simply compresses that job into forty-five minutes and asks you to narrate your thinking out loud.

Finally, notice what this chapter does not try to do: it does not hand you a load balancer, a caching strategy, a replication topology, or a consensus algorithm — those get full chapters each, later in this volume, precisely because they deserve depth this orientation chapter cannot give them. What this chapter gives you instead is the scaffolding that makes those later chapters usable under interview or production pressure: how to scope a problem correctly, how to reason about scale with real arithmetic, and the vocabulary (stateless vs. stateful, vertical vs. horizontal, latency vs. throughput) that every subsequent chapter in this track will assume you already have cold.

2. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

2.1 The Monolith-on-One-Machine Era

For most of commercial computing history, "system design" barely existed as a distinct discipline, because most systems ran on one machine. A single server ran the application code, held the database, and served every user directly. Scaling meant buying a bigger machine — more RAM, a faster CPU, a faster disk — an approach now called vertical scaling. This worked fine as long as the number of users and the volume of data a business needed to handle stayed within what one increasingly expensive machine could hold. The web's growth in the late 1990s broke that assumption for an increasing number of companies, and broke it permanently.

2.2 The Three-Tier Architecture and the First Scaling Pressure (Late 1990s–2000s)

As traffic grew, engineers split the monolith into layers — a presentation tier, an application/business-logic tier, and a data tier — primarily to let each layer be developed, deployed, and scaled somewhat independently. This is the ancestor of the "app server behind a load balancer, talking to a database" shape that still underlies most systems designed in interviews today. Running multiple identical application servers in parallel, with a load balancer distributing requests across them, was the first widespread instance of horizontal scaling — and it immediately surfaced a problem that did not exist on a single machine: if a user's session data lived only in the memory of the one server that first handled their request, a load balancer sending their next request to a different server would find that server with no idea who the user was. This single, concrete failure is the historical origin of the stateless-service design principle covered in depth later in this chapter — it was not an abstract best practice invented in a textbook, it was a bug that production systems hit as soon as they tried to run more than one application server.

2.3 The Web-Scale Papers That Created Modern Distributed Systems Thinking (2003–2007)

The specific vocabulary and trade-offs this entire track is built on trace directly to a small number of papers published by companies that hit scale problems years before anyone else did. Google's 2003 Google File System paper and 2004 MapReduce paper showed that commodity hardware, used in large numbers with the right software layer on top, could do what previously required extremely expensive specialized machines — establishing "scale out with cheap machines, not up with expensive ones" as the dominant industry strategy. Amazon's 2007 Dynamo paper, describing the highly-available key-value store behind Amazon's shopping cart, popularized the idea that a system could deliberately trade strict consistency for availability and partition tolerance — a trade-off formalized slightly earlier, in 2000, by Eric Brewer's CAP theorem (the full subject of Chapter 2). These papers did not just describe internal systems; they taught an entire generation of engineers that distributed systems have structurally different failure modes and trade-offs than single-machine systems, which is exactly the shift in thinking a system design interview is testing for today.

2.4 Cloud, Microservices, and the Interview Format Itself (2010s–Present)

The 2010s made horizontal scaling the default rather than the exception, for two compounding reasons: cloud providers (AWS, GCP, Azure) made provisioning a hundred servers as easy as provisioning one, removing the capital-expense barrier that previously made vertical scaling the pragmatic default for smaller companies; and the microservices pattern — decomposing a monolith into many independently deployable services communicating over a network — made distributed-systems trade-offs (network partitions, eventual consistency, service discovery, distributed tracing) a concern for ordinary backend engineers, not just a small number of infrastructure specialists at a handful of web-scale companies. The system design interview format itself solidified during this period specifically because companies needed a way to assess whether a candidate could reason about these trade-offs — the interview you are preparing for is a relatively direct reflection of what this decade's shift actually demanded of working engineers.

When you're asked to justify a design choice in an interview, you get real credit for connecting it to the concrete failure it prevents ("we externalize session state because otherwise a load balancer can't freely route a user's next request to a different server"), not just naming the pattern.

3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

Every chapter in this book series so far in this track addresses one piece of a larger question. This chapter states that question precisely, because every subsequent chapter is really just supplying one more tool for answering it:

Given a product requirement, finite engineering time, finite money, and an uncertain future scale, how do you choose an architecture whose behavior you can predict and justify — under both normal load and partial failure — using explicit trade-offs rather than a single memorized "correct" design?

Three things make this harder than it sounds, and each one maps directly onto a later section of this chapter. First, requirements are almost never fully specified up front — real product requirements, and interview prompts deliberately imitating them, leave out details on purpose, and the engineer who doesn't actively recover those details (Section 4.1, scoping) ends up solving a different problem than the one that was actually asked. Second, "will it work" is a different question from "will it work at the scale this will actually see" — a design that is obviously correct for 100 users per day can be entirely wrong for 100 million, not because the logic is incorrect but because the physical constraints (network bandwidth, disk I/O, memory) that were irrelevant at small scale become the dominant cost at large scale, which is why estimation (Section 6) is not optional busywork but a load-bearing step in the design process. Third, distributed systems fail in ways single-machine systems structurally cannot — a network link can degrade without fully failing, two servers can disagree about the current state of the world for a brief window, a message can be delivered twice — and a design that silently assumes none of this will happen is not a simplification, it is a latent production incident.

Note what this problem statement is explicitly not asking: it is not asking "what is the single best architecture for a chat application," because no such universal answer exists independent of scale, latency requirements, consistency requirements, team size, and budget. A system design interview — and a real system design task at work — is always implicitly parameterized by requirements that have to be extracted or assumed, which is exactly why Section 4's scoping discipline comes before any discussion of load balancers, caches, or databases: you cannot correctly select a tool before you know what you're building.

4. THEORY

4.1 Scoping a Problem: Functional vs. Non-Functional Requirements

Every system design problem splits cleanly into two categories of requirements, and conflating them is one of the fastest ways to produce a design that technically does what was asked but is unusable in practice. Functional requirements describe what the system does — the features and behaviors a user or client can observe: "users can post a short message," "users can follow other users," "a shortened URL redirects to the original URL." Non-functional requirements describe how well the system does it, under what constraints, and they are the requirements that actually determine architecture: expected scale (how many users, how many requests per second), latency targets (how fast must a response return), availability targets (how much downtime is tolerable), consistency requirements (can a user ever see slightly stale data, or must every read reflect the latest write), and durability requirements (can any data ever be lost).

The reason this distinction matters so much in practice: two systems with identical functional requirements can require completely different architectures once their non-functional requirements diverge. "Users can post a short message" describes both Twitter and a tiny internal team-status tool — but one needs to serve hundreds of thousands of reads per second with sub-second latency and tolerate eventual consistency on a user's own feed, while the other can run comfortably on a single small server with a single relational database and strict consistency. A candidate (or engineer) who starts sketching Kafka topics and sharded databases before establishing which of these two worlds they're actually in has skipped the one step that determines whether any of that complexity is justified.

4.2 Clarifying Questions: Recovering the Requirements Nobody Stated

Because non-functional requirements are rarely handed to you explicitly, the discipline of asking clarifying questions is not interview theater — it is the only mechanism available for converting an underspecified prompt into a well-posed engineering problem. A useful clarifying pass, in roughly this order, covers: the core use cases to actually support (do not try to design every feature a real version of this product has — pick the two or three that matter most and say so explicitly); the read/write ratio (a system dominated by reads, like a news feed, is architected very differently from one dominated by writes, like a logging/metrics pipeline); the expected scale (daily active users, requests per second, data volume — even rough orders of magnitude change the answer, as Section 6 makes concrete); the consistency requirements (can this tolerate eventually-consistent reads, or does correctness require every read to reflect the most recent write); and the latency/availability targets (is this a user-facing path where every extra 100ms is felt directly, or a background/batch process where minutes of delay are acceptable).

Critically, the goal of this step is not to extract a complete specification before doing any design work — that is neither possible nor expected. The goal is to state your assumptions explicitly, out loud, so that the rest of your design can be evaluated against them, and so that if an assumption turns out to be wrong, exactly one part of the design needs to change rather than the whole thing being invalidated silently.

4.3 Vertical Scaling vs. Horizontal Scaling

Once requirements are scoped, the single most foundational architectural lever is how you plan to handle growth in load. Vertical scaling (scaling up) means increasing the capacity of a single machine — more CPU cores, more RAM, faster disks, a faster network interface. Horizontal scaling (scaling out) means adding more machines and distributing load across all of them.

In practice, almost every real production system uses both: individual machines are sized reasonably (not the cheapest possible instance, but not an extreme one either), and horizontal scaling is layered on top to handle growth and provide redundancy. The architectural decision that matters most is not "which one, exclusively" but "is my application capable of running as multiple horizontally-scaled instances at all" — which is precisely the stateless-vs-stateful question below, because a stateful application cannot be scaled horizontally without first solving where the state lives.

4.4 Stateless vs. Stateful Services, and Why Statelessness Enables Horizontal Scaling

A service is stateful if it stores data about an ongoing interaction — a user's session, an in-progress multi-step transaction, an in-memory cache of a user's recent activity — in the memory (or local disk) of the specific instance that handled a previous request, such that a different instance handling that same user's next request would not have access to that data. A service is stateless if every request carries (or can retrieve from a shared, external store) everything needed to handle it, so that literally any instance of the service can handle any request, with no dependency on which instance handled the previous one.

Statelessness is not a stylistic preference — it is the specific property that makes horizontal scaling and load balancing actually work. A load balancer distributing requests across many instances, by design, does not guarantee that the same user's requests land on the same instance twice in a row (and should not be made to, since "sticky sessions" that force this reintroduce the exact single-point-of-failure problem horizontal scaling was meant to remove). If an application instance holds state a request depends on only in its own local memory, routing that user's next request to a different instance produces either an outright error or, worse, silently incorrect behavior. The fix, covered concretely in the Java/Spring perspective in Section 9, is to externalize whatever state would otherwise live locally — move session data into a shared store like Redis, move file uploads into object storage rather than local disk, and treat every individual application instance as disposable and interchangeable. Once that's true, horizontal scaling becomes a matter of adding more identical, interchangeable instances behind the load balancer — and, just as importantly, any single instance can be killed and replaced (due to a crash, a deployment, or autoscaling) without the user ever noticing, because no instance holds anything that can't be recovered elsewhere.

4.5 A One-Sentence Primer on the Building Blocks (Full Chapters Ahead)

The rest of this track gives each of the following its own dedicated, deep treatment. This chapter introduces them only so you have working vocabulary for the diagrams and architecture discussion later in this chapter — do not expect, or need, full mastery of any of these yet.

  • Load balancer — a component that sits in front of multiple application instances and distributes incoming requests across them, enabling horizontal scaling and removing any single instance as a point of failure (full treatment, alongside DNS, in Chapter 3).
  • Cache — a fast, usually in-memory store that holds a copy of frequently-accessed data closer to where it's needed, reducing load on slower backing systems like a database (full treatment, alongside CDNs, in Chapter 4).
  • Database (with replication and partitioning) — the system of record for durable data, which itself needs to scale via copies of the data (replication) and splitting data across machines (sharding) once a single database instance can no longer hold or serve the full load (full treatment in Chapters 5 and 6).
  • Message queue — a component that lets one part of a system hand off work to another part asynchronously, decoupling the producer's speed from the consumer's speed and absorbing bursts of load (full treatment, alongside event-driven architecture, in Chapter 7).

Every one of these exists to solve a specific problem that shows up once you've done the scoping and estimation work this chapter teaches — they are tools selected in response to a quantified need, never a reflexive default.

5. INTERNAL WORKING

5.1 The Mechanical Process of Scoping and Designing Under Time Pressure

Section 4 explained scoping conceptually; this section walks through the actual sequence of steps, in order, the way you would execute them in a forty-five-minute interview or a real design-review meeting. Skipping a step, or doing them out of order, is the single most common structural mistake candidates make, independent of how much they know about any individual building block.

  1. Clarify the scope (2–5 minutes). State which use cases you will and will not cover, and ask the handful of questions from Section 4.2 that materially change the design (scale, read/write ratio, consistency needs, latency target). Do not ask questions whose answer wouldn't change anything you'd design — that wastes time without reducing ambiguity that matters.
  2. Estimate scale (3–5 minutes). Convert whatever numbers you have (or reasonable assumptions, stated explicitly) into concrete requests-per-second, storage, and bandwidth figures using the math in Section 6. This single step is what lets you justify every subsequent architectural choice with a number instead of a guess.
  3. Define the API / interface (optional, 2–3 minutes). For many problems, sketching the handful of core API endpoints or method signatures a client would call forces you to confirm you actually understand the functional requirements before you draw any boxes and arrows.
  4. Design the high-level architecture (10–15 minutes). Draw the major components — client, load balancer, application tier, cache, database, queue — and the data flow between them for the core use cases identified in step 1. At this stage, favor the simplest design that satisfies the requirements; added complexity should be introduced later, in response to a specific bottleneck you can name.
  5. Deep-dive on one or two components (10–15 minutes). The interviewer will typically steer you toward the part of the design they most want to probe — how you'd partition the database, how you'd keep a cache consistent, how you'd handle a specific failure mode. This is where the later chapters in this track (replication, consistent hashing, consensus) get applied.
  6. Identify bottlenecks and trade-offs (remaining time). Proactively name the weakest point in your own design — the component most likely to become a bottleneck at 10x the scale you designed for, or the trade-off (e.g., eventual consistency) you accepted and why. This step, done unprompted, is one of the strongest signals of seniority an interviewer sees.

The internal logic behind this ordering is worth making explicit: each step produces an input the next step needs. You cannot estimate scale (step 2) without knowing which use cases you're estimating for (step 1); you cannot meaningfully choose a database or caching strategy (step 4) without knowing the read/write ratio and data volume (step 2); and you cannot have a productive deep-dive (step 5) on a component whose role in the overall data flow hasn't been established (step 4). Jumping straight to step 4 or 5 — drawing a detailed diagram with a specific NoSQL database and a specific caching layer before any scoping or estimation — is exactly the failure mode Section 1 described, and it is recoverable only by backing up and doing the skipped steps, which costs far more time than doing them first would have.

5.2 How Statelessness Actually Works Underneath a Load Balancer

Mechanically, when a stateless application tier sits behind a load balancer, each incoming request is handled as follows: the load balancer selects an available instance (by round-robin, least-connections, or another algorithm covered in Chapter 3) with no memory of which instance handled this client's previous request. The chosen instance reads everything it needs to process the request from the request itself (headers, body, an auth token) and from shared external stores (a database, a distributed cache like Redis holding session data) — never from its own local, instance-specific memory for anything that must persist across requests. Once the response is sent, the instance retains nothing about that request that a future request depends on. This is what makes it safe for the load balancer to send the client's very next request to a completely different, interchangeable instance, and it's what makes it safe for an autoscaling system to terminate an instance mid-traffic (after draining its in-flight requests) without any user-visible data loss — the terminated instance was never the sole holder of anything important.

6. MATHEMATICS

"Back-of-the-envelope" estimation is the single most concrete, checkable skill this chapter teaches, and it is graded far more literally than most candidates expect — a wildly wrong order of magnitude (confusing thousands with millions, or megabytes with gigabytes) is a real, visible mistake, not a stylistic quibble. The goal is never precision; it's getting within the right order of magnitude fast enough to let it actually inform a design decision.

6.1 The Core Formulas

Queries Per Second (QPS):
    QPS = (number of users) x (actions per user per day) / (seconds per day)
    seconds per day ≈ 86,400  (round to 100,000 for fast mental math)

Peak QPS (traffic is never perfectly uniform across the day):
    Peak QPS ≈ Average QPS x (peak factor, typically 2x-3x)

Storage:
    Total Storage = (number of records) x (average size per record)
    Storage over time = (writes per day) x (size per write) x (retention period in days)

Bandwidth:
    Bandwidth = QPS x (average payload size per request)

6.2 Worked Example: Estimating for a URL-Shortener-Style Service

Suppose a clarifying conversation establishes: 100 million monthly active users, each user shortens 1 URL per month on average (write path) but clicks on shortened links far more often — assume a 100:1 read-to-write ratio, a typical shape for this kind of service. Links are retained for 5 years. Each stored record (short code, original URL, metadata) averages roughly 500 bytes. 

Writes (new short URLs created):
    100,000,000 users x 1 write/month  = 100,000,000 writes/month
    100,000,000 / 30 days               ≈ 3,333,333 writes/day
    3,333,333 / 86,400 seconds           ≈ 39 writes/second (average)
    Peak write QPS (x3)                 ≈ 117 writes/second

Reads (redirects served):
    Read-to-write ratio 100:1  →  100 x 39 ≈ 3,900 reads/second (average)
    Peak read QPS (x3)                  ≈ 11,700 reads/second

Storage (5-year retention):
    3,333,333 writes/day x 365 x 5 years ≈ 6.08 billion records
    6.08 billion x 500 bytes             ≈ 3.04 TB total storage over 5 years

Bandwidth (read path, assuming ~1 KB average response incl. redirect overhead):
    Peak reads/sec (11,700) x 1 KB        ≈ 11.7 MB/second peak read bandwidth

Three design decisions fall directly out of this arithmetic, which is the entire point of doing it. First, the read path (thousands of QPS) is roughly two orders of magnitude busier than the write path (tens to low hundreds of QPS) — this single ratio is what justifies prioritizing a caching layer (Chapter 4) for redirects over optimizing the write path further. Second, 3 TB over five years is entirely tractable for a single well-chosen database or a modestly sharded setup — it does not, by itself, justify an exotic, heavily-partitioned data store; that conclusion only becomes available once you've actually done the math instead of assuming "a lot of data" requires a specific exotic technology. Third, 117 peak writes/second and 11,700 peak reads/second are both comfortably within what a well-designed single-region deployment with a handful of horizontally-scaled application instances and a cache in front of the database can serve — this estimate tells you not to over-architect for a scale this specific product doesn't actually have, which is exactly as valuable a conclusion as knowing when you do need heavier infrastructure.

6.3 Latency vs. Throughput, Made Precise

These two terms get conflated constantly, and the confusion produces real design mistakes. Latency is how long a single request takes, end to end, from the moment it's sent to the moment its response is received — measured in time (milliseconds). Throughput is how many requests a system can process per unit of time — measured in a rate (requests per second). A system can have high latency and high throughput simultaneously (each request takes a while, but the system processes enormous numbers of them in parallel — think of a batch data pipeline), and a system can have low latency but low throughput (each individual request is lightning fast, but the system can only handle a handful at a time due to limited parallelism).

Little's Law, borrowed from queueing theory, gives the precise relationship connecting them through a third quantity, concurrency (how many requests are in flight at once): 

Concurrency (L) = Throughput (λ) x Latency (W)

Rearranged, the relationship a backend engineer uses most often:
Throughput (λ) = Concurrency (L) / Latency (W)

The practical reading: for a fixed amount of available concurrency (a fixed pool of worker threads, a fixed number of database connections), throughput and latency are directly linked — if you cannot add more concurrency, the only way to raise throughput is to reduce latency per request, and if latency per request increases (a slower downstream dependency, for example), throughput necessarily falls proportionally unless concurrency is increased to compensate. This is precisely why a single slow dependency deep in a request path can silently cap an entire system's throughput, and why "just add more threads/connections" only helps up to the point where some other resource (CPU, a downstream database's own connection limit) becomes the new bottleneck.

7.3 Stateful Routing (Broken) vs. Stateless Routing (Works)

 STATEFUL (session lives only in one instance's memory) — BROKEN:

   Request 1 ──► LB ──► App Instance A  (session created in A's local memory)
   Request 2 ──► LB ──► App Instance B  (B has never heard of this session!)
                                          ✗ user appears logged out / loses state

 STATELESS (session externalized to a shared store) — WORKS:

   Request 1 ──► LB ──► App Instance A ──► reads/writes session in Redis
   Request 2 ──► LB ──► App Instance B ──► reads/writes SAME session in Redis
                                          ✓ either instance can serve either request

The third diagram is the single most important picture in this chapter. Every later chapter on load balancing, caching, and database scaling assumes the application tier has already solved the stateful-routing problem by externalizing its state — if it hasn't, no amount of load balancer sophistication fixes the underlying bug.

         ▼                                           ▼
 ┌───────────────────────────────┐          ┌───────────────────────┐
 │  Database                      │ — Ch. 5,6 │  Background Workers    │
 │  (primary + read replicas,      │          │  (async processing)    │
 │   sharded at scale)             │          └───────────────────────┘
 └───────────────────────────────┘

Three things are worth internalizing about this diagram before moving on, each a preview of a judgment call later chapters teach in full depth. First, not every box belongs in every design — a message queue only earns its place once you've identified genuinely asynchronous work (an email notification, a thumbnail generation job) that the user-facing request path doesn't need to wait on; adding one reflexively, without that justification, is exactly the over-engineering Section 11 warns against. Second, the cache and the database are drawn as separate boxes deliberately: the cache is an optimization, not the system of record — if it's lost entirely, the system must still be correct (just slower) by falling back to the database, never silently wrong. Third, this entire diagram sits on top of the stateless application tier from Section 4.4 — remove that one property and the autoscaling group, the load balancer's freedom to route anywhere, and the ability to lose an instance without losing data all stop being true simultaneously.

9. JAVA / SPRING PERSPECTIVE

As a Java/Spring Boot engineer, the theory in this chapter is not abstract — it shows up directly in how you structure a service, and getting it wrong produces bugs that only appear once you deploy more than one instance, which is exactly why they're easy to miss in local development.

9.1 Designing a Genuinely Stateless Spring Boot Service

The most common violation of statelessness in a Spring Boot application is relying on the default in-memory HttpSession implementation, which stores session data in the memory of whichever instance first created it — invisible to every other instance behind the load balancer, exactly as diagrammed in Section 7.3. The standard fix is Spring Session backed by Redis, which transparently replaces the in-memory session store with a shared, external one, so that any instance can read any user's session: 

// build.gradle / pom.xml: spring-session-data-redis + spring-boot-starter-data-redis

@Configuration
@EnableRedisHttpSession   // externalizes HttpSession storage to Redis
public class SessionConfig {
    @Bean
    public LettuceConnectionFactory connectionFactory() {
        return new LettuceConnectionFactory(
            new RedisStandaloneConfiguration("redis-host", 6379));
    }
}

// From here on, ordinary session code needs NO changes:
@RestController
public class CartController {

    @PostMapping("/cart/items")
    public ResponseEntity<Void> addItem(HttpSession session, @RequestBody Item item) {
        // session is transparently backed by Redis now — ANY instance
        // behind the load balancer can read this on the next request.
        session.setAttribute("cart", item);
        return ResponseEntity.ok().build();
    }
}

The same discipline applies beyond HTTP sessions: uploaded files belong in object storage (e.g., S3), not the local filesystem of one instance; any in-memory cache a developer is tempted to add with a plain HashMap field on a @Service bean needs to either be acceptable as purely instance-local (a small, non-critical optimization) or externalized to Redis if correctness depends on every instance seeing the same value.

9.2 Capacity Signals: Spring Boot Actuator

The back-of-the-envelope estimates from Section 6 are a starting point, not a substitute for real measurement once a service is running. Spring Boot Actuator exposes exactly the operational signals needed to validate — and correct — those estimates in production: /actuator/health for liveness/readiness (what a load balancer or Kubernetes uses to decide whether to route traffic to an instance at all), and /actuator/metrics for request latency distributions, JVM memory and thread-pool utilization, and database connection-pool saturation. Watching these metrics is how an engineer discovers, for instance, that the "average QPS" estimate from a clarifying conversation was reasonable but the measured p99 latency during actual peak traffic reveals a specific downstream dependency (frequently a database connection pool sized too small for the real concurrency level) as the true bottleneck — turning Section 6's theoretical estimation into an empirically-grounded, correctable capacity plan.

9.3 Horizontal Scaling in Practice

Once a Spring Boot service is genuinely stateless, horizontal scaling becomes an infrastructure/deployment concern rather than an application-code concern: running it behind a cloud load balancer with an autoscaling group, or inside Kubernetes with a Deployment of multiple replicas and a HorizontalPodAutoscaler reacting to CPU or custom metrics, both work cleanly specifically because no instance holds state the next request needs that another instance can't also see. This is the concrete, deployable payoff of the theory in Section 4.4: statelessness is not an academic property, it's the specific precondition that makes "just add more pods/instances" an actually correct scaling strategy rather than one that silently drops user state under load.

10. BEST PRACTICES

  • Clarify before you design. Spend real time (not a token single question) establishing scope, scale, and non-functional requirements before drawing any architecture — every later step depends on these answers, and getting them wrong invalidates everything built on top.
  • Quantify before you choose a technology. Run the back-of-the-envelope math from Section 6 before reaching for a specific database, cache, or queue — "this needs to scale" is not a justification; "this needs to serve 12,000 reads/second with sub-100ms p99 latency" is.
  • Default to statelessness in the application tier. Treat any in-memory state that must survive across requests or outlive a single instance as a bug to fix, not a detail to defer — it is far cheaper to design for statelessness from the start than to retrofit it once an application tier has quietly grown dependent on local state.
  • Start simple, then add complexity in response to a named bottleneck. Design the simplest architecture that satisfies the stated requirements first; introduce a cache, a queue, sharding, or additional replicas only once you can point to the specific number (from Section 6, or from real production metrics per Section 9.2) that justifies it.
  • State trade-offs explicitly, and defend the side you picked. Every meaningful design decision trades one property for another (consistency for availability, latency for throughput, cost for redundancy) — naming the trade-off and justifying your choice for this problem's actual requirements is worth more, to an interviewer or a design-review panel, than presenting a single option as though no alternative existed.

11. COMMON MISTAKES

  • Jumping straight to a detailed architecture diagram. Skipping the scoping conversation entirely and immediately drawing boxes for a specific database, cache, and message queue almost always means solving the wrong version of the problem, discovered only after significant time has been spent on it.
  • Treating "we'll use a NoSQL database" (or any specific technology) as a design decision by itself. Naming a technology without the quantified reasoning (data model, access patterns, scale, consistency needs) that led to it reads as pattern-matching from a checklist, not engineering judgment — and it usually falls apart the moment an interviewer asks "why not a relational database here?"
  • Over-engineering for a scale nobody asked for. Introducing sharding, multi-region replication, or a message queue for a system whose own back-of-the-envelope numbers (Section 6) show comfortably low QPS and data volume signals that complexity is being added for its own sake, not in response to an actual constraint — this is read as a lack of judgment, not thoroughness.
  • Presenting the first design as the only possible one. Failing to name any trade-off, alternative considered, or weak point in your own design suggests either that you don't see the trade-offs or that you're not comfortable discussing them — both are weaker signals than naming a trade-off and defending your choice.
  • Ignoring non-functional requirements until asked. Designing purely for the functional "what it does" while leaving latency, availability, and consistency entirely unaddressed until the interviewer specifically probes for them suggests those properties weren't part of your design thinking from the start, when they should have shaped it from the first sketch.
  • Assuming an application instance is permanent. Designing as though a given server will run forever, rather than planning for routine instance replacement (deploys, autoscaling, crashes), reproduces exactly the stateful-routing failure from Section 7.3 the first time it's deployed behind a real load balancer or orchestrator.

12. PERFORMANCE CONSIDERATIONS

Performance in a distributed system is rarely a single number — it's a distribution, and the shape of that distribution matters as much as its average.

  • Average latency hides the experience that matters. A system reporting "50ms average latency" can still have a meaningful fraction of requests taking 2 full seconds — production systems are almost always evaluated on tail latency (p95, p99, p99.9 — the latency below which 95%, 99%, or 99.9% of requests complete), because for a user-facing system, the slow outliers are disproportionately visible and disproportionately damaging to perceived reliability.
  • Horizontal scaling raises throughput ceiling, not necessarily per-request latency. Adding more stateless instances behind a load balancer lets the system handle more total requests per second (Little's Law, Section 6.3, with more concurrency available), but it does nothing by itself to make any single request faster — reducing per-request latency requires attacking the request's own critical path (a slow query, a slow downstream call, excessive serialization overhead), independent of how many instances are running.
  • Caching trades a small amount of staleness risk for a large reduction in load on slower backing systems. A well-placed cache (full treatment in Chapter 4) can take a database from being the throughput bottleneck to barely being touched for the hottest data — but every cache introduces the question of how and when it becomes stale, which has to be answered deliberately, not left implicit.
  • Estimate before you optimize. The arithmetic in Section 6 exists precisely to tell you, before writing or redesigning any code, whether a given component is anywhere close to its load limit — optimizing a component that back-of-the-envelope math shows is nowhere near its ceiling is wasted engineering effort that a quick calculation would have prevented.

13. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

Security is not a separate concern bolted onto a system design after the architecture is settled — several of the structural choices this chapter covers have direct, foundational security implications that later chapters build on.

  • Externalized session state must itself be protected. Moving session data to Redis (Section 9.1) solves the statelessness problem, but it creates a new asset worth protecting: if that shared store is reachable by anything other than the application tier, or session tokens are predictable or long-lived without rotation, an attacker who can read or guess a session can impersonate a user across every stateless instance equally — externalizing state does not externalize the responsibility to secure it.
  • The load balancer and edge are the natural place for rate limiting. Because every request passes through it, the load balancer/API gateway layer (Chapter 3) is the cheapest place to apply coarse-grained protection against abusive traffic (a flood of requests from one source) before it ever reaches the application tier — full rate limiting and backpressure design gets its own dedicated treatment in Chapter 8, but the placement decision (as early in the request path as possible) is already implied by the architecture in Section 8.
  • Horizontal scaling expands the attack surface, not just the capacity. Every additional instance is an additional thing that must be patched, configured consistently, and granted only the access it needs — a design that scales out application instances without equally scaling out consistent configuration management and least-privilege access to the database and cache has scaled its vulnerabilities along with its capacity.
  • Encrypt data in transit between every tier, not just at the client-facing edge. It's a common and costly mistake to terminate TLS at the load balancer and assume internal traffic between the application tier, cache, and database is automatically safe because it's "inside the network" — internal traffic should be encrypted and authenticated between services as a default, not an afterthought reserved for traffic that crosses the public internet.

14. INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

JUNIORQ1. An interviewer tells you your design was "technically correct" but still scores it weakly. What were they actually grading, and what gap does that feedback usually point to?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

"Technically correct" means the boxes and arrows would work, but the score reflects the reasoning that got you there: whether you clarified scope and requirements instead of designing against assumed ones, whether you backed component choices with real estimation instead of pattern-matching a familiar diagram, whether you named the trade-offs your design made instead of presenting one option as the only one, and whether you surfaced your own design's weak points unprompted. A technically-correct design with none of that visible reads as memorized, not reasoned — and that's almost always the gap behind a weak score despite a working architecture.

JUNIORQ2. Two products both reduce to "users can post a short message" — one is Twitter, one is an internal team-status tool. Why might their architectures end up nothing alike despite identical functional requirements?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Because the functional requirement never determined the architecture — the non-functional requirements did. Twitter needs to serve hundreds of thousands of reads per second with sub-second latency and tolerate eventual consistency on a feed; the internal tool can run on one server with a single relational database and strict consistency, because its scale, latency target, and consistency bar are all orders of magnitude smaller. If you only scope the functional requirement, you have no basis to pick between those two architectures — the non-functional axis (scale, latency, consistency) is what actually does the choosing, which is why getting it wrong in a scoping conversation sinks the rest of the design regardless of how clean the diagram looks.

JUNIORQ3. You're handed "design a URL shortener" with nothing else. You have maybe two minutes before you need to start designing. What do you actually ask, and — just as important — what do you deliberately skip asking?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Ask the handful of questions that would actually change the design: rough scale (DAU or requests/day), the read/write ratio, whether redirects need to be instant or can tolerate a cache miss, retention period, and whether custom aliases or analytics are in scope. Skip questions whose answer wouldn't move your architecture at all — exact character set for short codes, specific UI copy, whether links can be edited after creation — those are implementation detail, not scoping, and burning clarifying-question time on them is itself a signal to the interviewer that you can't tell load-bearing questions from trivia. The discipline isn't "ask lots of questions," it's recovering exactly the non-functional requirements that would change your answer and stating everything else as an explicit assumption instead.

JUNIORQ4. You've got budget for either one much bigger box or three more identical boxes behind a load balancer for a CPU-bound service. What actually decides which way you go, beyond the reflex "horizontal is more scalable"?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

First check whether the service can even go horizontal today — if it holds any state locally (in-memory sessions, local-disk uploads), horizontal scaling isn't actually available to you until that's fixed, which can make "buy a bigger box" the only real short-term option even though it's the worse long-term one. If the service is already stateless, weigh the cost curve (a bigger box costs non-linearly more per unit of capacity; three more commodity boxes cost roughly linearly more), the ceiling (vertical scaling tops out at the largest machine money buys; horizontal doesn't), and the failure domain (one bigger box is still one single point of failure; three boxes survive one of them dying). In practice this is rarely binary — most systems size individual machines reasonably and layer horizontal scaling on top for both growth and redundancy, so the real decision is "is statelessness solved yet," not "which scaling style do I prefer."

JUNIORQ5. A teammate says "our service is basically stateless — we just keep a local HashMap cache of user preferences on each instance for speed." Is that still stateless? What breaks if it isn't?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

It depends entirely on what correctness requires. If that HashMap is a pure, re-derivable optimization — losing it just costs a cheap re-fetch from the real source of truth, and every instance would eventually converge on the same value — it's acceptable local state and doesn't violate statelessness in any way that matters. But if two instances can disagree about a user's preferences because one has a stale or different local copy and something downstream depends on that agreement, it's not stateless, it's a hidden consistency bug waiting for a load balancer to route two requests from the same user to two different instances. The test isn't "is there any local memory at all," it's "does correctness depend on every instance seeing the same value" — if yes, externalize it; if it's a disposable, re-derivable cache, local is fine.

JUNIORQ6. You "fix" a stateful session bug by turning on sticky sessions at the load balancer instead of externalizing the session. What have you actually done to your system's scalability and failure characteristics?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

You haven't fixed the underlying bug — you've reintroduced the exact single-point-of-failure problem horizontal scaling was supposed to remove. Now a given user is pinned to one instance, so that instance's crash or restart loses that user's session anyway, load can't be rebalanced evenly across instances since some carry more "stuck" long-lived sessions than others, and you can't freely drain and replace an instance (for a deploy or autoscaling event) without actually disrupting the users pinned to it. It masks the symptom in the common case while leaving the real failure mode — state that only one instance knows about — fully intact, which is why sticky sessions are a workaround, not a substitute for externalizing the state itself.

JUNIORQ7. Your PM tells you "the app has 10 million daily active users." That's not a QPS number. Walk through turning it into one, and say which assumption in that conversion is most likely to be wrong in practice.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Multiply DAU by an assumed average number of actions per user per day, divide by seconds in a day (≈86,400, or ≈100,000 for fast mental math) to get average QPS, then apply a peak factor (commonly 2x-3x) since traffic isn't uniform across the day. The weakest link in that chain is almost always "actions per user per day" — it's the one number you're guessing rather than measuring, and it's usually wildly different across user segments (a power user and a dormant account are not "an average user"), so it's worth stating that assumption out loud and flagging it as the first thing you'd validate against real analytics once the system is live, rather than treating the resulting QPS figure as more precise than the guess underneath it.

JUNIORQ8. A system can have high throughput and high latency at the same time. Give a case where that combination is perfectly fine, and one where it would be a real production problem.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Fine: a nightly batch pipeline that processes a billion records — each record's individual processing latency doesn't matter because nothing is waiting synchronously on it, only the total volume processed per hour matters. A real problem: a checkout API that also has high throughput (handling thousands of concurrent carts) but 3-second-per-request latency — nobody is blocked waiting on the batch pipeline, but every user is blocked waiting on checkout, so the same latency number that's a non-issue in one system is a user-facing outage-grade problem in the other. The two metrics are independent enough that "the system handles a lot of load" tells you nothing about whether any individual user is having a bad time.

JUNIORQ9. You're 90 seconds into a system design interview and already sketching a load balancer and three app servers talking to a specific NoSQL database. What have you actually skipped, and how does an interviewer's next question expose it?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

You've skipped scoping and estimation, and it shows the instant the interviewer asks "why that database, specifically, over a relational one?" — you have no scale number, no access-pattern reasoning, and no consistency requirement to point to, because you never established them before drawing the diagram. At that point you're forced to retroactively invent justification for a choice you already committed to, which reads as rationalizing a guess rather than reasoning toward an answer — and it often means backing up to redo the scoping you skipped, which costs far more interview time than spending two minutes on it up front would have.

JUNIORQ10. You put a load balancer in front of three app instances but never addressed whether the app is stateless. What happens the moment real traffic hits it, and why doesn't the load balancer itself prevent the problem?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

The load balancer has no concept of "this user's session lives on instance A" — it distributes requests by its own algorithm (round-robin, least-connections) with zero awareness of what state any instance is holding locally. So a user's second request lands on instance B, which has never heard of their session, and the user appears logged out or loses in-progress state — intermittently, depending on routing, which makes it a maddening bug to reproduce. The load balancer's job is purely to distribute requests; it was never responsible for making the application tier stateless, and assuming it would "handle" that is exactly the gap that produces this class of bug the first time more than one instance is actually running.

JUNIORQ11. A payment balance and a social-media like-count are both, functionally, "show a number." Why might you pick two completely different databases for them?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Because the non-functional requirement — how wrong can this number be allowed to look, and for how long — is completely different even though the functional shape is identical. A payment balance needs strong consistency: a stale read could mean a user acts on money they don't actually have, so you want a database with real transactional guarantees even at some cost to availability or latency. A like-count can tolerate a user seeing a slightly stale number for a brief window with no real harm, so you can trade strict consistency for higher availability and throughput. Same feature on paper, opposite answer on the one requirement that actually drives the database choice.

JUNIORQ12. You estimate average QPS at 1,000 and provision exactly 1,000 QPS of capacity. What happens the first time real traffic spikes, and what number should you have designed against instead?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

The system gets overwhelmed the moment traffic exceeds the average — which it will, routinely, since real traffic is never uniform across the day and concentrates around predictable peaks (morning login rush, a marketing push, a viral moment). You should have designed against peak QPS, typically average QPS multiplied by a peak factor of roughly 2x-3x, because capacity planning that targets the average is, by construction, planning to be under capacity for a large fraction of real traffic patterns — the average was never the number that was going to page you at 3 a.m.

JUNIORQ13. An interviewer asks "why NoSQL here?" and the honest answer in your head is "because it scales." What's actually wrong with that answer, even if the technology choice turns out to be the right one?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

It's a conclusion with the reasoning deleted — "scales" isn't a property specific to NoSQL (a well-sharded relational database scales too), so the answer doesn't actually explain why this data model and access pattern fit this technology better than the alternative. A defensible answer names the specific thing that makes the fit real: a key-value access pattern with no need for multi-row transactions, at a write volume a single relational instance can't comfortably serve, for example — something concrete enough that "why not Postgres, sharded?" has an actual answer instead of forcing you back to "because it scales" a second time.

MIDQ14. Walk through the ordered steps you'd follow to approach an open-ended system design problem.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Clarify scope and requirements first; estimate scale (QPS, storage, bandwidth) using those requirements; optionally sketch the core API; design the high-level architecture for the core use cases, favoring the simplest design that satisfies the stated requirements; deep-dive on the one or two components the interviewer steers toward; and finally, proactively identify bottlenecks and trade-offs in your own design. Each step supplies an input the next step needs, which is why skipping or reordering them tends to produce a design built on unstated or unvalidated assumptions.

MIDQ15. Walk through a back-of-the-envelope QPS estimate for a hypothetical service with 50 million daily active users, each performing 5 actions per day.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Total daily actions = 50,000,000 x 5 = 250,000,000. Dividing by seconds per day (≈86,400, or ≈100,000 for faster mental math) gives roughly 2,500-2,900 average QPS. Applying a peak factor of 3x gives a peak estimate of roughly 7,500-8,700 QPS — the number that should actually drive capacity planning, not the average.

MIDQ16. Explain Little's Law and what it tells you about the relationship between latency, throughput, and concurrency.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Little's Law states that concurrency (requests in flight) equals throughput multiplied by latency: L = λ × W. Rearranged, throughput = concurrency / latency. For a fixed amount of available concurrency (a fixed thread pool or connection pool), raising throughput requires either reducing latency per request or increasing concurrency — and if latency increases without a compensating increase in concurrency, throughput necessarily falls, which is exactly why one slow downstream dependency can silently cap an entire system's throughput.

MIDQ17. Why is tail latency (p99) typically a more important metric than average latency for a production system?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

An average can look healthy while a meaningful fraction of requests (the tail) are much slower — a system reporting 50ms average latency could still have 1% of requests taking several seconds. For user-facing systems, slow outliers are disproportionately visible and damaging to perceived reliability, and at scale even a small percentage of slow requests represents a large absolute number of bad experiences, which is why p95/p99/p99.9 are the metrics production systems are actually held to.

MIDQ18. How would you decide whether a given design needs a message queue at all?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Identify whether there's genuinely asynchronous work in the flow — processing that doesn't need to complete before a response is returned to the user, such as sending a notification email or generating a thumbnail. If every piece of work in the request path is required for the response itself, a queue adds operational complexity (another component to run, monitor, and handle failure for) without a corresponding benefit — it should be introduced in response to an identified decoupling need, not added by default because "production systems use queues."

MIDQ19. What specific bug does session externalization (e.g., Spring Session with Redis) fix, mechanically?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

With the default in-memory HttpSession, session data lives only in the memory of the instance that created it. A load balancer routing that user's next request to a different instance finds an instance with no knowledge of the session, causing the user to appear logged out or lose in-progress state. Externalizing the session to a shared store like Redis means any instance can read the same session data, making it safe for the load balancer to route freely across instances without this failure.

MIDQ20. Describe a scenario where a system should intentionally prioritize throughput over latency, or vice versa.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A nightly batch pipeline computing analytics aggregates over a day's data should prioritize throughput — processing the largest possible volume of records per unit time matters far more than any single record's processing latency, since nothing is waiting synchronously on an individual result. By contrast, an interactive user-facing search-as-you-type feature should prioritize latency even at some cost to raw throughput, since a slow individual response is directly felt by the user regardless of how many total queries the system can handle per second.

MIDQ21. What's the risk of designing a system's architecture before completing a back-of-the-envelope estimation?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Without quantified scale, there's no basis for choosing between a simple design and a more complex, scaled-out one — you either guess conservatively and risk under-building for real load, or guess defensively and over-engineer for a scale the system doesn't actually have, both of which an interviewer or a cost-conscious engineering organization will rightly question once the real numbers surface.

MIDQ22. Why might a system intentionally choose vertical scaling over horizontal scaling, even today?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Horizontal scaling adds real complexity — a load balancer, the requirement for statelessness, and distributed-systems failure modes that don't exist on one machine. For a system whose scale is modest and unlikely to grow dramatically (an internal tool, an early-stage product validating demand), vertical scaling can be the pragmatically correct choice: simpler to build, operate, and reason about, with the option to move to horizontal scaling later once actual growth justifies the added complexity.

MIDQ23. How does the read/write ratio of a system influence architectural decisions?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

A read-heavy system (most social media feeds, content sites) benefits enormously from caching and read replicas, since the bulk of load can be absorbed before it reaches the primary data store. A write-heavy system (logging, metrics ingestion, IoT telemetry) gets comparatively little benefit from read caching and instead needs the write path itself optimized — batching, partitioning writes across shards, or a write-optimized storage engine — making the read/write ratio one of the first numbers worth establishing during scoping.

MIDQ24. What does "start simple, add complexity in response to a bottleneck" mean in practice, and why is it a best practice rather than just a time-saving shortcut?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

It means designing the simplest architecture that satisfies the stated requirements first, and only introducing a cache, a queue, sharding, or additional replicas once a specific, named bottleneck justifies it — rather than preemptively building for problems that may never materialize. It's a best practice, not just a shortcut, because every added component is also added operational surface area (something to deploy, monitor, and debug), and unnecessary complexity has a real ongoing cost even when it isn't actively causing a bug.

MIDQ25. What's a practical mechanism for discovering whether a back-of-the-envelope capacity estimate was accurate, once a system is live?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Real production metrics — request latency distributions, throughput, resource utilization (CPU, memory, connection-pool saturation) — collected via an observability layer such as Spring Boot Actuator's metrics endpoint (or an equivalent in another stack), compared against the original estimate. A consistent gap between the estimate and measured reality (e.g., a database connection pool saturating well below the estimated peak QPS) pinpoints exactly which assumption in the original estimation was wrong, turning the theoretical math into an empirically corrected capacity plan.

MIDQ26. Why is object storage (e.g., S3) generally preferred over local disk for file uploads in a horizontally-scaled service?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A file saved to one instance's local disk is invisible to every other instance behind the load balancer, exactly as a session held only in local memory would be — a later request (perhaps to retrieve that file) routed to a different instance would fail to find it. Object storage is shared and reachable by every instance identically, so it fits the same statelessness requirement that governs session data, caches, and any other data that must outlive or be visible beyond a single request-handling instance.

MIDQ27. What does it mean to say a cache is "an optimization, not the system of record"?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

It means the database (or another durable store) holds the authoritative, correct data, and the cache holds a copy for speed — if the cache is lost entirely (cleared, crashed, evicted), the system must still produce correct results, just more slowly, by falling back to the database. A design where losing the cache causes incorrect results, not just slower ones, has mistakenly made the cache part of the system of record, which is a design defect that full treatment in the caching chapter addresses directly.

MIDQ28. How would you justify, with numbers, whether a system needs database sharding at launch?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Run the storage and write-QPS estimates from Section 6 against what a single well-provisioned database instance can realistically handle (commonly on the order of low thousands of writes/second and multiple terabytes of storage for a modern managed relational database, though exact figures depend on the specific engine and hardware). If the estimated load is comfortably within that range, sharding at launch adds operational complexity (application-level routing logic, harder cross-shard queries, rebalancing concerns) without a load-bearing justification — the better plan is to launch unsharded and introduce sharding once real growth, measured against this same math, actually approaches the single-instance ceiling.

MIDQ29. What's the difference between designing for a known, bounded scale versus designing for "web scale" by default?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Designing for a known, bounded scale means the architecture's complexity is proportional to actual, quantified requirements — a system serving a few thousand internal users doesn't need the same infrastructure as one serving hundreds of millions, even though both could theoretically grow. Designing for "web scale" by default, without evidence the system will ever reach that scale, means paying the ongoing cost (engineering time, operational complexity, infrastructure spend) of capabilities the system doesn't need yet and may never need — the discipline this chapter teaches is to let estimation, not reflexive ambition, set the bar.

MIDQ30. In the stateless vs. stateful discussion, what's an example of state that is acceptable to keep purely local to one instance?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium

A small, non-critical, instance-local cache of data that's also derivable or re-fetchable from a shared source — for example, a brief in-process cache of a rarely-changing configuration value, where losing it on instance restart or routing to a different instance only costs a cheap re-fetch, not incorrect behavior. The dividing line is whether correctness depends on every instance seeing the same value: if yes, it must be externalized; if a purely local, re-derivable optimization, local state is acceptable.

MIDQ31. How does horizontal scaling affect a system's resilience to a single instance failing, compared to vertical scaling?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

With vertical scaling, there's exactly one instance — if it fails, the entire system is down, a hard single point of failure. With horizontal scaling and a properly stateless application tier, losing one instance out of many means the load balancer simply routes traffic to the remaining healthy instances, with no single instance's failure capable of taking down the whole system — this redundancy is a direct, valuable side effect of horizontal scaling, not just a capacity benefit.

MIDQ32. What's a concrete way bandwidth estimation (Section 6) could change a design decision?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

If peak bandwidth estimates for serving media (images, video) are large relative to what a single-region origin server or database can comfortably push, that's a direct, quantified justification for introducing a CDN to serve that traffic from edge locations closer to users — rather than reaching for a CDN as a default, the bandwidth math gives a concrete threshold for when it becomes necessary.

SENIORQ33. A candidate proposes sharding the database immediately, before any scale estimation. How do you push back, and what would change your mind?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Push back by asking for the numbers that justify it: what's the estimated write QPS and total data volume, and what can a single well-provisioned instance of the proposed database actually handle? Sharding introduces real, ongoing complexity — application-level routing, harder cross-shard transactions and queries, rebalancing work as data grows unevenly across shards — that is only worth paying once a single instance's throughput or storage ceiling is genuinely approached. What would change my mind: a concrete estimate (from Section 6's math, or from measured production data) showing write throughput or storage requirements that a single instance, even generously provisioned, could not sustain — at which point sharding stops being premature and becomes the justified next step.

Interview Gold Point: Explicitly separate "sharding is wrong" from "sharding without justification is wrong" — this shows the interviewer you're not rejecting a valid future tool, you're rejecting introducing it before the evidence calls for it, which is a more senior distinction than a blanket objection.
SENIORQ34. How would you reason about the trade-off between strong consistency and availability for a specific feature, using a concrete example?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Take a payment balance versus a "like" count as contrasting examples. A payment balance needs strong consistency — a user (or a downstream service) acting on a stale balance could cause real financial harm, so the design should prefer correctness even at the cost of occasionally rejecting a request during a partition or coordination delay, which is explored formally via the CAP theorem in the next chapter. A "like" count, by contrast, can tolerate a user seeing a slightly stale number for a brief window with no meaningful harm, so the design should prefer availability and low latency, accepting eventual consistency, because the cost of a wrong-but-recoverable count is far lower than the cost of an unavailable feature. The senior-level point is that this decision is made per-feature, based on the actual cost of being wrong, not applied uniformly across an entire system.

SENIORQ35. Your back-of-the-envelope estimate and your production metrics disagree significantly once the system is live. Walk through how you'd investigate the discrepancy.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

First, check whether the underlying assumption that drove the estimate was wrong — actual daily active users, actions per user, or the read/write ratio may differ materially from what was assumed during scoping, and real usage telemetry (not the original guess) should replace that assumption going forward. Second, check whether the peak factor used was realistic — actual traffic patterns (time-of-day concentration, viral spikes) can exceed a generic 2x-3x multiplier for specific products, and historical traffic data, once available, should replace the generic assumption. Third, check whether the bottleneck is where the estimate assumed it would be at all — a mismatch sometimes reveals that a downstream dependency (a third-party API, a database index that isn't being used as expected) is the actual limiting factor, not the component the original estimate focused on. The discipline is to treat the original estimate as a hypothesis to be validated against real data, not a fixed truth, and to update the mental model — and the architecture, if warranted — based on what the discrepancy actually reveals.

Interview Gold Point: Explicitly say that a back-of-the-envelope estimate is a hypothesis, not a commitment — interviewers and senior reviewers respond well to hearing "I'd expect to revisit this number once real traffic data exists" because it shows you understand estimation as an ongoing discipline, not a one-time gate you pass and forget.
SENIORQ36. How would you design the scoping conversation differently for a 45-minute interview versus a real multi-week system design effort at work?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

In a 45-minute interview, scoping has to be fast and deliberately narrow — pick two or three core use cases, state explicit assumptions for anything that would take too long to fully resolve, and move forward, because the interview is testing whether you can make reasonable, defensible decisions under time pressure, not whether you can exhaustively map every edge case. In a real multi-week effort, scoping can and should be far more thorough — actual stakeholder interviews, review of existing analytics for real traffic patterns rather than estimated ones, and iterative validation of assumptions with the people who will own the system in production — because the cost of a wrong assumption compounds across weeks of implementation rather than being contained to a single interview's hypothetical design.

SENIORQ37. A junior engineer argues that since the system currently runs fine on one server, there's no need to design it to be stateless. How do you respond?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

I'd agree that a single-server deployment doesn't need statelessness to function correctly today — the risk isn't in the present, it's in how expensive the fix becomes later. Retrofitting statelessness onto a system that has quietly accumulated local, instance-specific state (in-memory caches callers depend on, sessions, local file writes) across many code paths is significantly more expensive and error-prone than designing for it from the start, because by the time horizontal scaling is actually needed (often under real production pressure, not at a convenient moment), every one of those dependencies has to be found and fixed under time pressure. The pragmatic middle ground: it's reasonable to defer the operational complexity of running multiple instances until it's needed, but it's not reasonable to defer the discipline of not introducing local-only state in new code, since that discipline costs almost nothing to maintain and a great deal to retrofit.

SENIORQ38. How would you explain to a product manager why "just add more servers" doesn't automatically fix a slow system?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Adding more servers (horizontal scaling) increases how many requests the system can handle concurrently — throughput — but it does nothing to make any individual request faster, because latency is determined by that request's own critical path (a slow query, a slow external call, serialization overhead), independent of how many other requests are running in parallel elsewhere. If users are complaining that pages feel slow (a latency complaint), adding servers addresses a capacity problem the users may not actually be experiencing, and the real fix requires identifying and reducing the specific slow step in the request's own path — this distinction, grounded in Little's Law, is worth walking a non-engineering stakeholder through explicitly before committing budget to additional infrastructure that won't solve their actual complaint.

Interview Gold Point: Bring Little's Law into this answer by name and show the arithmetic briefly — throughput = concurrency / latency — because tying a stakeholder-facing explanation back to a precise, nameable relationship, rather than just intuition, is exactly the kind of grounding that separates a senior answer from a merely plausible-sounding one.
SENIORQ39. When would you recommend starting with a monolith rather than microservices for a new system, given everything this chapter covers?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

When the team is small, the domain boundaries aren't yet well understood (which is common for a genuinely new product), and the estimated scale doesn't demand independent scaling of different components. A monolith, kept internally well-organized, is simpler to develop, deploy, and reason about than a distributed system, and it defers the real cost of microservices — network calls where function calls used to be, distributed tracing, independent deployment coordination, and the full weight of distributed-systems failure modes this track covers — until there's an actual, evidenced need (either a scaling bottleneck in one specific component, or an organizational need to let separate teams deploy independently) that justifies paying it. Scoping and estimation, again, are what turn "when" from a matter of opinion into a decision backed by a specific, named trigger.

SENIORQ40. How would you identify, proactively, the weakest point in a design you just finished presenting?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Walk the data flow end to end and ask, at each component, "what happens if this fails, or if load here grows 10x beyond what I estimated?" Specifically: is there a single instance anywhere that isn't horizontally scaled (a classic overlooked single point of failure is a database primary that everything else correctly scales around but that itself has no failover plan); is there a component whose capacity estimate was closest to its assumed ceiling, making it the first thing to break under unexpectedly higher real traffic; and is there a trade-off (eventual consistency, a cache that could serve stale data) whose consequences haven't been fully reasoned through for every use case the design covers. Naming this unprompted, rather than waiting for the interviewer to find it, is a stronger signal of seniority than a design that appears flawless because the gaps were never surfaced.

Interview Gold Point: Say the weak point out loud even if the interviewer never asks for one — volunteering "if I had more time, the part I'd want to stress-test further is X" before being prompted reads as genuine self-awareness, while only producing it under direct questioning reads as something you were hoping wouldn't come up.
SENIORQ41. Explain how you'd use Spring Boot Actuator metrics to decide whether a performance problem is a latency problem or a throughput problem.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Check the latency distribution metrics (average and percentiles) for the affected endpoint first — if p99 latency is elevated even under normal, non-peak load, that points to a latency problem in the request's own critical path (a slow query, an external call, excessive serialization), independent of overall load. If latency per request looks normal under light load but degrades specifically as concurrent request volume rises, and thread-pool or database-connection-pool metrics show saturation at that same point, that points to a throughput/concurrency ceiling (Little's Law) rather than a problem with any individual request's logic — the fix in that case is increasing available concurrency (pool sizes, more instances) rather than optimizing a query that was never actually slow in isolation.

SENIORQ42. A stakeholder asks for a system to be designed to handle "whatever scale we might need in the future." How do you respond as the engineer scoping the work?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

I'd push for specific numbers, or at minimum a specific time horizon and growth assumption, because "whatever scale we might need" isn't an engineering requirement — it's not falsifiable, and a design can't be evaluated against it. I'd propose designing for a concrete, stated target (e.g., 10x current traffic over the next two years, a number the business can actually reason about and commit to), built on an architecture that can be extended further later without a full rewrite — favoring stateless services and clean boundaries between components specifically because they're cheap to scale out further if the unspecified "future scale" does arrive, while not paying the full cost of extreme scale infrastructure against a target nobody has actually committed to.

SENIORQ43. How would you approach estimating storage growth over time differently from estimating current storage needs?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Current storage needs are a snapshot: records that exist today multiplied by average record size. Growth over time requires an additional variable — the write rate and a retention/time horizon, as in Section 6.2's worked example (writes per day x retention period x record size) — and critically, a growth-rate assumption for the write rate itself, since a service growing its user base will not have a constant daily write volume over a multi-year retention window. The senior-level distinction is recognizing that a single current-state estimate badly understates true long-term storage needs for any growing product, which is why capacity planning for storage should explicitly model a growth curve, not just today's numbers extrapolated linearly.

SENIORQ44. Why can a system with plenty of spare CPU capacity on every instance still be throughput-limited?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Throughput can be capped by a resource other than CPU — a database connection pool sized too small for the actual concurrency, a downstream API with its own rate limit, or a lock/queue inside the application serializing access to a shared resource. Little's Law applies to whichever resource is actually the binding constraint, and CPU headroom on the application instances says nothing about whether that other resource has headroom too — this is exactly the kind of mismatch that Spring Boot Actuator's connection-pool and thread-pool metrics are designed to surface, and why "just add more CPU/instances" is an incomplete diagnosis without first identifying the true bottleneck resource.

SENIORQ45. How would you decide where, physically, to introduce a cache versus relying purely on database read replicas for a read-heavy system?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Read replicas scale read throughput for the full dataset and preserve relatively fresh, queryable data with the database's normal query capabilities intact, but every replica still pays the cost of a full query execution. A cache in front of the database serves hot, frequently-accessed data far faster and with less load on the database entirely, but only helps for data patterns with meaningful read locality (the same small set of keys requested repeatedly) and introduces the question of staleness and invalidation. The decision in practice is rarely either/or: read replicas handle the broad, less-hot query traffic a cache wouldn't efficiently cover, while a cache absorbs the smaller set of very hot keys (a trending post, a popular profile) that would otherwise hit every replica disproportionately hard — identifying which access pattern dominates, from real usage data or the read/write and locality assumptions from scoping, is what should drive this decision rather than defaulting to one or the other.

STAFFQ46. You're asked to review a team's system design doc before it goes to implementation. The doc jumps straight into a detailed microservices architecture with no stated scale estimates. What do you do?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Before commenting on the architecture itself, I'd ask the team to add an explicit scoping and estimation section — current and projected scale, read/write ratio, latency and consistency requirements — because without it, there's no way to evaluate whether the proposed complexity (multiple services, presumably with their own data stores and network calls between them) is actually justified by the system's real requirements, versus adopted as a default pattern. If the resulting numbers show genuinely modest scale and a single, cohesive domain, I'd push back on the microservices decomposition specifically, recommending a well-structured monolith instead, and reserve the review's deeper technical feedback (service boundaries, data ownership, failure handling) for after the team has demonstrated, with numbers, that the complexity is warranted. The staff-level responsibility here is protecting the team from committing significant implementation effort to an architecture whose cost may not be justified by anything the document has actually shown.

Interview Gold Point: Frame this as protecting engineering time and operational cost, not as a stylistic objection to microservices — staff-level pushback lands harder, and is received better, when it's tied to a concrete cost (ongoing on-call burden, slower feature velocity from cross-service coordination) rather than a general preference.
STAFFQ47. How would you build an organizational habit of "estimate before you architect" across multiple teams, rather than relying on individual engineers remembering to do it?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Individual discipline doesn't scale reliably across an organization, so I'd push for a lightweight, mandatory template section in whatever design-review or RFC process the organization already uses — a short, required "Scale & Requirements" section that must be filled in (current and projected QPS, data volume, latency/consistency targets, explicit assumptions where real data isn't yet available) before a design proceeds to detailed review. Making this a structural gate, rather than a norm individuals are expected to self-apply, is what actually changes behavior organization-wide, and it has the secondary benefit of giving reviewers a fast, consistent way to check whether a proposed architecture's complexity is proportionate to its stated requirements, rather than relitigating that question informally and inconsistently on every review.

STAFFQ48. A system that was correctly scoped and estimated two years ago is now struggling under load 20x higher than the original estimate. How do you think about whether this represents an original design failure or expected evolution?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

I'd separate two very different failure modes before assigning any judgment. If the original estimate was reasonable given the information available at the time, and 20x growth genuinely wasn't foreseeable or was explicitly out of scope for that design's stated time horizon, then struggling now isn't an original design failure — it's expected evolution, and the correct response is to re-run the scoping and estimation process fresh against today's actual numbers and design the next iteration accordingly, the same discipline this chapter teaches applied a second time rather than treated as a one-time exercise. If, however, the original design made no provision at all for extending itself later — no clean seams for introducing a cache, no path to adding replicas or horizontal scaling without a full rewrite — that's a legitimate design critique independent of whether the specific growth was predictable, because a well-scoped design for a known scale should still leave room to extend, even if it correctly doesn't pre-build for scale it wasn't asked to support. The staff-level judgment is distinguishing "the estimate was reasonable and growth happened" from "the design didn't leave itself room to grow," since the fix for each is different — one is simply redoing the estimation and design cycle, the other is a structural lesson about how designs should be built even under known, modest requirements.

Interview Gold Point: Make the distinction explicit between "wrong estimate" and "inextensible design" — a staff-level postmortem that conflates the two tends to produce the wrong lesson learned (either blaming an estimate that was reasonable at the time, or missing a real structural gap behind a growth number that looked like simple, unavoidable success).
STAFFQ49. How do you coach an engineer who is technically strong on individual components (caching, databases, queues) but consistently struggles in system design interviews or reviews?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at staff level

This is almost always the gap this exact chapter targets: deep component knowledge without the connective scaffolding — scoping, estimation, and explicit trade-off reasoning — that determines which components to reach for and why. I'd have them practice narrating the ordered process from Section 5.1 out loud on problems they already know the "answer" to, specifically to build the habit of stating assumptions and running estimation math before describing any component, since the knowledge gap isn't the components themselves, it's the discipline of applying them in response to quantified requirements rather than from memorized association ("read-heavy means add a cache" as a reflex, rather than as a conclusion reached via the read/write ratio and estimated hot-key traffic). The specific, actionable coaching point: after every practice session, have them identify out loud the one or two numbers from their own estimate that most directly justified their biggest architectural decision — if they can't name one, the gap is confirmed and the fix is deliberate practice on exactly that connective step, not more study of individual components they may already understand well.

STAFFQ50. Looking across this track's upcoming chapters (CAP theorem, load balancing, caching, replication, sharding, queues, rate limiting, consensus), what's the single theme from this foundations chapter you'd want every engineer to carry into all of them?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

That every one of those topics is a tool selected in response to a quantified, named requirement — never a default. A staff engineer reviewing a design that reaches for consistent hashing, or a specific consensus algorithm, or aggressive caching, should always expect to see the scoping and estimation trail that led there: what scale, what access pattern, what failure mode is actually being addressed. The single habit this chapter exists to install is treating "why this, specifically, for this problem's actual numbers" as a question every subsequent chapter's techniques must answer before being adopted — the depth this track builds in each individual topic is only valuable to the extent an engineer also retains the discipline, from this chapter, to know when and why to reach for it at all.

16. SUMMARY

  • A system design interview (and a real system design task) evaluates reasoning under ambiguity — clarifying scope, quantifying scale, and justifying trade-offs — not the ability to reproduce one memorized "correct" architecture.
  • Functional requirements describe what a system does; non-functional requirements (scale, latency, availability, consistency) describe how well it does it, and it's the non-functional requirements that actually determine architecture.
  • Vertical scaling increases a single machine's capacity and has a hard ceiling; horizontal scaling adds more machines and is in principle unbounded, but requires the application tier to be stateless to work correctly behind a load balancer.
  • Statelessness — never depending on data held only in one instance's local memory for anything that must survive across requests — is the specific property that makes horizontal scaling, autoscaling, and graceful instance replacement all actually work.
  • Back-of-the-envelope estimation (QPS, storage, bandwidth) converts an underspecified problem into concrete numbers that justify every subsequent architectural decision, and prevents both under-building and reflexive over-engineering.
  • Latency (time per request) and throughput (requests per unit time) are related but distinct, connected precisely by Little's Law through concurrency — a distinction that explains why "add more servers" fixes a throughput problem but not a latency problem.
  • Load balancers, caches, databases, and message queues are tools this track covers in full depth ahead — each should be adopted in response to a quantified need established through the scoping and estimation discipline this chapter teaches, never as a reflexive default.
