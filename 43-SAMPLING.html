CHAPTER 43 — SAMPLING

Volume 1 — AI Foundations · Chapter 16 of 22

The Context Windows chapter drew the boundary of what a model can see at generation time. This chapter is about what happens the instant after: given everything visible in that window, the model produces a probability distribution over its entire vocabulary for the very next token, and something has to turn that distribution into an actual, chosen word. That "something" — sampling — is the single most under-examined lever in the entire inference pipeline, and it is directly responsible for whether your production system feels creative or reckless, deterministic or flaky, cheap or expensive.

1. MOTIVATION

Every time you call a chat completion endpoint — through Spring AI's ChatClient, a raw REST call, or a vendor SDK — you send a handful of parameters that most engineers copy-paste from a tutorial and never think about again: temperature, top_p, sometimes top_k, sometimes frequency_penalty. These are not cosmetic knobs. They are the entire mechanism by which a model's raw internal output — a dense vector of numbers over every possible next token — becomes the one word that actually gets streamed back to your user. Get this wrong and you will see it in production as one of three failure modes: an assistant that repeats itself in a loop, an assistant that hallucinates wildly creative nonsense on a task that needed rigid precision, or an assistant that gives a different answer to the same support ticket every time a customer refreshes the page.

This matters more, not less, as you move from "playing with a model in a chat UI" to "engineering a system other people depend on." A chat UI hides sampling behind a single vendor-chosen default and nobody complains because a slightly different phrasing of a casual answer is invisible. A production system does not get that luxury: a coding assistant that samples too greedily gets stuck regenerating the same buggy pattern; a customer-support bot that samples too loosely invents refund policies that do not exist; an evaluation harness that does not pin sampling parameters produces flaky, non-reproducible test results that make you doubt a real regression is real. Sampling is where the probabilistic nature of language models — the property you have been building intuition for since the very first chapter of this volume — becomes a concrete, tunable engineering surface instead of an abstract fact about how the models work.

There is also a subtler reason this deserves its own chapter rather than a footnote inside "how transformers work": sampling strategy is one of the few places in the entire LLM stack where you, the application engineer, have direct, cheap, per-request control over the model's behavior without retraining, fine-tuning, or even changing the prompt. Prompt engineering is powerful but expensive to iterate on and easy to overload (as the previous volume's discussion of instruction-stacking showed). Sampling parameters are a single field in a request body. Understanding them precisely is one of the highest-leverage-per-line-of-code skills in this entire book.

2. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

Sampling as a problem did not begin with large language models — it is inherited wholesale from decades of work in statistical language modeling and, further back, from classical statistics on drawing values from a probability distribution. But the specific set of techniques you will use daily in AI Engineering crystallized through a fairly short, traceable sequence of research responses to a very concrete, embarrassing failure mode.

2.1 Greedy Decoding and Beam Search (Pre-2018 Neural Sequence Models)

Before large-scale Transformer language models existed, neural sequence-to-sequence models (used for machine translation, early text generation, and speech recognition) already faced the exact problem this chapter is about: at each step, the model outputs a probability distribution over the vocabulary, and something must pick the next token. The two dominant approaches were greedy decoding (always pick the single highest-probability token) and beam search (track several of the most likely partial sequences in parallel and keep expanding the most promising ones, discarding the rest). Beam search was — and still is — the standard choice for tasks with one "best" answer, like machine translation, because it is a much closer approximation to finding the globally most likely full sentence than greedy decoding, which can lock in an early mistake it can never undo.

Beam search has a specific, well-documented pathology when applied to open-ended text generation rather than translation: it produces text that is technically high-probability but reads as bland, repetitive, and generic — the "least surprising possible sentence" rather than a natural one. Researchers observed that human-written text is not, in fact, the maximum-likelihood sequence of words; humans naturally introduce moderate-probability, "surprising" word choices constantly, and text that avoids all such choices reads as robotic and loops on itself (a beam-searched story generator would frequently degenerate into repeating the same phrase, because a repeated phrase is often locally very high-probability).

2.2 The Shift to Stochastic Sampling (2018–2020)

The fix that emerged was a philosophical pivot as significant, in its own narrow domain, as the statistical-ML pivot from the "What Is Intelligence" chapter's history: stop trying to find the single most likely sequence, and instead randomly draw tokens from the model's predicted distribution, the way the text was originally generated by a human making a long chain of moderately-likely word choices. Pure random sampling from the full distribution, however, immediately exposed a new problem: language model vocabularies contain tens of thousands of tokens, and even a very unlikely token has some nonzero probability assigned to it. Sample purely at random over the full distribution enough times and you will eventually draw one of these low-probability "garbage" tokens, and once one nonsense token enters the generated text, everything the model generates after it tends to compound into further nonsense, because the model is now conditioning on its own broken output.

This is the exact problem that produced the two techniques that now dominate LLM inference: top-k sampling (Fan, Lewis & Dauphin, 2018, in work on hierarchical story generation) restricted random draws to only the k most likely tokens at each step, truncating the long tail of nonsense options before sampling. Top-p / nucleus sampling (Holtzman et al., "The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration," 2019) refined this further by observing that a fixed k is a poor truncation rule because the model's confidence varies wildly from step to step — sometimes only two tokens are plausible, sometimes hundreds are — and proposed instead truncating to the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds a threshold p, adapting the truncation width dynamically per step. Both papers were written specifically to fix the degeneration problems of beam search and naive sampling, and both are still the default decoding strategy shipped in essentially every production LLM API today, including every model you will call through Spring AI.

2.3 Temperature: An Older Idea Borrowed from Statistical Mechanics

The temperature parameter predates all of this. It is borrowed directly from the Boltzmann distribution in statistical physics, where temperature controls how sharply a system's states are weighted by energy — high temperature flattens the distribution toward uniform randomness, low temperature sharpens it toward the lowest-energy (most probable) state. Applying this same rescaling to a language model's output distribution before sampling gives exactly the intuitive control production systems rely on today: low temperature sharpens the distribution toward the model's single most confident guess (closer to greedy decoding), high temperature flattens it toward more uniform randomness across many plausible tokens.

When you set topP to 0.9 instead of leaving it at a vendor default, you are not guessing — you are directly invoking Holtzman et al.'s fix for neural text degeneration. Knowing the failure each parameter targets tells you exactly when to reach for it.

3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

Strip away the history, and sampling reduces to one precise engineering problem, sitting at the exact boundary between the model's internal computation and the text your application actually receives:

Given a probability distribution over an entire vocabulary at each generation step, choose exactly one token — in a way that is controllable, avoids both degenerate repetition and incoherent noise, and can be tuned per task to trade off determinism against diversity.

Every word in that sentence is load-bearing. "Controllable" means the strategy must expose parameters an application engineer can set per request, not a fixed, opaque choice baked into the model. "Avoids degenerate repetition" is the beam-search failure mode from Section 2. "Avoids incoherent noise" is the pure-random-sampling failure mode from Section 2. "Tuned per task" is the crucial engineering insight this chapter builds toward: there is no single correct sampling configuration — a SQL-generation tool and a creative brainstorming assistant have opposite requirements, and shipping both with the same default temperature is a design mistake, not a neutral choice.

It is worth being explicit about what sampling is not solving. It is not solving hallucination (covered in the next chapter) — a model can sample perfectly correctly from its distribution and still hallucinate, because the distribution itself, learned from training data, can assign high probability to a fluent, wrong answer. It is not solving context management (the previous chapter) — sampling operates purely on the single-step output distribution the model produces after all context has already been processed. And it is not a substitute for structured output validation — sampling controls how a token is chosen, never whether the resulting text obeys a schema, a format, or a business rule. Keep these boundaries sharp; conflating them is one of the common mistakes catalogued later in this chapter.

4. THEORY

4.1 The Raw Output: Logits, Not Words

Recall from the internal-working chapters on Transformers and Neural Networks that a language model's final layer does not output a word — it outputs a vector of raw, unnormalized scores, one per vocabulary entry, called logits. A modern LLM vocabulary typically contains 30,000 to over 100,000 possible tokens, so at every single generation step the model produces a list of that many numbers, where a higher number means "the model thinks this token is a more likely continuation," but the numbers themselves are not probabilities — they can be negative, they do not sum to 1, and their absolute magnitude carries no direct meaning on its own.

Turning logits into an actual probability distribution requires the softmax function, which exponentiates every logit and divides by the sum of all the exponentiated logits, guaranteeing the results are all positive and sum to exactly 1. This distribution — one probability per vocabulary token, given everything generated so far — is the raw material every sampling strategy operates on. Sampling, in its entirety, is the set of techniques for turning this one distribution into one chosen token, thousands of times per response, one token at a time.

4.2 Temperature: Reshaping the Distribution Before Choosing

The most intuitive mental model for temperature is a dial that controls how "opinionated" the model is allowed to be before you even get to the choosing step. Temperature divides every logit by a value T before the softmax is applied. Picture the model's raw confidence as a skyline of buildings, one per candidate token, with height representing logit value.

  • Low temperature (T → 0): dividing by a small number amplifies differences between logits enormously before exponentiating — the tallest building becomes overwhelmingly taller relative to the rest. The resulting probability distribution collapses almost entirely onto the single most likely token. At exactly T = 0, this becomes greedy decoding: always pick the top token, deterministically.
  • Temperature = 1: the logits are used as-is (after softmax) — this is the model's "natural," untouched confidence level as learned during training.
  • High temperature (T > 1): dividing by a number greater than 1 compresses differences between logits — the skyline flattens, tokens that were previously unlikely become meaningfully more probable, and the model's choices become noticeably more varied and, past a point, incoherent.

Temperature never changes which tokens are candidates or their relative ranking — token A that was more likely than token B before temperature scaling is still more likely than B afterward. It only changes how much more likely, which changes how often a sampling step will pick something other than the top choice.

4.3 Top-k: A Hard Cutoff on Candidates

Top-k sampling takes the distribution (optionally after temperature scaling) and discards every token except the k with the highest probability, renormalizing the remaining probabilities to sum to 1, then sampling from that restricted set. This is a simple, effective guardrail against the long tail of near-zero-probability garbage tokens that pure random sampling would occasionally draw. Its weakness, which motivated nucleus sampling, is that k is fixed regardless of context: if the model is extremely confident and only two tokens are plausible, top-k = 40 still considers 38 tokens that shouldn't realistically be candidates; if the model is genuinely uncertain among 100 plausible continuations, top-k = 40 arbitrarily excludes 60 of them.

4.4 Top-p / Nucleus Sampling: An Adaptive Cutoff

Top-p sampling fixes exactly this weakness by making the cutoff adapt to the shape of the distribution at each step rather than fixing a count. It sorts tokens by probability from highest to lowest, then walks down the list accumulating probability mass until the running total exceeds the threshold p (a common production default is 0.9 or 0.95), and samples only from that "nucleus" of tokens — discarding everything after the cutoff and renormalizing. When the model is very confident, the nucleus might contain only two or three tokens even with p = 0.95, because those few tokens already account for 95% of the probability mass. When the model is uncertain, the nucleus naturally widens to include dozens of tokens. This context-sensitivity is precisely why nucleus sampling became the default across essentially the entire industry.

4.5 Repetition and Presence Penalties

A separate family of controls addresses a different failure mode entirely: even with well-tuned temperature and top-p, autoregressive models have a tendency to loop, because once a phrase appears in the model's own context, that phrase becomes statistically likely to reappear (the model is, after all, predicting based on patterns it has just seen, including its own output). Frequency penalty reduces a token's logit in proportion to how many times it has already appeared in the generated output so far — a token used ten times gets penalized more than one used twice. Presence penalty applies a flat penalty to any token that has appeared at all, regardless of count, which more strongly encourages introducing entirely new vocabulary rather than just discouraging heavy repetition of the same few words. Both are applied directly to the logits, before temperature and top-p/top-k are applied, and both are complementary to, not substitutes for, the distribution-shaping techniques above.

4.6 Determinism, Seeds, and the Limits of Reproducibility

A subtlety worth naming explicitly because it trips up engineers building evaluation and testing infrastructure: setting temperature to 0 makes token selection deterministic (always the argmax of the distribution), but it does not, by itself, guarantee bit-for-bit identical output across repeated calls to a hosted API. Floating-point non-associativity across different batching configurations on the provider's GPUs, non-deterministic kernel execution order, and provider-side load balancing across slightly different hardware or model revisions can all introduce small numerical differences that occasionally flip which token has the highest logit in a near-tie. Some providers expose an explicit seed parameter specifically to improve (not perfectly guarantee) reproducibility by controlling the pseudo-random elements of the serving stack. The honest engineering takeaway: temperature 0 gets you "very close to deterministic," not "provably deterministic" — a distinction that matters when you are building a test suite that asserts exact string equality against model output.

5. INTERNAL WORKING — THE SAMPLING PIPELINE, STEP BY STEP

Here is the complete, ordered pipeline a production inference server runs at every single token-generation step, from raw model output to the one token that gets appended to the sequence and streamed to your application. Understanding the ordering matters — these transformations are not commutative, and the order determines what interacts with what.

  1. Forward pass produces logits. The Transformer's final linear layer produces one raw score per vocabulary token, conditioned on everything in the context window so far (including the model's own previously generated tokens in this response).
  2. Logit-level penalties are applied. Frequency penalty and presence penalty subtract adjustments from the raw logits of tokens that have already appeared in the output, based on the response generated so far in this request.
  3. Temperature scaling. Every (penalty-adjusted) logit is divided by the temperature value, reshaping how sharply peaked or flat the eventual distribution will be.
  4. Softmax normalization. The temperature-scaled logits are converted into an actual probability distribution — positive values summing to 1.
  5. Top-k truncation (if configured). All but the k highest-probability tokens are discarded, and the remaining probabilities are renormalized to sum to 1 again.
  6. Top-p / nucleus truncation (if configured). Starting from the highest-probability remaining token, accumulate probability mass until the threshold p is crossed; discard everything beyond that point and renormalize once more.
  7. Sample. A single token is drawn from the final, truncated, renormalized distribution using a pseudo-random number generator (optionally seeded).
  8. Append and repeat. The chosen token is appended to the sequence, becomes part of the context for the next step, and the entire pipeline runs again for the next token — until an end-of-sequence token is sampled, a configured stop sequence is matched, or a maximum token limit is reached.

A few edge cases fall directly out of this ordering and are worth reasoning through explicitly, because interview questions and production bugs both live here:

  • What if top-k and top-p are both set? Most inference stacks apply top-k first, then top-p on the surviving subset — meaning top-k acts as a coarse pre-filter and top-p refines it further. Setting both aggressively (e.g., k=5 and p=0.5) can produce an extremely narrow, near-deterministic distribution even though neither value alone looks that restrictive.
  • What if temperature is set to exactly 0? Dividing by zero is undefined, so implementations special-case T=0 as pure greedy decoding (skip softmax-based sampling entirely, always take the argmax) rather than literally performing the division.
  • What if top-p is set to 1.0? The nucleus includes the entire vocabulary — top-p becomes a no-op, and the effective behavior is governed purely by temperature (and top-k, if also set).
  • What happens to penalties across a long response? Frequency penalty accumulates as generation proceeds — a token that was fine to repeat once early in a long response becomes progressively more penalized the more times it recurs, which is why frequency penalty is a more aggressive anti-repetition tool for long-form generation than presence penalty, which never intensifies past the first occurrence.

6. MATHEMATICS — THE INTUITION FIRST, FORMALISM SECOND

You do not need heavy mathematics to use sampling correctly, but a light formal grounding makes the parameters' behavior predictable rather than magical. Two formulas carry essentially all of it.

6.1 Softmax with Temperature

Given logits z₁, z₂, ..., zₙ for the n vocabulary tokens and a temperature T, the probability assigned to token i is: 

                 exp(z_i / T)
   P(token_i) = ───────────────
                 Σ_j exp(z_j / T)

The intuition to hold onto: dividing every logit by T before exponentiating is what stretches or compresses the gaps between them. Because exp is a fast-growing function, small changes in the gap between the top logit and the rest translate into large changes in relative probability once exponentiated — this is exactly why temperature has an outsized, nonlinear effect on output diversity even though the formula itself looks mild.

6.2 Nucleus (Top-p) Selection

Sort tokens by probability descending, so P(1) ≥ P(2) ≥ ... ≥ P(n). Define the nucleus as the smallest prefix of this sorted list whose cumulative probability meets or exceeds p: 

   nucleus = smallest set { 1, 2, ..., m } such that   Σ P(i)  ≥  p
                                                        i=1..m

Renormalize probabilities within the nucleus (divide each by the nucleus's total probability mass) and sample from that restricted, renormalized distribution. The key mathematical property worth internalizing: m — the number of tokens actually considered — is not fixed by you; it emerges from the shape of the distribution at that specific step. This is precisely the adaptivity that made nucleus sampling superior to a fixed top-k cutoff.

6.3 A Worked Numeric Example

Suppose, at one generation step, the model assigns these probabilities (already after softmax at T = 1) to its top five candidate tokens, with a long tail of thousands of other tokens sharing the remaining tiny probability mass:

TokenProbabilityCumulative
"the"0.420.42
"a"0.210.63
"this"0.140.77
"an"0.090.86
"that"0.050.91

With top-p = 0.9, the nucleus is the first five tokens (cumulative reaches 0.91, crossing the 0.9 threshold at "that") — everything after "that," including thousands of near-zero-probability tail tokens, is excluded, and sampling proceeds over just these five, renormalized. With top-p = 0.6, the nucleus shrinks to just "the" and "a" (cumulative 0.63 ≥ 0.6), a much narrower, more deterministic-feeling choice. Notice how a 0.3 change in the p threshold changed the candidate pool from 5 tokens to 2 — this is the adaptive narrowing that a fixed top-k value cannot replicate without being re-tuned per prompt.

Sampling parameters are decided far earlier in a real system's design than most engineers assume — they belong in the same category of decision as choosing a model, not a runtime tweak an operator adjusts on the fly. Two production realities are worth calling out explicitly. First, sampling parameters are a first-class piece of an evaluation and observability strategy — if your tracing does not capture the exact temperature and top-p used for every logged generation, you cannot distinguish "the model got worse" from "someone changed a config default," which is a genuinely common incident root cause. Second, teams that run agentic pipelines (Volume 4 territory) very often use different sampling profiles for different stages of the same request — near-zero temperature for a step that must emit a structured tool call or JSON payload, moderate temperature for a step that drafts a user-facing explanation of the result. Treating sampling configuration as uniform across an entire multi-step pipeline is a missed opportunity, not a simplification.

9. JAVA / SPRING PERSPECTIVE

Spring AI exposes sampling parameters as ordinary, strongly-typed configuration on the request, following exactly the same "swap the implementation without touching business logic" philosophy the earlier Java/Spring Perspective sections in this volume have emphasized. The concrete mechanism is a provider-specific ChatOptions implementation (for example OpenAiChatOptions, or the equivalent for the Anthropic/other model integrations Spring AI supports) that you build with a fluent builder and attach to a call through ChatClient. 

// Defining a reusable, task-specific sampling profile
ChatOptions preciseOptions = OpenAiChatOptions.builder()
        .temperature(0.1)
        .topP(0.9)
        .maxTokens(512)
        .build();

ChatOptions creativeOptions = OpenAiChatOptions.builder()
        .temperature(1.0)
        .topP(0.95)
        .frequencyPenalty(0.4)
        .maxTokens(1024)
        .build();

// Using it through ChatClient for a precision-sensitive task,
// e.g. generating a structured SQL query from natural language
String sql = chatClient.prompt()
        .system("You translate natural language into read-only SQL. "
                + "Return ONLY the SQL statement, nothing else.")
        .user(userQuestion)
        .options(preciseOptions)
        .call()
        .content();

// The same ChatClient, same model, different sampling profile,
// for an open-ended brainstorming feature
String ideas = chatClient.prompt()
        .system("You are a creative brainstorming partner.")
        .user(userPrompt)
        .options(creativeOptions)
        .call()
        .content();

Notice what this design buys you architecturally, echoing the earlier table of "patterns you already know": defining named ChatOptions profiles as Spring beans is exactly the Strategy pattern applied to decoding behavior, and it means a change to your "precise" profile's temperature is a one-line, centrally-tested change rather than a value duplicated across every call site in the codebase. It also composes cleanly with the ChatModel abstraction from earlier in this volume — the same ChatOptions shape (with provider-specific fields where needed) lets you swap the underlying model provider without rewriting the sampling logic that took real tuning effort to get right.

A second, easily missed detail matters for correctness: options set at the ChatClient.Builder level (application-wide defaults, often configured once in a @Configuration class) are overridden per-call by options passed to .options(...) on an individual prompt request, not merged field-by-field in every provider integration — always verify the specific merge semantics for the provider you are using before assuming a partially-specified per-call options object will fall back cleanly to your defaults for the fields you left unset.

10. BEST PRACTICES

  • Pick sampling parameters per task, not per application. A single global default temperature is a design smell — structured extraction, tool-calling, and code generation want near-zero temperature; open-ended writing and ideation want meaningfully higher values.
  • Prefer adjusting temperature or top-p, not both aggressively at once, unless you have measured the combination. Because they compound through the pipeline in Section 5, tuning both independently without an evaluation harness makes it very hard to attribute an output-quality change to the right knob.
  • Treat temperature 0 as "very low variance," not "guaranteed identical output," when designing tests or reproducibility guarantees — as covered in Section 4.6, provider-side floating-point and serving nondeterminism can still produce small differences.
  • Log the exact sampling parameters with every generation in production, alongside the prompt and response, so that quality regressions can be correctly attributed to a config change, a prompt change, or a genuine model behavior shift.
  • Use lower temperature and tighter top-p for any step whose output must parse (JSON, SQL, a tool-call argument), since a single unexpected low-probability token in a highly structured output is far more likely to break a parser than to add useful variety.
  • Reach for frequency/presence penalties specifically to fix observed repetition loops, not as a default — they are a targeted fix for a specific symptom, and applying them blindly can push a model away from legitimately necessary repeated terms (variable names, entity names) in technical output.

11. COMMON MISTAKES

  • Using a "creative" default temperature (often 0.7–1.0) for tasks that need precision — classification, structured data extraction, or anything downstream code will parse — and then treating the resulting occasional malformed output as a "hallucination bug" when it is actually a sampling misconfiguration.
  • Confusing temperature with "intelligence" or "quality." Temperature does not make a model smarter or dumber, more or less knowledgeable — it only changes how much randomness is injected into token selection from the same underlying distribution. A higher-temperature response is not a "more thoughtful" one; it is a noisier draw from the identical probability distribution.
  • Assuming top-p and top-k are redundant and only ever setting one. They are complementary truncation strategies with different failure modes (Section 4.3–4.4); many production configurations benefit from combining a generous top-k as a coarse safety net with top-p for the fine-grained, context-adaptive cutoff.
  • Writing exact-match tests against non-zero-temperature output and treating the resulting flakiness as a test infrastructure bug rather than the expected consequence of stochastic sampling — the correct fix (as covered in the earlier "What Is Intelligence" chapter) is statistical or rubric-based evaluation, or pinning temperature to 0 specifically for the test path if determinism-adjacent behavior is required.
  • Applying the same sampling profile to every stage of a multi-step agentic pipeline, missing the opportunity to run precision-critical steps (tool argument generation) at near-zero temperature while allowing a final user-facing summarization step more room for natural variation.
  • Cranking temperature up to "fix" repetitive output when the actual problem is looping caused by a lack of frequency/presence penalty, or an issue with context management — high temperature masks the symptom while making unrelated parts of the output noisier and less reliable.

12. PERFORMANCE CONSIDERATIONS

Sampling's performance footprint is smaller and more localized than most other topics in this volume, but it is not zero, and two effects are worth understanding precisely.

  • Computational cost of the sampling step itself is negligible relative to the forward pass. Softmax, truncation, and weighted random draws over a vocabulary of tens of thousands of entries are cheap operations compared to the matrix multiplications of a full Transformer forward pass; changing temperature or top-p does not materially change per-token latency or GPU cost on the provider side.
  • Indirect cost through output length and retries is where sampling actually affects cost and latency at the application level. A poorly tuned high-temperature configuration that produces occasional incoherent or malformed output on structured tasks forces application-level retries, doubling or tripling the effective token cost and latency for that request; conversely, a configuration that causes repetitive looping can run a response all the way to its maxTokens ceiling, maximizing (wasted) token cost and latency for a response that should have terminated much earlier via a natural end-of-sequence token.
  • Streaming interacts with sampling in a way worth designing around. Because each token is sampled independently given prior tokens (including the model's own already-streamed output), a response that starts to go off the rails is visible to the user token by token as it streams — this is an argument for lower temperature specifically on any user-facing path where a mid-stream correction (silently discarding and retrying) is not feasible.
  • Batch/throughput considerations on self-hosted inference. If you operate your own inference server (vLLM, TGI, or similar) rather than calling a hosted API, sampling parameters are typically uniform-cost across a batch, but per-request temperature/top-p differences do not meaningfully affect batching efficiency — the dominant throughput levers remain sequence length and batch size, not the sampling configuration itself.

13. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

Sampling has a genuinely limited security surface on its own — it is not, by itself, an attack vector the way prompt injection or unvalidated tool output are. Padding this section with speculative risk would misrepresent the topic. That said, two real, narrow considerations are worth naming honestly rather than ignoring.

First, sampling parameters interact with jailbreak and adversarial-prompt robustness in a documented, non-trivial way: higher temperature increases the probability that a model samples a lower-probability token that happens to fall outside its trained refusal behavior for a borderline request, effectively increasing variance in how reliably safety-relevant behavior holds across repeated attempts at the same adversarial prompt. This is a second-order effect, not a primary attack surface, but it is a real reason safety-critical or compliance-sensitive generation paths (content moderation decisions, policy-adherent customer communications) should generally run at low temperature rather than a "creative" default.

Second, in adversarial or red-teaming contexts, an attacker who can control sampling parameters directly (for example, through an exposed API that passes user-supplied temperature/top-p straight through to the model without server-side bounds) gains a small additional lever for probing a model's behavior boundary through repeated high-temperature sampling — a reason to treat sampling parameters as configuration you validate and bound server-side, not raw user input you forward unchecked, in any public-facing API you build.

14. INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

JUNIORQ1. What is the difference between temperature and top-p, in one sentence each?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Temperature rescales the sharpness of the probability distribution before sampling (lower = more deterministic, higher = more random); top-p (nucleus sampling) truncates the candidate pool to the smallest set of highest-probability tokens whose cumulative probability meets a threshold, discarding the unlikely long tail before sampling.

JUNIORQ2. What happens if you set temperature to 0?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

The model always selects the single highest-probability token at each step — this is greedy decoding, and it makes the output effectively deterministic (though not perfectly bit-for-bit reproducible across API calls due to server-side floating-point and serving nondeterminism).

MIDQ3. Why did the field move away from beam search for open-ended text generation, and what replaced it?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Beam search finds high-likelihood full sequences, but for open-ended generation this produces bland, repetitive, "least surprising" text and is prone to looping, because human-written text is not itself the maximum-likelihood sequence of words. Stochastic sampling strategies — top-k and, later, top-p/nucleus sampling — replaced it by randomly drawing from the model's distribution (restricted to plausible candidates) rather than searching for the single most likely sequence, producing text that reads more naturally.

MIDQ4. Explain why top-p is generally preferred over a fixed top-k cutoff.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Top-k fixes the number of candidate tokens regardless of the model's actual confidence at that step, which is either too restrictive when the model is genuinely uncertain among many plausible options, or too permissive when the model is highly confident in just one or two tokens. Top-p adapts the candidate pool size dynamically per step by including exactly as many top tokens as needed to reach a cumulative probability threshold, so the cutoff naturally narrows when the model is confident and widens when it is uncertain.

MIDQ5. What are frequency and presence penalties, and how do they differ?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Both reduce the logits of tokens that have already appeared in the generated output, applied before temperature and top-p/top-k. Frequency penalty scales with how many times a token has already appeared — the more it recurs, the more strongly it's suppressed — making it effective against escalating repetition loops. Presence penalty applies a flat, one-time penalty the first time a token appears, regardless of subsequent count, which more strongly encourages introducing new vocabulary rather than just discouraging heavy reuse of already-used words.

SENIORQ6. You are designing sampling configuration for a multi-step agent pipeline: one step parses user intent into a structured tool call, another step drafts a natural-language summary of the tool's result for the user. How would you configure sampling differently for each, and why?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

The tool-call parsing step should run at or near temperature 0 with a tight top-p, because its output must parse as valid structured data (a function name and arguments matching a schema) — any injected randomness risks producing a malformed call that breaks the pipeline, with no upside to variety in this step. The summarization step can run at a moderate temperature (roughly 0.6–0.9) with a standard top-p (0.9–0.95), because natural-sounding prose benefits from some lexical variety and there is no strict parser downstream that a slightly less predictable word choice would break. Applying a single uniform sampling profile across both steps either makes the tool call unreliable (if tuned for the summary step) or makes the summary read stilted and repetitive (if tuned for the tool call step).

Interview Gold Point: Mention explicitly that sampling configuration should be a per-step, per-task design decision within a pipeline, not a single application-wide default — and that you'd log the exact parameters used per step in your observability layer specifically so a quality regression can be attributed to a config change rather than guessed at. This signals you think about sampling as production configuration, not a one-time tutorial setting.
SENIORQ7. A teammate says "we should just always use temperature 0 everywhere for consistency and safety." How do you respond?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Temperature 0 is the right default for tasks with one correct or near-correct answer — structured extraction, classification, tool-call generation, deterministic-feeling support responses — but it is actively harmful for tasks whose value depends on variety or exploration, such as brainstorming, creative writing, or generating multiple diverse candidate solutions to review. It can also amplify certain failure modes: a model stuck in a bad pattern at temperature 0 will deterministically repeat that exact mistake on retry, whereas a small amount of temperature gives a regeneration attempt a genuine chance to escape a bad local pattern. The right framing is "choose sampling per task against measured criteria," not a single global rule — and any safety argument for temperature 0 should be scoped specifically to safety- or compliance-sensitive generation paths, not treated as a universal policy.

STAFFQ8. How would you design an evaluation and monitoring strategy to catch problems caused specifically by sampling misconfiguration, as distinct from prompt regressions or model version changes?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Instrument every generation call to log the exact resolved sampling parameters (temperature, top-p, top-k, penalties, model version, prompt hash) alongside the output, so any three of "prompt changed," "model changed," and "sampling config changed" can be independently ruled in or out during an incident, rather than conflated into a single "the model got worse" hypothesis. Build sampling-specific regression checks into the evaluation harness: run a fixed test set at the production sampling configuration across multiple samples per input to measure output variance directly (a sudden increase in variance on a task expected to be near-deterministic is itself a signal, independent of whether individual outputs pass quality checks), and separately track structured-output parse-failure rate as a leading indicator that a precision-sensitive path's temperature or top-p has drifted too high. Treat sampling configuration as versioned, reviewed configuration — changes should go through the same review and staged-rollout process as a prompt or model version change, not be adjustable ad hoc by any caller.

16. SUMMARY

  • Sampling is the mechanism that turns a model's raw per-step probability distribution over its vocabulary into one chosen token — it sits at the exact seam between the model's internal computation and the text your application actually receives.
  • The field arrived at today's default toolkit (temperature, top-k, top-p/nucleus sampling, frequency/presence penalties) through a specific, traceable sequence of fixes to named failure modes: beam search's blandness and repetition, then pure random sampling's long-tail nonsense.
  • The full pipeline — penalties, then temperature scaling, then softmax, then top-k truncation, then top-p truncation, then the random draw — is ordered and non-commutative; parameters interact, and tuning one without understanding the others produces confusing, hard-to-attribute behavior changes.
  • There is no single correct sampling configuration: precision-critical tasks (structured output, tool calls) want low temperature and tight top-p; open-ended, exploratory tasks want meaningfully higher values — and a single global default across an entire application is a design smell, not a simplification.
  • Temperature 0 is very-low-variance, not provably deterministic, and it is not a substitute for statistical or rubric-based evaluation — a lesson that connects directly back to the non-deterministic-systems framing established in the very first chapter of this volume.
  • In Spring AI, sampling configuration lives as strongly-typed, provider-specific ChatOptions objects attached per call through ChatClient, which lets you define reusable, task-specific, centrally-tuned sampling profiles rather than scattering magic numbers across call sites.
