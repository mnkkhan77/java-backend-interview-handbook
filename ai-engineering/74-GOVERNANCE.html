CHAPTER 74 — GOVERNANCE

Volume 5 — Enterprise AI Engineering · Chapter 11 of 12

Distributed Systems for AI (Chapter 73) gave you the mechanics of running model inference reliably across many machines — replication, queuing, scaling, failure recovery. None of that answers a harder question a Fortune 500 risk committee will ask before any of it ships: who approved this model to talk to customers, on what evidence, and who is accountable when it says something wrong? Governance is the organizational and technical control layer that turns "we built an AI system that works" into "we built an AI system the enterprise is allowed to run" — and unlike the systems concerns of the previous chapter, getting this wrong does not page an on-call engineer, it ends up in a regulator's file or a courtroom.

1. MOTIVATION

Every chapter so far in this volume has assumed a fairly narrow question: is the AI system technically sound? Does it retrieve the right documents, call the right tools, stay within latency budgets, resist prompt injection, scale under load? Governance exists because a technically sound AI system can still be an organizationally reckless one. A model that is accurate, fast, and secure can still be deployed by a team with no idea whether it is legal to use for that purpose, no record of who approved it, no way to prove to an auditor what it was trained or fine-tuned on, and no process for retiring it when a better or safer alternative appears. Enterprises do not fail at AI primarily because the transformer math was wrong — they fail because nobody could answer "prove to me this was responsibly built and is being responsibly operated" when the question mattered most: during an incident, an audit, a regulatory inquiry, or a lawsuit.

For you, the Java/Spring engineer moving into AI Engineering, this chapter marks a shift in altitude. Chapters on retrieval, tool calling, and observability made you a better builder. This chapter makes you a builder whose systems survive contact with an enterprise's legal, risk, compliance, and audit functions — the functions that decide whether your feature ships at all. In a regulated bank, insurer, healthcare provider, or public-sector agency, an AI feature with excellent recall and low latency will still be blocked at the gate if it cannot answer basic governance questions: What data trained or grounds this model? Who is the accountable owner? What is the approved use case, and what happens outside it? How do we roll it back? This is not bureaucracy layered on top of engineering — it is the risk-management discipline that determines whether engineering work is allowed to reach production users at all, and increasingly, as you will see in the next chapter on Compliance, whether it is legal to operate.

Practically, this matters to your career trajectory too. Senior and staff AI engineers are distinguished less by which framework they know and more by whether they can design systems that are governable by construction — systems where lineage, ownership, approval status, and rollback are first-class artifacts, not things bolted on after an incident forces the question. This chapter builds the mental model and the concrete mechanisms (model cards, model registries, approval gates, audit trails, human-in-the-loop checkpoints) that make that possible.

2. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

2.1 Governance Before AI: Model Risk Management in Finance

AI governance did not appear from nothing when large language models became popular — it is a direct descendant of a much older discipline: Model Risk Management (MRM), formalized in banking regulation over decades and codified sharply after the 2008 financial crisis in guidance such as the U.S. Federal Reserve's SR 11-7. Banks had been using statistical and later machine-learning models for credit scoring, fraud detection, and trading decisions long before "AI Engineering" was a job title, and regulators had already learned the hard way that a model quietly making bad decisions at scale (mispriced mortgage risk models, for instance) can cause systemic harm. SR 11-7 established the pattern nearly every modern AI governance framework still follows: independent validation of a model before deployment, ongoing monitoring after deployment, clear documentation of assumptions and limitations, and a named, accountable owner — not "the team," a specific person or committee.

2.2 Software Governance: Change Management and SDLC Gates

In parallel, mainstream software engineering developed its own governance lineage that you have almost certainly lived through even if nobody called it "governance": change advisory boards, release approval gates, SOC 2 and ISO 27001 audits of your SDLC, and mandatory code review before merge. These controls exist because deterministic software can still cause enormous harm through a bad deploy, and enterprises needed provable, auditable answers to "who approved this change and why" long before AI entered the picture. If you have ever needed a second approver on a pull request touching a payments service, or filled out a change ticket before a production deploy, you have already practiced governance — just not on a system whose behavior is probabilistic.

2.3 The AI-Specific Turn: From Model Cards to Formal Frameworks (2018–present)

AI-specific governance crystallized once machine learning models moved from research artifacts to consumer-facing products at scale. Google researchers proposed Model Cards in 2018 — short, structured documents describing a model's intended use, training data, evaluation results, and known limitations, modeled loosely on nutrition labels. This was the first widely-adopted answer to "how do we document a model well enough that someone other than its author can evaluate whether it's safe to use." As LLMs proliferated, this evolved into much heavier frameworks: the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF), published in 2023, gave U.S. organizations a structured, four-function model (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) for AI risk that many enterprises now use as their internal blueprint regardless of jurisdiction. The EU AI Act, finalized in 2024, went further and made governance a legal requirement with tiered obligations by risk category — you will study its specific compliance mechanics in the next chapter, but its existence is why "AI governance" changed, in a few years, from a nice-to-have engineering practice into a board-level, legally mandated function at any enterprise operating in or selling into the EU.

Why this history matters: nothing about AI governance is ad hoc or unique to language models — it is the model-risk-management and change-management disciplines you may already have brushed against, re-applied to a class of system whose failure mode is probabilistic rather than a stack trace. If you already understand SOC 2 controls or a bank's model validation process, you are closer to understanding AI governance than you think.

3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

Strip away the frameworks and acronyms, and AI governance solves one operational problem:

Ensure that every AI system in production has a known, accountable owner; a documented, approved purpose; traceable lineage from data and training/fine-tuning through to the deployed artifact; and a working mechanism to detect, escalate, and roll back harmful behavior — before regulators, auditors, or incidents force the question.

Notice the shape of that sentence: it is entirely about traceability and accountability, not about model quality directly. A governance framework does not make your retrieval pipeline more accurate or your prompts less injectable — those are the concerns of earlier chapters in this volume. Governance answers a different, orthogonal question: if this system causes harm, can the organization show, with evidence, that it exercised reasonable care before and during deployment? This is precisely the same distinction as "does this code work" versus "can we prove, via code review history and CI logs, who approved this change and why" — the second question is the one an auditor, a plaintiff's attorney, or a regulator actually asks.

A second, subtler part of the problem statement: governance must scale with risk, not uniformly. A low-stakes internal tool that summarizes meeting notes does not need the same approval rigor as a model that recommends loan denials or triages patient symptoms. A governance framework that applies maximum rigor everywhere collapses under its own weight and gets bypassed by engineers under deadline pressure; a framework with no risk tiering fails to catch the systems that actually need scrutiny. Designing the right tiering is itself a core governance engineering problem, covered in Section 5.

4. THEORY

4.1 The Three Lines of Defense

Borrowed directly from financial risk management, this is the mental model almost every mature AI governance program is built on. Picture three concentric rings of accountability around any AI system:

LineWhoResponsibility
First lineThe engineering/product team building and operating the systemOwns the risk day-to-day: builds it responsibly, monitors it, is the first to know when it misbehaves
Second lineRisk, compliance, or a dedicated AI governance/ethics functionSets policy, reviews and approves before deployment, independently challenges the first line's assumptions
Third lineInternal auditIndependently verifies that the first and second lines actually followed the process — audits the auditors, in effect

As a Java/Spring engineer, you and your team are almost always the first line. Your job is not to personally invent governance policy — it is to build systems that make the first line's obligations checkable: emitting the audit logs, model version metadata, and approval-gate integrations that the second and third lines depend on. A system that cannot answer "which model version, with which prompt template, answered this specific user's request on this date" fails the first line's most basic obligation, regardless of how well the retrieval pipeline performs.

4.2 Model Cards and System Cards: Documentation as a Governance Artifact

A model card is a structured document — not free-form prose — attached to a specific model or model version, answering a fixed set of questions: What is this model's intended use? What is it explicitly not intended for? What data was it trained or fine-tuned on, at what level of detail can be disclosed? What evaluation metrics were measured, on what benchmark or test set, with what results, including known failure modes and demographic performance gaps if measured? Who is the accountable owner? When was it last revalidated?

Think of a model card as the AI-era equivalent of an API's OpenAPI specification combined with its SLA document — it is the contract other people (auditors, downstream consumers, incident responders) rely on without having to read your training code or your RAG pipeline's implementation. A system card extends this up a level: since most production AI features (as covered in the Production Architecture sections of earlier chapters) are not a single model but a full pipeline — retrieval, tools, guardrails, multiple models chained together — a system card documents the assembled system's intended use and limitations, not just one component's.

4.3 The Model Registry: Governance's Technical Backbone

If model cards are the documentation, the model registry is the system of record that makes governance enforceable rather than aspirational. A model registry is a versioned catalog — conceptually similar to a Maven artifact repository or a container image registry you already use — where every model, prompt template, and fine-tuned checkpoint that could reach production is registered with: a unique version identifier, its model card, its approval status (draft, in review, approved, deprecated, retired), the environments it is approved for, and a full history of who changed its status and when. The critical theoretical point: if a model artifact is not in the registry with an "approved" status, it structurally cannot be deployed — the deployment pipeline itself enforces this, the same way your CI/CD pipeline refuses to deploy an artifact that failed its build. Governance stops being a policy document nobody reads and becomes a gate the software itself enforces.

4.4 Human-in-the-Loop as a Governance Control, Not Just a UX Choice

Earlier volumes may have introduced human-in-the-loop patterns (approval steps before an agent takes a consequential action) as a reliability or safety mechanism. Governance reframes the same mechanism as a control: a documented, auditable checkpoint where a specific accountable human reviewed a specific AI-generated decision before it took effect. The distinction matters because a control must be provable after the fact — you need a log entry showing who clicked approve, when, and what exactly they were shown, not just a UI that happens to have a confirm button.

5. INTERNAL WORKING — HOW A GOVERNANCE PROGRAM ACTUALLY OPERATES

5.1 Step 1 — Risk Tiering at Intake

Before any code is written, a proposed AI use case is classified into a risk tier. This is typically driven by a short intake questionnaire answering: Does this system make or materially influence decisions about individuals (credit, employment, healthcare, legal outcomes)? Does it process sensitive personal data? Is there a human able to override its output before consequences occur, or is it fully autonomous? Is the user aware they are interacting with AI? The answers map to a tier — commonly labeled something like Minimal, Limited, High, and Unacceptable risk, deliberately mirroring the EU AI Act's own tiering scheme covered in the next chapter — and the tier determines everything downstream: how many approvers are required, how much documentation is mandatory, how frequently the system must be revalidated, and whether it can be built at all.

5.2 Step 2 — Design-Time Review

For anything above the lowest risk tier, a design review happens before significant engineering investment, not after. This catches problems cheaply: a proposed use case that turns out to require a protected-category inference the legal team will not approve, or a data source the system cannot legally use for this purpose, is far cheaper to redirect at the design stage than after a working prototype exists that everyone is emotionally invested in shipping.

5.3 Step 3 — Pre-Deployment Validation and Sign-Off

This is where the second line of defense (Section 4.1) does independent evaluation — not the same team that built the system re-running their own tests, but an independent reviewer checking the evaluation results (accuracy, fairness metrics across demographic slices where relevant, adversarial/red-team testing, as covered in this volume's Evaluation and Guardrails chapters) against the risk tier's required bar. Sign-off is recorded as a discrete, timestamped event against a specific model/prompt version in the registry — this is the artifact an auditor asks for eighteen months later, so it must be durable, not a Slack thread that ages out of retention.

5.4 Step 4 — Deployment Gate Enforcement

The deployment pipeline itself checks the registry before promoting an artifact: is this exact model version, with this exact prompt template hash, marked "approved" for this exact target environment? If not, the deploy fails automatically — mirroring how a Spring Boot CI/CD pipeline refuses to promote a build that failed its quality gate. This is the step that converts governance from a policy people are supposed to follow into a technical control people cannot bypass by accident, though it must always have a documented, logged emergency-override path for genuine incident response, because an inflexible gate invites people to route around it entirely.

5.5 Step 5 — Continuous Monitoring and Drift-Triggered Re-Review

Approval is not a one-time event. A model approved against a fairness and accuracy bar six months ago can degrade as real-world input distributions drift (a concern this volume's Observability chapter covers from the monitoring-mechanics side). Governance defines the trigger conditions — a metric crossing a threshold, a new class of complaint appearing, a scheduled periodic revalidation date — that automatically re-open the review process, moving the model's registry status back to "in review" and, depending on severity, forcing an automatic rollback to the last known-good version while re-review happens.

5.6 Step 6 — Incident Response and Post-Incident Governance Update

When something does go wrong — a hallucinated answer causes customer harm, a jailbreak succeeds, a fairness complaint is substantiated — governance defines who is notified, on what timeline, and requires a documented root-cause analysis that explicitly asks "did our governance controls fail, or did the system fail despite controls working correctly?" The answer usually updates the intake questionnaire, the required evaluation bar for that risk tier, or the monitoring thresholds — governance, done well, is a learning loop, not a static checklist.

5.7 Edge Cases Worth Reasoning Through Yourself

  • Shadow AI: a team quietly wires a third-party LLM API into a feature without going through intake at all. A governance program that relies purely on voluntary registration will miss this; mature programs pair policy with technical detection (API gateway egress monitoring for known LLM provider endpoints, cloud spend anomaly review) to find ungoverned usage.
  • Prompt-only changes: if the underlying model is unchanged but the system prompt is edited, does that require re-approval? Most mature frameworks say yes for anything above minimal risk, because prompt changes can materially change behavior (as this volume's Prompt Engineering coverage would predict) — yet many real programs get this wrong by gating only on model version, leaving prompt drift completely ungoverned.
  • Vendor model updates: your provider silently upgrades the model behind an API endpoint you call. Your registry entry for "gpt-4o" or an equivalent may now point at materially different underlying behavior without you changing a single line of code. Good governance pins model versions explicitly where the provider allows it, and treats provider-forced upgrades as a mandatory re-validation trigger, not a non-event.

6. MATHEMATICS

Governance is fundamentally an organizational and risk-management discipline, not a mathematical one, so this section stays deliberately light rather than manufacturing formalism that would not genuinely help you — consistent with this book's approach of skipping mathematics where the topic does not need it. The one piece of quantitative reasoning worth internalizing is how risk tiering is actually a simplified expected-harm calculation, because it explains why the tiers are structured the way they are and helps you defend tiering decisions in review.

Model the risk of a given AI use case, informally, as: 

Risk  ≈  Severity(harm if wrong)  ×  Likelihood(being wrong)  ×  Scale(number of people affected)
                                    ÷  Reversibility(how easily the harm can be undone)

This is the same expected-value-of-loss reasoning used in classical operational risk management, just applied to AI-specific failure modes. It explains several governance decisions that otherwise look arbitrary: a hallucination in a low-traffic internal tool used by five employees (low Scale) is tiered far below the same hallucination rate in a customer-facing system serving millions (high Scale). A wrong output that a human always reviews before it takes effect (high Reversibility, because the human catches it) is tiered lower than the identical model acting autonomously. A factual error in a chatbot's trivia answer (low Severity) is tiered far below a factual error in a system recommending medication dosages (high Severity). You do not need to compute this formally — no enterprise governance framework literally multiplies these numbers — but every intake questionnaire in Section 5.1 is implicitly probing exactly these four variables, and understanding that lets you correctly argue a tiering decision instead of just filling out a form by rote.

9. JAVA / SPRING PERSPECTIVE

Spring AI's existing abstractions give you natural seams to hang governance controls on, because the framework already separates orchestration concerns from the raw model call — exactly the seam governance needs.

9.1 A Governance Advisor

Spring AI's Advisor interface (used earlier in this series for memory, logging, and guardrail concerns) is the correct extension point for governance enforcement, because it wraps every ChatClient call without scattering approval-check logic through business code: 

@Component
public class GovernanceAdvisor implements CallAdvisor {

    private final ModelRegistryClient registry;
    private final AuditLogWriter auditLog;

    @Override
    public ChatClientResponse adviseCall(ChatClientRequest request,
                                          CallAdvisorChain chain) {

        String modelVersion = request.context().get("modelRegistryVersion");
        String promptHash   = request.context().get("promptTemplateHash");

        RegistryEntry entry = registry.lookup(modelVersion, promptHash);

        if (entry == null || entry.status() != ApprovalStatus.APPROVED) {
            auditLog.recordBlocked(modelVersion, promptHash, currentEnv());
            throw new UnapprovedModelException(
                "Model/prompt combination is not approved for " + currentEnv());
        }

        if (entry.riskTier() == RiskTier.HIGH && !request.context()
                .containsKey("humanApprovalToken")) {
            throw new HumanApprovalRequiredException(entry.id());
        }

        ChatClientResponse response = chain.nextCall(request);

        auditLog.recordCall(modelVersion, promptHash, entry.riskTier(),
                             currentUser(), Instant.now());

        return response;
    }
}

Note what this deliberately does not do: it does not decide policy. It enforces a decision already recorded in the registry — the same separation of concerns you would apply to an authorization filter that checks a permission the security team defined, rather than one that invents authorization rules inline.

9.2 The Registry Itself as a Spring-Managed Service

A model registry can be as simple as a versioned table (Spring Data JPA entity with status, riskTier, approvedBy, revalidateBy columns) backing a small internal service, or as sophisticated as integrating an existing MLOps platform's model registry via its REST API. Either way, the discipline that matters is treating registry entries as immutable, append-only records — an approval is never edited in place; a new status change is a new row referencing the previous one, mirroring event-sourcing patterns you likely already use for audit-sensitive domains like payments.

9.3 Structured Output and Human-Approval Gates

This volume's coverage of structured output constraints applies directly here: when a high-risk action requires human sign-off before execution, force the model to emit a structured, schema-validated proposal (using Spring AI's structured output support) rather than free text, so the human reviewer — and the audit log — sees an unambiguous, machine-checkable record of exactly what was approved, not a paraphrase.

10. BEST PRACTICES

  • Make governance metadata structural, not documentary. Registry version, approval status, and risk tier should be fields your code reads and enforces, not facts recorded in a wiki page that drifts out of sync with reality within weeks.
  • Tier risk before you build, not after. Retrofitting governance onto a system already in production is far more expensive and politically fraught than tiering it at intake, when redirecting the design is nearly free.
  • Name a single accountable owner per deployed AI system, by role, not by team. "The platform team" cannot be paged, cannot sign an approval, and disappears in a reorg; a named accountable role can.
  • Version prompts as rigorously as you version code. A prompt-only change can materially change model behavior; if your registry gates on model version alone, you have a governance gap you may not discover until an incident.
  • Treat the audit log as a security-critical, append-only system — the same durability and tamper-resistance guarantees you would demand of a financial ledger, because its entire value is being trustworthy after the fact, when someone has an incentive to dispute what happened.
  • Build a documented emergency-override path with mandatory after-the-fact review. A gate with no escape hatch gets bypassed outside the system entirely during a genuine incident; the fix is a logged, reviewed override, not the absence of a gate.

11. COMMON MISTAKES

  • Confusing a model card with marketing copy. A model card that omits known limitations and failure modes to look good in review defeats its entire purpose — the second and third lines of defense need the honest version, not the polished one.
  • Applying uniform maximum rigor to every AI feature. This predictably causes engineers to route around governance entirely for low-stakes features, which is worse than proportionate tiering that they actually follow.
  • Treating governance approval as a one-time gate with no revalidation trigger. Models and their surrounding data drift; a program with no periodic or drift-triggered re-review is one incident away from discovering it approved a system that stopped being safe months ago.
  • Letting "shadow AI" go undetected because governance relies purely on teams voluntarily submitting for review, with no technical detection (API gateway monitoring, cloud billing review) to catch usage that skipped the process.
  • Gating on model version but not prompt version. As covered in Section 5.7, this leaves an entire, common class of behavioral change completely outside the governance program's visibility.
  • Writing audit logs to a mutable store an engineer can edit. If an audit trail can be quietly altered after the fact, it provides no defensible evidence at all — it is worse than no audit trail, because it creates false confidence.

12. PERFORMANCE CONSIDERATIONS

Governance's performance footprint is usually small in absolute terms but must be engineered deliberately, because a naive implementation can silently become a latency or availability liability:

  • Registry lookups on the hot path. A synchronous network call to a remote registry service on every single chat request adds latency and a new failure dependency. Cache approval status locally with a short TTL and a fail-safe default (typically fail closed — block the call — for high-risk tiers, but consider fail-open with aggressive alerting for low-risk tiers where availability matters more than the marginal governance risk).
  • Human-approval checkpoints introduce real wall-clock latency — potentially minutes to hours, not milliseconds — so any user-facing flow with a mandatory human-in-the-loop step needs an asynchronous design (notify-and-resume, not block-and-wait) rather than holding an HTTP request open.
  • Audit log write volume scales with request volume, not with anything governance-specific; at high throughput this is a standard high-write-volume logging problem, best solved with the same asynchronous, batched writing patterns you would already use for high-volume application logs, not a bespoke synchronous write per request.
  • Revalidation cadence is a cost trade-off, not just a risk one — running a full independent evaluation suite (this volume's Evaluation chapters) monthly for every registered model at a large enterprise can itself become a significant compute and reviewer-time cost; the risk tiering from Section 5.1 should also drive revalidation frequency, not just initial approval rigor.

13. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

Governance and security overlap substantially, and each strengthens the other. The registry and audit trail are themselves security-sensitive assets: if an attacker (or a careless insider) can forge an "APPROVED" status in the registry, or edit an audit log entry after an incident, the entire governance program becomes theater rather than a real control — so the registry needs the same access-control rigor (least privilege, strong authentication, tamper-evident storage, ideally cryptographic signing of approval records) you would apply to a production secrets store. The human-approval checkpoint described in Sections 4.4 and 9.3 is also a genuine attack surface: if the identity behind an "approve" action is not strongly authenticated (Spring Security-backed, MFA-enforced for high-risk approvals), an attacker who compromises a low-privilege account could forge sign-off on a high-risk AI action. Beyond these two points, governance itself is not primarily a security discipline — it is worth being honest that most of its value is organizational accountability and regulatory defensibility, not a direct defense against the prompt-injection or data-exfiltration attack classes covered in this volume's dedicated Security chapters; treat this section as a reminder to secure the governance system itself, not a claim that governance secures your AI system's behavior.

14. INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

JUNIORQ1. What is a model card, and why would an engineer bother writing one?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

A model card is a structured document attached to a specific model version describing its intended use, what it should not be used for, what data it was trained/grounded on, its evaluation results, and known limitations. Engineers write them so that people who did not build the system — auditors, downstream teams, incident responders, risk committees — can evaluate whether it is safe and appropriate to use without reading the implementation.

JUNIORQ2. What is the difference between a code review and a model governance review?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

A code review checks whether the implementation is correct, maintainable, and follows engineering standards. A governance review checks whether the system is approved to exist at all for its intended purpose — evaluating risk tier, accountable ownership, data lineage, and compliance obligations — independently of whether the code itself is well-written. A system can pass code review and still be blocked by governance review, and vice versa.

MIDQ3. Explain the "three lines of defense" model and where a typical application engineering team sits within it.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

The first line is the team building and operating the system, owning risk day-to-day. The second line is an independent risk/compliance/governance function that sets policy and independently validates before deployment. The third line is internal audit, which verifies the first and second lines actually followed the process. Most application engineering teams, including Java/Spring teams building AI features, are the first line — their job is to build systems whose behavior and lineage are checkable by the second and third lines, not to self-certify their own compliance.

MIDQ4. Why should a model registry gate deployments on prompt version, not just model version?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Because a prompt change can materially change a model's behavior even when the underlying model is unchanged — the same way a configuration change can alter a deterministic system's behavior without a code deploy. If the registry only tracks and gates on model version, prompt changes bypass governance entirely, creating a real gap where an unreviewed, potentially unsafe prompt could reach production under an already-approved model.

SENIORQ5. Design a risk-tiering scheme for AI use cases at a mid-size enterprise. What factors would you use, and how would the tier affect engineering process?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Tier primarily on the combination of severity of potential harm, likelihood of error, scale of people affected, and reversibility (whether a human reviews output before consequences occur). A simple three- or four-tier scheme (e.g., Minimal, Limited, High, Unacceptable) maps cleanly onto process: Minimal risk needs lightweight self-certification only; Limited risk needs design review and basic evaluation sign-off; High risk needs independent second-line validation, mandatory human-in-the-loop for consequential actions, and periodic revalidation; Unacceptable risk use cases are not built at all. The scheme should be applied at intake, before significant engineering investment, and re-evaluated if the use case's scope changes.

SENIORQ6. Your team discovers another team quietly integrated a third-party LLM API into a customer-facing feature without going through the AI governance intake process. How do you respond, and how do you prevent recurrence?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Immediately assess actual risk exposure (what data reaches the third party, what decisions the output influences, current user impact) rather than treating it purely as a process violation — a genuinely high-risk shadow deployment may need immediate mitigation, such as pausing the feature, ahead of any process conversation. Bring the use case through intake retroactively rather than shutting it down reflexively if risk is low. To prevent recurrence, pair policy with technical detection: API gateway egress monitoring for known LLM provider endpoints, cloud billing anomaly alerts, and making the sanctioned path (an approved internal ChatModel/ChatClient integration with governance advisors already wired in) genuinely easier to use than going around it — friction-based prevention fails if the compliant path is harder than the shortcut.

Interview Gold Point: Explicitly note that the long-term fix is making the governed path the path of least resistance for engineers — a shared internal Spring AI starter with the Governance Advisor pre-wired — rather than relying purely on policy enforcement after the fact. Interviewers at staff level are listening for this systems-thinking move from "punish the violation" to "redesign the incentives."
STAFFQ7. A product leader argues that mandatory governance review is slowing AI feature delivery and should be relaxed for a fast-follow competitive launch. How do you respond as the engineer accountable for the platform?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Reframe the conversation around risk-proportionate process rather than a binary "governance on/off" choice: confirm the launch's actual risk tier using the criteria from Q5, and if it is genuinely low risk, show that the lightweight tier's process is already fast — the perceived slowness may be a symptom of a mis-tiered or overly uniform governance program rather than governance itself being the wrong idea. If the use case is genuinely high risk (customer financial or health decisions, for example), make the trade-off explicit and visible to leadership in business terms: shipping without independent validation converts an engineering risk into a legal, regulatory, and reputational one that the product leader, not just engineering, will own if it goes wrong. Offer to accelerate the review itself — pre-built evaluation harnesses, a standing risk-committee fast lane for well-documented submissions — rather than removing the review, which is almost always the actual bottleneck worth fixing.

STAFFQ8. How would you design a model registry and deployment gate so that governance is enforced structurally rather than relying on teams following policy voluntarily?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

The CI/CD pipeline itself queries the registry before promoting any model or prompt artifact to a given environment, and refuses to deploy anything not marked approved for that exact environment — mirroring how a quality gate blocks a failing build, so bypassing governance requires bypassing the deployment pipeline itself, not just skipping a form. Every application-level model call is wrapped by an advisor (in Spring AI, a custom Advisor) that independently re-checks registry status at call time and writes an immutable audit entry, so even an artifact deployed through an alternate path cannot silently serve traffic without leaving a trace. Approval status changes are append-only, tied to a strongly authenticated identity, and revalidation dates are enforced by automatically reverting status to "in review" when a due date passes, requiring active human action to keep an aging approval live. Finally, an emergency-override path exists but is itself logged, time-boxed, and forces a mandatory after-the-fact review, so the system remains usable during genuine incidents without becoming an unmonitored backdoor.

16. SUMMARY

  • Governance answers a different question than the rest of this volume: not "does the AI system work well" but "can the organization prove it exercised reasonable care before and during deployment" — a traceability and accountability problem, not an accuracy problem.
  • It descends directly from bank Model Risk Management and software change-management disciplines, re-applied to systems whose failure mode is probabilistic rather than a stack trace.
  • The core mechanisms are risk tiering at intake, model/system cards for documentation, a versioned model registry as an enforceable system of record, and human-in-the-loop checkpoints treated as auditable controls, not just UX.
  • Effective governance is proportionate to risk — uniform maximum rigor everywhere causes engineers to bypass the process, while no tiering fails to catch the systems that actually need scrutiny.
  • Spring AI's Advisor abstraction is the natural enforcement point for governance checks and audit logging, keeping policy decisions in a registry the advisor consults rather than hard-coded in business logic.
  • Governance must be structural, not voluntary: deployment pipelines that query the registry and fail closed convert policy into a technical control that cannot be quietly skipped under deadline pressure.
