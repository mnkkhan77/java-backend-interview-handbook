CHAPTER 72 — KUBERNETES FOR AI WORKLOADS

Volume 5 — Enterprise AI Engineering · Chapter 9 of 12

Scaling (Chapter 71) taught you what to scale — replicas, queues, autoscaling signals — but stopped short of the substrate that actually schedules those replicas onto machines, holds a GPU steady for a model server, and decides what happens when a pod serving live inference traffic suddenly dies. That substrate, for the overwhelming majority of production AI systems today, is Kubernetes. This chapter is where your Spring Boot deployment habits meet a scheduler that was designed for stateless web pods and now has to be persuaded, configured, and sometimes fought, into safely running GPU-bound, latency-sensitive, expensive AI workloads.

1. MOTIVATION

Every chapter in this volume so far has assumed a running platform underneath your application — something that keeps your Spring Boot service alive, restarts it when it crashes, and spreads traffic across instances. Up to now that platform could have been almost anything: a handful of EC2 instances behind a load balancer, an App Engine deployment, a PaaS. For AI workloads specifically, that assumption stops being safe. The moment your architecture includes a self-hosted embedding model, a fine-tuned Llama variant, a vector database with persistent state, or even just a fleet of stateless Spring AI services that need to autoscale sharply and safely around unpredictable LLM-call latency, you are running workloads with resource shapes and failure modes that generic PaaS abstractions were never designed for.

Kubernetes has become the default answer not because it is the simplest tool for the job, but because it is the only widely adopted platform that natively expresses the primitives AI workloads actually need: fine-grained, hardware-aware scheduling (a GPU is not a CPU core you can slice arbitrarily); the ability to describe long, cold-start-heavy container images loaded with multi-gigabyte model weights; readiness semantics that must account for a model server that takes ninety seconds to warm up before it can serve a single token; and horizontal/vertical scaling policies that must key off custom signals like queue depth and GPU utilization rather than plain CPU percentage. Every major inference-serving framework you will encounter later in this series — vLLM, Triton, TorchServe, Ray Serve — assumes it is being orchestrated by Kubernetes, and every managed AI platform (SageMaker, Vertex AI, Azure ML, KServe, Seldon) is, underneath its friendly API, a Kubernetes control plane wearing a costume.

For you, a Java/Spring engineer, the stakes are concrete and immediate. The Spring Boot service you build with ChatClient in front of a self-hosted model is not "done" when it compiles and passes its tests — it is done when it survives a node failure at 2 a.m. without dropping in-flight requests, scales out fast enough to absorb a traffic spike without your p99 latency exploding, and does not silently route requests to a GPU pod that has not finished loading its weights. None of that is Spring AI's job. All of it is Kubernetes' job, configured correctly by you. This chapter builds the mental model and the concrete manifests you need to do that correctly, rather than by trial and error in production.

2. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

2.1 Before Orchestration: Pets, Not Cattle

Before container orchestration existed as a discipline, deploying a machine-learning model into production typically meant provisioning a dedicated, hand-configured server — often a physical or virtual machine with a GPU attached, set up once by an ML engineer or ops specialist, and then treated as a fragile, irreplaceable "pet." If that box died, someone paged in the middle of the night to rebuild it by hand: reinstall CUDA drivers, reinstall the correct version of a deep learning framework, re-download model weights, and hope the configuration matched what was documented (it rarely fully did). Scaling out meant manually cloning that fragile setup, which was slow, error-prone, and expensive in engineering time. This is the same "pets vs. cattle" problem that drove general infrastructure toward containers and orchestration in the mid-2010s — AI infrastructure simply arrived at the same wall a few years later, once models grew large and expensive enough that treating GPU servers as disposable, identical, orchestrated units became economically necessary rather than optional.

2.2 Kubernetes' Own Origin, and Why It Wasn't Built for This

Kubernetes itself descends from Google's internal Borg system, open-sourced in 2014, and its founding design center was stateless, horizontally scalable web and batch workloads — think web servers, not GPU-bound model servers holding tens of gigabytes of weights in device memory. Early Kubernetes had no first-class concept of a GPU at all; a pod could request CPU and memory, full stop. GPU support arrived later, bolted on through a mechanism called device plugins (introduced around Kubernetes 1.8, roughly 2017) that let a vendor (NVIDIA, AMD) advertise specialized hardware to the scheduler as an opaque, non-shareable, non-fractional resource. This history matters because it explains a limitation you will hit directly: out of the box, Kubernetes treats a GPU as an all-or-nothing resource — one pod, one whole GPU — because the original design never anticipated needing to time-slice or fractionally share accelerators the way it already knew how to share CPU cores.

2.3 The Rise of AI-Native Layers on Top of Kubernetes (2018–present)

As GPU-bound and LLM-serving workloads became mainstream, the ecosystem responded not by replacing Kubernetes but by building specialized layers on top of it: Kubeflow for ML pipelines and training jobs, KServe (formerly KFServing) and Seldon Core for standardized model-serving abstractions, the NVIDIA GPU Operator to automate driver and device-plugin installation, and inference-specific serving engines like vLLM and Triton Inference Server that are typically deployed as Kubernetes Deployments with GPU resource requests. This "layers on top" pattern is the same evolutionary shape you have already seen in this series with Spring AI sitting on top of raw model provider SDKs — a general-purpose substrate gets specialized libraries and operators built on it rather than being replaced, because the underlying scheduling, networking, and lifecycle-management problems Kubernetes solves are still exactly the right problems to solve for AI workloads; only the resource model needed extension.

Knowing this saves you from assuming a missing feature is a misconfiguration you can fix; sometimes it is a genuine platform gap you must engineer around (Section 9 and 12 cover the concrete workarounds).

3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

Given a fleet of heterogeneous, expensive, and failure-prone compute nodes (some with GPUs, some without), reliably schedule, scale, network, and recover AI workloads — model servers, inference gateways, and the Spring AI services that call them — so that the system as a whole stays available and cost-efficient even as individual nodes, pods, and models fail or change.

Unpack the specific sub-problems hiding inside that sentence, because each one maps directly to a Kubernetes feature you will configure later in this chapter:

  • Placement: a pod that needs a GPU must land only on a node that has one, and ideally a node whose GPU has enough free memory for that specific model's weights and KV cache — a much sharper constraint than "any node with free CPU."
  • Slow, resource-heavy startup: a model server pulling a 15 GB container image and loading multi-gigabyte weights into GPU memory can take tens of seconds to minutes before it can serve a single request — traffic must not be routed to it before that point.
  • Asymmetric cost of failure: restarting a stateless Spring Boot pod is nearly free; restarting a GPU pod mid-inference wastes an expensive, scarce resource and can drop in-flight generations, so recovery policy must be tuned differently.
  • Non-CPU scaling signals: the thing that predicts whether you need more capacity is rarely CPU utilization for an LLM-serving pod — it is GPU utilization, request queue depth, or tokens-per-second throughput, none of which the default autoscaler understands without help.
  • Mixed-criticality traffic: a single platform typically runs cheap, latency-tolerant batch embedding jobs alongside expensive, latency-sensitive chat inference, and the scheduler needs to be told which is which so it does not starve the wrong one under resource pressure.

This is the precise problem this chapter solves: not "how do I run a container" (you already know that), but "how do I express AI-specific constraints and failure semantics in a system whose native vocabulary was built for stateless CPU workloads."

4. THEORY

4.1 The Core Mental Model: Declarative Desired State, Reconciled Continuously

If you take away exactly one idea from Kubernetes theory, make it this one: you never tell Kubernetes what to do — you tell it what should be true, and a set of tireless control loops continuously compares that description against reality and takes action to close the gap. This is a sharp departure from the imperative scripts you may have used to deploy a Spring Boot app in the past ("SSH in, stop the old jar, copy the new jar, start it"). You instead write a YAML manifest declaring "there should be 3 running copies of this container, each with 1 GPU and 16 GB of memory," submit it to the API server, and a component called the controller manager spends forever afterward making that statement true — creating pods when there are too few, deleting them when there are too many, rescheduling them when a node dies.

This "declare desired state, reconcile continuously" loop is called the control loop pattern, and it is the single mechanism underneath almost everything Kubernetes does: Deployments reconcile replica count, the Horizontal Pod Autoscaler reconciles replica count against a metric target, the scheduler reconciles "pending pods" against "nodes with capacity." Once this clicks, most of Kubernetes stops looking like a grab-bag of YAML kinds and starts looking like the same idea, applied repeatedly, to different resources.

4.2 The Object Hierarchy You Actually Need for AI Workloads

Analogy first: think of a Pod as a single running instance of your process — the AI-workload equivalent of one JVM instance running your Spring Boot jar, plus any tightly-coupled sidecar containers (a metrics exporter, a model-loading init container). A Pod is deliberately ephemeral and disposable; you almost never create one directly in production. A Deployment is the durable declaration "keep N healthy copies of this Pod template running at all times" — it is what actually reconciles replica count, performs rolling updates, and replaces failed Pods. A Service is a stable network identity and load-balancing rule in front of a changing set of Pod IPs — because Pod IPs are not stable (a rescheduled Pod gets a new IP), your Spring AI client should never talk to a Pod directly, only to a Service.

On top of that base triad, AI workloads specifically add: a StatefulSet instead of a Deployment when Pods need stable identity and durable per-instance storage (a vector database shard, a model cache volume that should survive a restart of that specific instance); a Job or CronJob for one-shot or scheduled work like a nightly batch embedding pipeline or a fine-tuning run, which is fundamentally different from a Deployment because it is expected to finish, not run forever; and a Custom Resource Definition (CRD), which is how AI-native operators like KServe extend Kubernetes' vocabulary with new kinds such as InferenceService that bundle Deployment, Service, autoscaling, and traffic-splitting concerns into one AI-shaped object.

4.3 Requests, Limits, and Why GPUs Break the Usual Rules

Every container in Kubernetes states two numbers per resource type: a request (the amount it is guaranteed, used by the scheduler to decide placement) and a limit (the hard ceiling it may never exceed, enforced by the kernel/runtime). For CPU and memory, requests can be fractional and the scheduler can pack many pods with modest requests onto one node, over-committing lightly and relying on the kernel to time-slice CPU fairly. GPUs do not work this way by default: a GPU is requested as a whole integer (nvidia.com/gpu: 1), it cannot be fractionally requested out of the box, and the Kubernetes scheduler treats it as an opaque, indivisible resource it does not understand the internals of — it just marks it consumed. This single asymmetry — CPU is a shareable, divisible resource; a GPU by default is not — is the theoretical root of nearly every "GPU utilization is embarrassingly low" complaint in real AI platforms, and Section 12 covers the concrete mitigations (MIG, time-slicing, batching).

5. INTERNAL WORKING

Walk through what actually happens, mechanically, from the moment you submit a manifest for a GPU-backed model-serving Deployment to the moment it is safely serving traffic — because the edge cases live in this sequence, not in the YAML syntax.

  1. Submission: you run kubectl apply -f model-server.yaml. The manifest is sent to the API server, validated, and persisted into etcd (Kubernetes' distributed key-value store, the single source of truth for all cluster state).
  2. Deployment controller reacts: a watch-loop notices a new Deployment object exists with zero matching Pods, and creates the requested number of Pod objects (still unscheduled — no node assigned yet), each carrying the resource request nvidia.com/gpu: 1.
  3. Scheduling: the scheduler watches for Pods with no assigned node. For each pending Pod it filters nodes by hard constraints (does this node have a free, schedulable GPU? does it satisfy any nodeSelector/affinity rules you wrote, like "only nodes labeled gpu-type=a100"?), then scores the remaining candidates (spread pods across nodes/zones, prefer bin-packing or spreading depending on configured priorities) and binds the Pod to the winning node.
  4. Kubelet takes over: the kubelet daemon on the chosen node sees a new Pod bound to it, pulls the container image (this step alone can take minutes for a multi-gigabyte AI serving image — a real, frequently underestimated source of slow rollouts), and asks the container runtime to start the container.
  5. Device plugin allocation: because the container requested a GPU, the kubelet consults the NVIDIA device plugin (a special pod running on that node that advertises available GPU units to the kubelet) to get the specific device ID, and injects the right environment variables/device files so the container can actually see and use that physical GPU.
  6. Container starts, but is not yet "ready": the process inside the container starts — for an inference server this typically means: initialize the runtime, load model weights from disk or a mounted volume into GPU memory, warm up any JIT-compiled kernels. This can take anywhere from a few seconds to several minutes depending on model size.
  7. Readiness probing gates traffic: Kubernetes will not send Service traffic to this Pod until its configured readiness probe succeeds (commonly an HTTP health endpoint that only returns 200 once weights are fully loaded). This is the single most important, most frequently misconfigured mechanism for AI workloads — get it wrong and users get routed to a pod that is still loading, receiving connection resets or multi-minute hangs.
  8. Liveness probing guards against silent hangs: separately, a liveness probe continuously checks whether the process is still functioning at all; if it fails repeatedly, the kubelet kills and restarts the container — useful for catching a model server wedged in a deadlock, but dangerous if configured too aggressively against a server that is merely slow under heavy load (Section 11 covers this exact trap).
  9. Ongoing reconciliation: from this point on, the Deployment controller, the Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (if configured), and the node's own health monitoring continue running their independent control loops indefinitely, reacting to node failures, scale-metric changes, or manual scaling commands — nothing about this is a one-shot deployment event; it never stops running.

Every one of these nine steps is a place where a generic, copy-pasted Kubernetes manifest silently does the wrong thing for an AI workload: default readiness probes assume near-instant startup, default rolling-update strategy assumes replacing a pod is cheap, default scheduling has no concept of "this GPU already has another model loaded and cannot fit a second." The remainder of this chapter is about closing those gaps deliberately rather than discovering them in an incident.

6. MATHEMATICS

Kubernetes for AI workloads is overwhelmingly a systems and control-theory topic rather than a mathematically dense one, so this section stays deliberately light — the goal is intuition for the one piece of math that actually drives operational decisions: autoscaling as a feedback control loop.

The Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA) computes a desired replica count from a simple ratio-based formula: 

desiredReplicas = ceil( currentReplicas × ( currentMetricValue / desiredMetricValue ) )

Example — scaling a Spring AI inference gateway on request queue depth:
  currentReplicas      = 4
  currentMetricValue   = 40   (average pending requests per pod, observed)
  desiredMetricValue   = 10   (target pending requests per pod, configured)

  desiredReplicas = ceil( 4 × (40 / 10) ) = ceil(16) = 16

The intuition matters more than the formula: this is a proportional controller — it scales replicas in direct proportion to how far the current metric is from target, the same "how far off am I, correct proportionally" logic you may already know from PID-style feedback loops in other systems. The practical trap is entirely about signal choice and latency, not the arithmetic: if the metric you feed this formula (say, CPU) does not actually correlate with the thing you care about (GPU saturation, or LLM latency), the formula will confidently compute a precisely wrong answer. Equally important is the lag baked into the loop — metrics are typically scraped and averaged over a window (commonly 15–60 seconds), and a new replica for a GPU-backed model server can take minutes to become ready (Section 5's startup sequence), so the effective response time of this "instant-looking" formula can be several minutes end to end. Designing for that lag — over-provisioning a buffer, or scaling on a leading indicator like queue depth rather than a lagging one like current latency — is the real engineering skill; the formula itself is almost incidental.

8. PRODUCTION ARCHITECTURE

A production Kubernetes cluster serving real AI traffic is never one homogeneous pool of identical nodes — it is deliberately partitioned by hardware and workload criticality, and the manifests below are the concrete, minimal shape of that partitioning.

First, keep GPU nodes reserved for workloads that actually need them, using a taint on the node and a matching toleration on the Pod — without this, the scheduler is free to pack ordinary CPU-only pods onto your expensive GPU nodes, wasting the very resource you provisioned them for:

# Applied once to every GPU node (e.g., via node pool config or kubectl taint)
kubectl taint nodes gpu-node-1 workload=gpu-inference:NoSchedule

apiVersion: apps/v1
kind: Deployment
metadata:
  name: vllm-model-server
  labels:
    app: vllm-model-server
spec:
  replicas: 2
  selector:
    matchLabels:
      app: vllm-model-server
  template:
    metadata:
      labels:
        app: vllm-model-server
    spec:
      tolerations:
        - key: "workload"
          operator: "Equal"
          value: "gpu-inference"
          effect: "NoSchedule"
      nodeSelector:
        gpu-type: "a100"
      containers:
        - name: vllm
          image: registry.internal/vllm-server:2.3.0
          resources:
            requests:
              nvidia.com/gpu: 1
              memory: "32Gi"
              cpu: "4"
            limits:
              nvidia.com/gpu: 1
              memory: "40Gi"
              cpu: "8"
          readinessProbe:
            httpGet:
              path: /health/ready
              port: 8000
            initialDelaySeconds: 30
            periodSeconds: 10
            failureThreshold: 18   # allow up to 3 minutes of warm-up
          livenessProbe:
            httpGet:
              path: /health/live
              port: 8000
            initialDelaySeconds: 60
            periodSeconds: 20
            failureThreshold: 3
---
apiVersion: v1
kind: Service
metadata:
  name: model-server
spec:
  selector:
    app: vllm-model-server
  ports:
    - port: 80
      targetPort: 8000

Notice three deliberate choices that a copy-pasted generic Deployment would get wrong: the toleration + nodeSelector pair pins this workload precisely to tainted GPU nodes labeled for the right accelerator type; the readiness probe's generous failureThreshold gives the model server minutes, not seconds, to load weights before being marked unready, directly reflecting the startup sequence from Section 5; and requests equal to limits for the GPU is not optional the way it might be for CPU — Kubernetes requires GPU requests and limits to match exactly, because GPUs cannot be overcommitted or throttled the way CPU can.

Sitting in front of this, the Spring Boot application tier runs as an entirely separate, much more elastic Deployment with no GPU dependency at all, scaled by its own HorizontalPodAutoscaler keyed on request concurrency rather than CPU, and reaching the model tier purely through the stable model-server Service DNS name — it never needs to know how many replicas exist, which node they are on, or that a GPU is involved at all.

9. JAVA / SPRING PERSPECTIVE

The good news for you specifically: almost none of Kubernetes' AI-specific complexity needs to leak into your Spring AI code. A well-designed Spring Boot service treats the model tier exactly the way it already treats any other network dependency — through a ChatModel implementation configured with a base URL, which in a Kubernetes-hosted setup is simply the internal Service DNS name from Section 8: 

spring:
  ai:
    openai:
      base-url: http://model-server.default.svc.cluster.local
      chat:
        options:
          model: local-vllm-model

@Service
public class InferenceService {

    private final ChatClient chatClient;

    public InferenceService(ChatClient.Builder builder) {
        this.chatClient = builder.build();
    }

    public String generate(String userMessage) {
        return chatClient.prompt()
                .user(userMessage)
                .call()
                .content();
    }
}

Nothing about this code changes whether the model server behind that URL is a single Docker container on your laptop or a fleet of GPU pods behind a Kubernetes Service — that decoupling is precisely the point of the ChatModel/ChatClient abstraction discussed earlier in this series, and Kubernetes is simply the mechanism that makes the right-hand side of that URL reliable at scale.

Where Kubernetes does become directly relevant to your Spring Boot configuration is in three places you should treat as first-class production concerns, not afterthoughts: first, graceful shutdown — set server.shutdown=graceful and a sensible spring.lifecycle.timeout-per-shutdown-phase so that when Kubernetes sends a Pod a SIGTERM during a rolling update or scale-down, in-flight requests (including ones waiting on a slow LLM call) finish before the process exits, rather than being cut off mid-response. Second, Spring Boot Actuator health endpoints should be wired directly into your Kubernetes readiness and liveness probes — Spring Boot ships dedicated /actuator/health/readiness and /actuator/health/liveness groups specifically designed for this integration, and your readiness check should genuinely verify the downstream model server is reachable, not just that the JVM is up, otherwise Kubernetes will happily route traffic to a Spring AI pod that cannot actually reach its model. Third, resource requests/limits on the Spring Boot container itself should be set with JVM heap sizing in mind — an undersized memory limit combined with a JVM that does not respect container cgroup limits (rare on modern JDKs, but worth verifying) is a classic cause of pods being OOMKilled under load that looks, from the outside, like a Kubernetes bug rather than a JVM configuration gap.

10. BEST PRACTICES

  • Separate GPU and non-GPU workloads into distinct node pools with taints/tolerations from day one. Retrofitting this separation after a cluster is already mixed is far more disruptive than designing it in up front.
  • Size readiness probes around your actual model load time, measured, not guessed. Time a cold start end to end and set initialDelaySeconds/failureThreshold with real headroom — a probe that fires before the model can respond causes flapping restarts that make the real problem (slow startup) look like an unrelated crash loop.
  • Scale the application tier and the model-serving tier independently, with independent autoscalers keyed on the right signal each. Spring AI gateway pods scale well on concurrency/CPU; GPU model servers should scale on GPU utilization or request queue depth, exposed via a custom metrics adapter feeding the HPA.
  • Use PodDisruptionBudgets on GPU-backed Deployments so voluntary disruptions (node upgrades, cluster autoscaler scale-downs) never take down every replica of a model server simultaneously.
  • Pin exact GPU driver and CUDA library versions across the cluster (typically via the NVIDIA GPU Operator) rather than letting nodes drift — a driver mismatch between nodes is one of the most time-consuming classes of "works on one node, not the other" incidents in AI-on-Kubernetes deployments.
  • Set requests and limits identically for GPUs, and set memory limits generously for model containers. An OOMKilled model server mid-generation is a worse user experience than a slightly over-provisioned node.

11. COMMON MISTAKES

  • Copy-pasting a generic web-app readiness/liveness probe configuration onto a model server. Default probe timings assume near-instant startup; applied unchanged to a GPU pod loading weights, this produces an endless restart loop the moment the Deployment is applied — the pod never gets a chance to finish loading before being killed and restarted.
  • Setting a liveness probe timeout too aggressively for a busy inference server. A model server under heavy load can be alive and correctly processing requests but momentarily slow to answer a health check; an overly strict liveness probe kills and restarts a perfectly healthy, simply busy pod, making load-induced slowness worse, not better.
  • Forgetting that GPUs cannot be overcommitted like CPU. Setting a GPU request lower than the limit (or omitting the limit) is invalid or misleading for GPU resources — teams used to CPU/memory overcommit patterns bring that instinct here and are surprised when it does not apply.
  • Not isolating GPU nodes, letting the scheduler pack arbitrary CPU-only pods onto them. This silently wastes the most expensive resource in the cluster and can even leave a GPU node without enough CPU/memory headroom left for the GPU pod it was provisioned for.
  • Autoscaling a model-serving Deployment on CPU utilization. A GPU-bound inference server can be fully saturated on the GPU while its CPU sits nearly idle — a CPU-based HPA target will simply never trigger a scale-out when it is needed most.
  • Ignoring image pull time in rollout planning. Multi-gigabyte AI serving images pulled fresh on every new node can make a rolling update or scale-out take many minutes longer than expected; pre-pulling images or using a private, geographically close registry mitigates this.

12. PERFORMANCE CONSIDERATIONS

Three performance dimensions dominate AI-on-Kubernetes decisions, and all three trace back to the "GPUs are indivisible by default" limitation established in Section 4.3.

GPU utilization and fractional sharing. Because a default GPU request consumes an entire device, a lightly-loaded model (one that doesn't need a full A100's throughput) still ties up the whole card, and utilization dashboards across a cluster of "one workload per GPU" often reveal embarrassingly low average utilization. Two mitigations exist, each with real trade-offs: NVIDIA Multi-Instance GPU (MIG), which physically partitions a supported GPU into several fully isolated, independently schedulable smaller GPUs at the hardware level (strong isolation, but a fixed, coarse set of partition sizes decided at boot); and time-slicing, which lets multiple pods share one GPU by taking turns on the same physical device (finer-grained sharing, but no memory isolation — one workload can starve another or even crash it by exhausting shared GPU memory). Choosing between them is a direct latency-isolation-versus-utilization trade-off you should make deliberately, not by default.

Cold start versus steady-state latency. The gap between "a GPU pod exists" and "a GPU pod can serve a request" (Section 5, steps 4–7) can be the dominant contributor to tail latency under bursty traffic if your autoscaler is reactive rather than anticipatory. Mitigations used in real systems include keeping a small warm pool of always-ready replicas sized above the autoscaler's minimum, using node-level GPU pre-warming so newly scheduled pods land on nodes that already have drivers and cached images ready, and preferring scale-up signals with lead time (queue depth trending upward) over lagging signals (current p99 latency, which by definition only degrades after the problem has already started).

Cost. GPU nodes are frequently the single largest line item in an AI platform's infrastructure bill, often by an order of magnitude over the CPU tier serving the same traffic. This makes the Section 10 practice of scaling GPU and non-GPU tiers independently a direct cost lever, not just an architectural nicety: an idle or over-provisioned GPU node pool burns money continuously whether or not it is serving traffic, which is why aggressive, well-tuned scale-to-zero or scale-to-minimum policies on the GPU tier — accepting some cold-start latency in exchange — are common in cost-sensitive production deployments, and why request batching at the inference-server level (covered in a later chapter) matters as much for cost as for throughput.

13. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

Kubernetes for AI workloads inherits every standard Kubernetes security concern in full — namespace isolation, RBAC scoping, network policies, image provenance, secrets management for API keys used by ChatModel implementations calling external providers — none of that is optional or reduced just because the workload happens to be AI-related, and this chapter will not repeat general Kubernetes hardening guidance covered elsewhere in this series. Two considerations are, however, specific enough to AI workloads to call out directly here. First, GPU device access is a privileged capability: a container with device-plugin-granted GPU access typically also gets broader access to that device's driver interfaces than a typical containerized workload would need for an equivalent CPU task, so GPU-enabled pods deserve the same scrutiny you would give any pod requesting elevated host-level access — restrict which namespaces/service accounts may even request GPU resources via admission policy, rather than trusting every workload equally. Second, model artifacts and weights pulled at container startup are a supply-chain surface exactly like any other dependency — a model server pulling weights from an untrusted or unpinned source at Pod startup is functionally equivalent to pulling an unpinned third-party library, and the same provenance and pinning discipline you apply to container images should apply to the model weights and tokenizer files a Pod downloads on its way to becoming ready.

14. INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

JUNIORQ1. What is the difference between a Kubernetes Pod, a Deployment, and a Service?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

A Pod is a single running instance of one or more tightly-coupled containers — the smallest deployable unit, and inherently ephemeral. A Deployment is a controller that declares and maintains a desired number of healthy Pod replicas over time, handling replacement and rolling updates automatically. A Service is a stable network endpoint (virtual IP and DNS name) that load-balances traffic across the current, changing set of Pods matching a label selector, so clients never need to track individual Pod IPs directly.

JUNIORQ2. Why can't you just request "half a GPU" the way you can request half a CPU core in Kubernetes?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

By default, Kubernetes exposes GPUs through vendor device plugins as opaque, indivisible integer resources — the scheduler has no built-in concept of fractional GPU allocation the way it does for CPU, which the kernel can time-slice cleanly. Fractional or shared GPU access requires additional mechanisms layered on top, such as NVIDIA MIG (hardware-level partitioning) or GPU time-slicing (software-level sharing without memory isolation).

MIDQ3. Walk through what happens between running `kubectl apply` on a GPU-backed Deployment and that pod actually being able to serve traffic.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

The manifest is validated and persisted to etcd via the API server; the Deployment controller creates the requested number of Pod objects; the scheduler filters nodes by GPU availability and any affinity/taint rules, then binds each Pod to a chosen node; the kubelet on that node pulls the container image, requests a specific GPU device from the device plugin, and starts the container; the process loads model weights into GPU memory; and only once the configured readiness probe starts succeeding does the Service begin routing traffic to that Pod. Before that point the Pod exists and is running but receives zero traffic.

MIDQ4. Why is a naive, copy-pasted readiness/liveness probe configuration dangerous for a model-serving pod specifically?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Typical default probe timings assume near-instant startup, which is true for a simple web service but false for a model server that must load multi-gigabyte weights into GPU memory before it can respond at all — applying defaults unchanged causes the readiness probe to keep failing during legitimate warm-up, and an aggressive liveness probe can even kill and restart the pod before it finishes loading, producing an endless crash loop that looks like the application is broken when it is actually the probe timing that's wrong.

SENIORQ5. Your Horizontal Pod Autoscaler is configured on CPU utilization for a GPU-backed inference Deployment, and it never scales out even during periods when users report high latency. Diagnose this.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

An inference server bottlenecked on GPU compute or GPU memory bandwidth can be fully saturated on the GPU while CPU usage remains low, since the CPU is mostly idle waiting on GPU work — CPU utilization simply does not correlate with the actual bottleneck for this workload shape, so the HPA's scaling condition never triggers even under real load. The fix is to autoscale on a metric that actually reflects saturation for this workload: GPU utilization exposed via a custom metrics pipeline (e.g., DCGM exporter feeding a Prometheus adapter into the HPA), or an application-level leading indicator like request queue depth or in-flight request count, ideally combined so the system reacts before latency has already degraded.

SENIORQ6. How would you design node pool and scheduling policy so that expensive GPU nodes are never wasted on non-AI workloads, without manually babysitting pod placement?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Apply a taint to every GPU node (e.g., workload=gpu-inference:NoSchedule) so no pod is scheduled there by default, and add a matching toleration only to Deployments that genuinely require a GPU, combined with a nodeSelector or node affinity rule targeting the correct GPU type label. This makes GPU-node placement an explicit opt-in rather than something the scheduler could do accidentally, and it composes cleanly with cluster-autoscaler node pools so GPU capacity scales independently of the general-purpose pool.

Interview Gold Point: Add that requests and limits for GPU resources must be set equal, since GPUs cannot be overcommitted or throttled the way CPU can — mentioning this shows you understand the resource model at a level beyond copy-pasted YAML, and it directly explains why "just lower the request a bit for better packing," a common CPU/memory optimization instinct, does not apply to GPU scheduling at all.
STAFFQ7. A platform team wants to run mixed workloads — a latency-sensitive chat-serving tier and a cost-sensitive nightly batch embedding job — on the same Kubernetes cluster's GPU capacity. What architecture would you propose, and what failure mode are you specifically protecting against?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Separate the two workloads by PriorityClass and, ideally, by distinct node pools or GPU partitioning (MIG) rather than letting them contend freely for the same devices — the chat-serving Deployment should carry a high priority class so that, under resource pressure, the scheduler preempts lower-priority batch Jobs rather than starving user-facing latency-sensitive traffic. The specific failure mode being protected against is priority inversion: without an explicit priority scheme, a large batch embedding Job submitted at the wrong moment can consume all available GPU capacity and leave a latency-critical chat-serving Deployment unable to scale out exactly when user traffic spikes, since Kubernetes' default scheduler has no inherent notion that one workload matters more than another unless you tell it so via PriorityClass and, if needed, resource quotas per namespace.

STAFFQ8. Why do most managed "AI platform" products (SageMaker endpoints, Vertex AI, KServe) look, underneath, like a thin API wrapped around a Kubernetes control plane, and what does that imply for a platform team deciding build-vs-buy?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

Kubernetes already solves the hard, general problems these platforms need — declarative desired-state reconciliation, hardware-aware scheduling, rolling updates, autoscaling control loops, service discovery — so building a competing orchestration layer from scratch would mean re-solving problems Kubernetes has already spent a decade hardening. Managed platforms instead standardize the AI-specific vocabulary on top (model versions, traffic splitting between model versions, endpoint autoscaling policies) and let Kubernetes do the underlying scheduling and lifecycle work, typically via CRDs like KServe's InferenceService. For a platform team, this means the real build-vs-buy decision is rarely "Kubernetes or not" — it is whether to adopt an existing AI-native CRD/operator layer (KServe, Seldon) on top of Kubernetes you already operate, versus building a comparable abstraction in-house, versus paying a managed provider to operate both layers; understanding that Kubernetes is the common foundation underneath all three options reframes the decision around operational ownership and vendor lock-in rather than around raw technical capability, which is largely equivalent across all three.

MIDQ9. What role does the NVIDIA device plugin actually play in making a GPU schedulable by Kubernetes?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Kubernetes has no native understanding of GPUs at all — the device plugin framework is the extension point that teaches it. The NVIDIA device plugin runs on every GPU node, discovers the physical GPUs present, and advertises them to the kubelet as an "extended resource" (e.g. nvidia.com/gpu: 4), which the API server then exposes as a schedulable, allocatable quantity on that Node object. Without the plugin running and healthy, the node reports zero allocatable GPUs and the scheduler will never place a GPU-requesting pod there, even though the hardware is physically installed.

MIDQ10. The NVIDIA device plugin is deployed as a DaemonSet rather than a Deployment. Why does that matter?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

A DaemonSet guarantees exactly one copy runs on every node matching its selector, automatically, as nodes join or leave the cluster — which is precisely the semantics device advertisement needs: each GPU node must independently report its own hardware to its own kubelet, and a Deployment's arbitrary N-replica model with no per-node guarantee would leave newly added GPU nodes with no plugin running and therefore zero advertised GPUs until someone noticed and intervened.

MIDQ11. What problem does Node Feature Discovery (NFD) solve for GPU clusters, and how does it interact with scheduling?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A cluster with mixed GPU generations (say, some A100 nodes and some H100 nodes) needs a way to route specific workloads to specific hardware, but Kubernetes labels don't appear on nodes automatically. NFD runs on each node, detects hardware and driver characteristics, and writes standardized labels (GPU model, driver version, CUDA capability, and similar) onto the Node object. Deployments can then use nodeAffinity against those labels to pin a workload that needs, say, 80GB of HBM to H100 nodes specifically, instead of relying on manual labeling that drifts out of sync as the cluster grows.

MIDQ12. What does the NVIDIA GPU Operator actually bundle, and why do most platform teams adopt it instead of installing GPU support by hand?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Getting a node GPU-ready by hand means installing the correct kernel driver version, the NVIDIA container toolkit, the device plugin, NFD, a DCGM metrics exporter, and keeping all of them in version lockstep across every node — a maintenance burden that scales badly. The GPU Operator packages all of these components as a set of cooperating Kubernetes-native controllers, so provisioning a new GPU node becomes "join the cluster, get labeled, get the right driver and toolkit installed automatically" rather than a manual per-node runbook, and upgrades are handled as a single coordinated rollout instead of N separate SSH sessions.

SENIORQ13. Compare NVIDIA MIG and GPU time-slicing as approaches to sharing a single physical GPU across multiple pods. When would you pick each?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

MIG (Multi-Instance GPU, available on A100/H100-class hardware) physically partitions a GPU into isolated instances at the hardware level, each with its own dedicated slice of compute, memory, and memory bandwidth — so one tenant's workload genuinely cannot starve or interfere with another's, at the cost of only supporting a handful of fixed partition profiles and losing access to the full GPU's memory for any single instance. Time-slicing instead lets multiple pods share the same GPU by having the driver context-switch between them, with no hardware isolation at all — any pod can still consume the full memory pool and starve others under contention. MIG is the right call for multi-tenant production isolation (different teams or customers on shared capacity); time-slicing suits development/testing clusters or workloads you already know are individually low-utilization and trust not to contend badly.

SENIORQ14. What is the specific risk of using GPU time-slicing for a production multi-tenant inference cluster?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Because time-slicing shares the physical device without memory isolation, one tenant's pod can allocate enough GPU memory to push another tenant's pod into an out-of-memory condition, and a compute-heavy tenant can materially degrade another's latency even though both pods appear, from Kubernetes' perspective, to each hold their own "GPU" resource. The scheduler's resource accounting gives a false sense of isolation that the hardware does not actually provide.

Interview Gold Point: Bring up that this is exactly the gap MIG was built to close — noting the trade-off explicitly (isolation vs. flexible fractional sizing) signals you understand GPU sharing is a spectrum of options with real trade-offs, not a solved problem with one obviously-correct answer.
STAFFQ15. Under the hood, how does the device plugin API let Kubernetes allocate a specific physical GPU to a specific pod, given that "extended resources" are otherwise just opaque integer counts to the scheduler?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

The scheduler itself only ever reasons about the integer count — it decrements the node's allocatable nvidia.com/gpu quantity and binds the pod, with no knowledge of which physical device index is involved. The actual device selection happens locally: the kubelet calls the device plugin's gRPC Allocate API with the number of devices requested, the plugin picks specific device IDs from its internal free list, and returns the environment variables and device file mounts (like /dev/nvidia0) needed to expose exactly those devices inside the container. This split — scheduler-level counting versus kubelet-level device plugin allocation — is why device plugins can implement sharing schemes like MIG profiles or time-slicing entirely below the scheduler's visibility, without any core Kubernetes changes.

SENIORQ16. What does the NVIDIA Container Toolkit actually do, and why can't a GPU-requesting pod just run on a stock container runtime?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A standard OCI container runtime has no concept of GPU devices — it can bind-mount arbitrary files, but it doesn't know which device nodes, driver libraries, and CUDA userspace components a given container needs to actually talk to the GPU. The NVIDIA Container Toolkit installs a runtime hook (nvidia-container-runtime) that intercepts container creation, inspects the requested GPU allocation from the device plugin, and injects the correct device files, driver shared libraries, and environment variables into the container at creation time — matching the driver version on the host to what the container expects. Without it, the container might start but crash immediately with a driver/library mismatch or simply be unable to see any GPU device at all.

SENIORQ17. A pod is scheduled successfully onto a GPU node, gets a GPU resource allocated, and starts running — but the application inside logs "no CUDA-capable device is detected." Where do you look first?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

This is the classic split-brain symptom of the scheduler-level/kubelet-level allocation gap: the pod being scheduled proves the extended-resource count was available, but it says nothing about whether the device was actually correctly exposed inside the container. The first things to check are a driver/CUDA-toolkit version mismatch between what's installed on the host and what the application's base image expects, whether the NVIDIA Container Toolkit runtime class is actually being used for that pod (a missing runtimeClassName: nvidia or misconfigured default runtime silently falls back to running the container with no GPU passthrough at all), and whether the device plugin itself is reporting healthy — a device plugin that's crash-looping can leave stale allocations that no longer map to a real, healthy GPU.

MIDQ18. You run three different GPU types (say, T4, A100, and H100) across your cluster for different cost/performance tiers. How do you make sure a workload lands on the right one?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Extended resource counts alone can't distinguish GPU type — a pod requesting nvidia.com/gpu: 1 is happy to land on any node with a free GPU slot, T4 or H100. The fix is combining node labels (either manually applied or, better, generated by Node Feature Discovery) that capture the GPU model with a nodeAffinity or nodeSelector rule in the Deployment spec that pins it to the correct label value, plus a matching taint/toleration pair per GPU tier if you want to actively prevent workloads that didn't specify a preference from landing on your most expensive nodes by accident.

SENIORQ19. What extra care does running GPU workloads on spot/preemptible nodes require, beyond what you'd do for stateless CPU workloads on spot capacity?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

GPU capacity is scarcer and more contended than general-purpose compute, so spot reclaim events tend to be more frequent and the replacement capacity less immediately available — a naive setup can end up GPU-starved for longer stretches than a CPU-only spot pool would be. Beyond the standard spot hygiene (handling the termination notice gracefully, running a PodDisruptionBudget so reclaim doesn't take out every replica at once), GPU-specific practice usually means reserving a smaller baseline of on-demand GPU capacity for latency-critical serving traffic and routing only cost-tolerant, restart-safe workloads (batch inference, checkpointed training) onto the spot GPU pool, tainted separately from the on-demand pool so nothing latency-sensitive lands there by scheduling accident.

SENIORQ20. How do you safely drain a GPU node that's running long-lived model-serving pods, without dropping in-flight requests?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium

The core mechanism is the same as any graceful rollout — kubectl drain triggers pod eviction, which sends SIGTERM and starts the terminationGracePeriodSeconds countdown, during which the Service should stop routing new traffic to the pod (once it fails readiness or the endpoint is removed) while existing in-flight requests finish. The GPU-specific wrinkle is that the grace period needs to be sized generously enough for the *longest* request the server might be mid-way through — a long generation on a large model can run tens of seconds, so a default 30-second grace period silently truncates responses — and a PodDisruptionBudget should guarantee enough surviving replicas stay up to absorb the traffic that was hitting the node being drained, since GPU pod replacement (image pull, weight load) is far slower than a typical stateless service's restart.

Interview Gold Point: Mention that some teams implement a custom pre-stop hook that flips the pod into a "draining" state visible to the load balancer immediately, rather than waiting for the readiness probe to catch up — shaving seconds off the drain that matter when a node needs to come down urgently for maintenance.
SENIORQ21. What makes GPU node pools harder for Cluster Autoscaler to scale from zero than a general-purpose CPU node pool?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Cluster Autoscaler decides whether to provision a new node from an empty node group by simulating whether a pending pod's requests would fit on a node of that group's instance type — but it has no built-in awareness of GPU capacity or type unless the node group is explicitly annotated with the GPU resources it would provide once scaled up (since there are, by definition, no running nodes yet to introspect). Get that annotation wrong or missing and Cluster Autoscaler will never scale the GPU pool up from zero, leaving GPU-requesting pods stuck Pending indefinitely with no visible error beyond "insufficient nvidia.com/gpu" — which looks identical to a capacity problem you can't actually fix by adding nodes when it's really a misconfiguration.

MIDQ22. How does the Horizontal Pod Autoscaler get access to GPU utilization as a scaling signal, given it doesn't understand GPUs natively?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

The HPA only ever reads from the Kubernetes metrics APIs (resource metrics for CPU/memory, or the custom/external metrics APIs for everything else) — it has no direct line to hardware telemetry. The standard pipeline is: a DCGM exporter runs alongside the GPU workload and exposes GPU utilization as Prometheus metrics, Prometheus scrapes and stores them, and the Prometheus Adapter translates a chosen PromQL query into the Kubernetes custom metrics API format that the HPA can query. Only once that chain is wired up can you write an HPA spec that scales on DCGM_FI_DEV_GPU_UTIL the same way a default HPA scales on CPU percentage.

SENIORQ23. What's the practical difference between the Kubernetes custom metrics API and the external metrics API, and which one does a typical GPU-utilization-based HPA setup use?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

The custom metrics API serves metrics tied to a specific Kubernetes object (a Pod, a Deployment) — this is what the Prometheus Adapter typically exposes for a GPU utilization metric scraped per-pod via DCGM. The external metrics API instead serves metrics from a system entirely outside Kubernetes' object model, like a cloud provider's SQS queue depth or a managed message broker's backlog size, which the HPA can still scale against even though there's no Kubernetes object the metric is "attached to." A GPU-utilization HPA almost always uses the custom metrics API, since the metric is naturally per-pod; the external metrics API becomes relevant when scaling an AI workload on an upstream queue depth signal instead of a resource-utilization signal.

SENIORQ24. Why can't the Vertical Pod Autoscaler simply resize a running pod's GPU request the way it can resize CPU and memory requests?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

VPA's CPU/memory recommendations work because those resources can, in principle, be adjusted in place or the pod can be recreated with a new request against fully fungible, finely-divisible capacity. GPUs are allocated as whole, specific physical devices via the device plugin's Allocate call — there's no notion of "give this pod 1.3 GPUs instead of 1," so a VPA recommendation to change a GPU request is only ever "1 device vs. 2 devices vs. 0," not a smooth resize. In practice, most teams don't run VPA against GPU requests at all; instead they treat GPU sizing as a workload-level architecture decision (how much a single request needs, whether it fits on one GPU or needs tensor-parallel sharding across several) made ahead of time rather than something an autoscaler tunes dynamically.

SENIORQ25. What does KEDA add on top of the HPA that makes it a common choice for scaling AI inference workloads, specifically batch or asynchronous ones?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

KEDA extends the HPA model with a large library of pre-built "scalers" for external event sources — queue depth in Kafka, RabbitMQ, SQS, Redis streams, and similar — and crucially supports scaling a Deployment to and from zero replicas, which vanilla HPA does not support at all. For an asynchronous inference workload (say, a queue of documents waiting to be embedded), that means the fleet of GPU pods can sit at zero replicas, consuming no GPU capacity or cost, until work actually arrives in the queue, at which point KEDA creates a HPA object under the hood that scales the Deployment up based on backlog size — giving genuinely elastic, cost-proportional GPU usage for bursty or intermittent AI workloads.

SENIORQ26. What's the trade-off of using KEDA's scale-to-zero for a GPU inference workload, compared to keeping a warm minimum replica count?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Scaling to zero eliminates idle GPU cost entirely, which matters a great deal given how expensive GPU capacity is compared to CPU — but the first request after a scale-to-zero period pays the full cold-start cost: node provisioning (if the GPU node pool itself scaled down too), image pull, and model weight load into GPU memory, which for a large model can be tens of seconds to minutes. That's an acceptable trade for genuinely bursty, latency-tolerant batch workloads, but it's usually the wrong choice for user-facing chat or completion endpoints, where most teams instead keep a small non-zero minimum replica count (accepting some idle cost) specifically to avoid making a live user wait through a cold start.

Interview Gold Point: Note that KEDA's minReplicaCount and cooldownPeriod settings let you tune exactly where on this spectrum you sit — a small nonzero floor with a longer cooldown gives you most of the cost savings of scale-to-zero while avoiding cold starts on the common case of intermittent-but-not-rare traffic.
STAFFQ27. When would you reach for Karpenter over the traditional Cluster Autoscaler for provisioning GPU capacity, and what's the underlying architectural difference?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

Cluster Autoscaler works against pre-defined, fixed node groups — it can only scale the count of nodes within a group whose instance type and configuration were decided ahead of time, which for GPU fleets often means maintaining separate node groups per GPU type and pre-guessing capacity mixes. Karpenter instead observes unschedulable pods directly and provisions right-sized nodes on demand from a much broader set of eligible instance types and capacity purchasing options (on-demand vs. spot) according to constraints you define, without needing pre-provisioned node groups per shape. For a platform running many different GPU-shaped workloads with genuinely varying requirements, Karpenter's just-in-time, pod-driven provisioning typically reduces both the number of node group definitions to maintain and the amount of stranded, wrong-shaped capacity sitting idle.

SENIORQ28. Your HPA is scaling a chat-serving Deployment up and down every few minutes in response to naturally bursty request patterns, and each scale-down kills pods that were about to receive traffic again. How do you fix the flapping?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

This is a classic case for the HPA's stabilization window, which prevents the autoscaler from acting on every single metric fluctuation by requiring a scaling decision to hold for a configured window before it's actually applied — a longer scaleDown.stabilizationWindowSeconds in particular smooths out the specific failure mode of scaling down right before load comes back. For a GPU workload specifically, biasing the policy toward being slow to scale down and comparatively quicker to scale up is usually the right asymmetry, since the cost of a premature scale-down (cold start delay, possibly dropped requests) is much higher than the cost of holding one extra GPU pod warm for a few more minutes than strictly necessary.

MIDQ29. Your traffic has a completely predictable daily pattern — high during business hours, near-zero overnight. Is there a Kubernetes-native way to scale ahead of that pattern rather than reactively?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Neither HPA nor KEDA natively supports time-of-day scheduling on its own, but KEDA's Cron scaler exists specifically for this — it lets you define a minimum and maximum replica count that changes on a cron schedule, so you can pre-warm GPU capacity a few minutes before the business-hours ramp begins rather than waiting for a reactive utilization-based scale-up to catch up after latency has already degraded. It's commonly layered alongside a regular utilization-based scaler so the schedule sets a sensible floor while reactive scaling still handles genuine unpredictability on top of the expected pattern.

SENIORQ30. Why is request queue depth often a better autoscaling signal than GPU utilization percentage for an LLM-serving workload?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

GPU utilization is a lagging, saturation-only signal — it tells you the GPU is busy, but a server can be at 100% utilization while queuing requests for seconds because the batching engine is already maxed out, and it also tells you nothing about *how much backlog* is building up behind that saturation. In-flight request count or queue depth, exposed as an application-level custom metric, is a much more direct proxy for user-experienced latency: it captures the exact thing you actually care about (is work piling up faster than it's draining) and, when combined with a target value tuned to the server's known throughput characteristics, lets the HPA react to the leading edge of a load spike rather than only after GPU saturation has already translated into degraded latency.

MIDQ31. What problem does the KServe InferenceService custom resource solve that you'd otherwise have to hand-roll with a plain Deployment and Service?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

A plain Deployment/Service pair gives you basic replica management and load balancing, but a production model-serving setup usually also needs request-response transformation, a standardized predict/explain protocol, traffic splitting between model versions for canary rollouts, scale-to-zero, and autoscaling tuned for inference specifically — each of which would otherwise be bespoke YAML and glue code per team. InferenceService packages all of that behind a single declarative spec: point it at a model artifact location and a predictor runtime (say, a specific version of a vLLM or Triton serving container), and KServe's controller reconciles the underlying Deployments, Services, and (via its Knative-based serverless mode) autoscaling and revisioning automatically.

SENIORQ32. How would you frame the difference between Seldon Core and KServe to someone deciding which to standardize on for a model-serving platform?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Both are Kubernetes-native CRD-and-operator approaches to model serving with substantial feature overlap (multi-framework runtimes, canary/traffic-split, autoscaling), so the meaningful differences at the margin come down to ecosystem fit: KServe grew out of, and integrates especially tightly with, the Knative serverless model and the broader Kubeflow ecosystem, making it a natural fit for teams already invested there or that want first-class scale-to-zero. Seldon Core's inference graph abstraction is more expressive for composing multi-step pipelines (pre-processing, an ensemble of models, post-processing, an outlier detector) as a single first-class object, which matters more for teams whose serving needs go beyond a single model behind an endpoint. In practice the decision is usually driven less by raw capability and more by which ecosystem the rest of the platform's tooling already assumes.

SENIORQ33. What does the KubeRay operator's RayCluster and RayService give you that you couldn't get from a plain Kubernetes Deployment, for a distributed training or serving workload built on Ray?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Ray's programming model assumes a head node coordinating a pool of worker nodes that can dynamically join and leave, with its own internal scheduler distributing tasks and actors across them — a shape that a stateless, identical-replica Deployment doesn't represent at all. RayCluster is a CRD that models exactly that head/worker topology as a native Kubernetes object, with the KubeRay operator responsible for provisioning the head pod, the worker pod group, and keeping the Ray cluster's actual membership in sync with the Kubernetes-level pod set (including autoscaling the worker group). RayService layers a managed deployment lifecycle on top specifically for serving, adding health checking and zero-downtime upgrades of the underlying Ray Serve application without you needing to hand-manage the Ray cluster's lifecycle yourself.

SENIORQ34. What Kubernetes-specific considerations come up when deploying vLLM as the serving container in a Deployment, beyond just pointing a container image at a model path?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

vLLM's continuous batching engine means a single pod's actual capacity for concurrent requests isn't a fixed number the way a traditional stateless web service's is — it depends on how much KV-cache memory is available, which interacts directly with the GPU memory request/limit you set and with vLLM's own gpu_memory_utilization setting, so under-provisioning either one silently caps throughput below what the hardware could otherwise deliver. Readiness needs to reflect actual model-load completion (vLLM's startup can take a meaningful amount of time to load weights and build its KV-cache pool), and if you're running tensor-parallel across multiple GPUs, the pod's GPU resource request must exactly match the parallelism degree and typically needs an accompanying shared-memory volume sized generously, since inter-process communication between the parallel workers commonly uses /dev/shm.

MIDQ35. Compare Text Generation Inference (TGI) to vLLM at the level of what changes in your Kubernetes deployment manifest.

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

The manifests look structurally similar — both are a Deployment wrapping a single serving-framework container reading model weights and exposing an HTTP endpoint — but the environment-variable and resource-tuning surface differs: TGI exposes its own sharding, quantization, and max-batch-related settings as container arguments/environment variables distinct from vLLM's equivalents, and its default health-check and startup-time characteristics differ enough that readiness/liveness probe timings tuned for one framework usually need re-tuning, not just copy-pasting, when switching to the other. The Kubernetes-level concerns (GPU resource requests, shared memory for multi-GPU sharding, startup-time-aware probes) are the same category of concern either way — only the framework-specific parameter names change.

SENIORQ36. How does KServe implement traffic-split canary rollouts between two versions of a model, and what does that actually change at the Kubernetes object level?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

An InferenceService spec can declare a canaryTrafficPercent alongside a new predictor revision; under the hood KServe's controller reconciles this into two separate underlying Knative Revisions (or, in raw-deployment mode, two Deployments) served behind a single logical route, with the percentage split enforced at the networking layer (Istio virtual services in the service-mesh mode, or Knative's own traffic splitting otherwise) rather than by the application itself making routing decisions. This means the canary rollout, rollback, and full promotion are all driven by editing the declarative traffic percentages in the CRD spec — the underlying pods, revisions, and routing rules are entirely managed by the controller, which is the point: the platform team gets safe, gradual model-version rollout without hand-writing routing logic per service.

STAFFQ37. What is the "model mesh" pattern, and what problem does it solve that a fleet of one-model-per-Deployment InferenceServices does not?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

A platform that needs to serve hundreds or thousands of individually low-traffic models runs into a packing problem if each model gets its own always-on Deployment and GPU allocation — most of that capacity sits idle most of the time, and the sheer number of pods becomes an operational burden on its own. The model mesh pattern instead runs a smaller, shared pool of serving pods that dynamically load and evict individual models into memory on demand (an LRU-style cache of "hot" models), multiplexing many logical models across far fewer physical GPU-backed replicas, with a routing layer tracking which pod currently has which model loaded. This trades a small amount of latency on a cold model (load-on-first-request) for dramatically better GPU utilization and a manageable pod count, which matters specifically when the number of distinct models is large relative to how much aggregate traffic they collectively receive.

SENIORQ38. When would you put model pre/post-processing logic in a sidecar container rather than inside the model-serving container itself?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Splitting pre/post-processing (tokenization, input validation, response formatting, PII redaction) into its own sidecar container inside the same pod lets it be developed, versioned, and scaled in resource terms independently of the model-serving container proper — useful when the processing logic changes far more often than the model itself, or is owned by a different team, or needs a different language runtime than the serving framework's container image supports. The trade-off is added latency from the extra inter-process hop within the pod (typically over localhost, so small but non-zero) and a slightly more complex pod spec to reason about jointly for resource sizing and probes; for simple, stable processing logic, baking it directly into the serving container or into the model server's own request/response transformer hooks (which KServe and Seldon both support) is usually simpler.

SENIORQ39. How does KServe's serverless mode achieve scale-to-zero for an InferenceService, and what latency cost does that impose?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

In serverless mode, KServe runs on top of Knative Serving, which tracks in-flight request concurrency per revision and scales the underlying Deployment down to zero pods once concurrency has been at zero for a configured idle window, routing subsequent requests through a lightweight "activator" component that buffers the request while a fresh pod is provisioned and becomes ready. For a model server this means the activator's buffering has to survive the full cold-start chain — image pull if not cached, container start, and model weight load into GPU memory — which for a large model can dominate total request latency by an order of magnitude compared to a warm pod, making scale-to-zero attractive mainly for genuinely infrequent or cost-sensitive workloads rather than for latency-SLA-bound user-facing endpoints.

MIDQ40. Is there ever a reason to run a model-serving workload as a StatefulSet instead of a Deployment?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Most stateless model-serving workloads belong on a Deployment, since replicas are interchangeable and Kubernetes doesn't need to distinguish one from another. A StatefulSet becomes the right choice when replica identity matters — most commonly when each replica needs its own dedicated, persistent per-replica volume (via volumeClaimTemplates) for a large local model cache that shouldn't be re-downloaded on every pod restart, or when a distributed serving setup (like a sharded model split across replicas, each responsible for a specific shard) needs stable, predictable network identities so peers can address each other reliably across restarts. If neither of those applies, a StatefulSet just adds operational complexity — ordered rollout and stable naming — for no actual benefit.

MIDQ41. What does a well-structured Helm chart's values.yaml look like for an AI service that needs to run identically across dev, staging, and production but with very different resource footprints?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

The chart templates should reference every environment-varying quantity — GPU count, CPU/memory requests and limits, replica count, autoscaling thresholds, model artifact path/version, and image tag — as values rather than hardcoding any of them, with a base values.yaml holding sane defaults and small per-environment override files (values-dev.yaml, values-prod.yaml) layered on top at install/upgrade time. The goal is that the templated manifests themselves never differ between environments — only the values fed into them do — so the same chart version that was tested in staging is provably the same set of Kubernetes objects deployed to production, just parameterized differently, rather than subtly different YAML per environment that's drifted apart over time.

SENIORQ42. How would you structure Helm values so that GPU resource requests are safe to override per environment without risking a typo that silently requests zero GPUs?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

A common failure mode is a values override that sets resources.limits.gpu instead of the correct resources.limits."nvidia.com/gpu" key, which Helm happily accepts as valid YAML and silently produces a pod with no GPU resource request at all rather than an error — the pod then schedules onto any node, including non-GPU ones, and fails inscrutably at runtime. Guarding against this means validating chart values against a JSON schema (Helm supports a values.schema.json alongside the chart) that requires the GPU key to be present and of the correct type, so a malformed override fails the helm install/upgrade immediately with a clear schema violation instead of producing a subtly broken deployment that only reveals itself once a pod is already running in the wrong place.

SENIORQ43. What would you use a Helm pre-install/pre-upgrade hook for in an AI service chart, and what's the risk of relying on one for something like model warm-up?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

A pre-install or pre-upgrade hook runs as a one-off Job before the main release's resources are applied, which is a reasonable place for tasks like validating that a referenced model artifact actually exists in object storage, or running a schema migration for a feature store the service depends on, before committing to rolling out pods that would otherwise fail at runtime. Using a hook specifically to "warm up" the model by loading it once and discarding the result is usually the wrong tool, though — the hook's Job runs in its own separate pod, so nothing about its warm-up transfers to the actual serving pods that get created afterward; genuine warm-up needs to happen inside each serving pod's own startup path (or via a shared, persistent model cache volume they all read from), not in a pre-install hook that runs somewhere else entirely.

STAFFQ44. You're packaging a multi-component AI stack — a serving layer, a vector database, a feature store, and a monitoring stack — as Helm charts for internal platform reuse. How would you structure the chart hierarchy?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

Each independently-versioned, independently-lifecycled component (the vector database, the monitoring stack) is a good candidate for its own subchart with its own values.yaml defaults, composed into a parent "umbrella" chart that declares them as dependencies and exposes a curated top-level values interface — so a consuming team installs one chart and gets a coherent stack, without needing to understand or directly touch the internals of each subchart unless they need to override something specific. The judgment call is where to draw subchart boundaries: components that genuinely evolve and get upgraded independently (a vector DB you might swap out entirely) belong as separate subcharts, while tightly-coupled pieces that only ever make sense deployed and versioned together (the serving Deployment and its dedicated ServiceMonitor) are better kept as templates directly in the umbrella chart, since splitting them into subcharts mainly adds indirection without buying independent versioning that will actually be exercised.

SENIORQ45. What specifically breaks with a default Ingress configuration when the backend is streaming a token-by-token LLM response over Server-Sent Events?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Most Ingress controllers, sitting on a reverse proxy like NGINX, apply buffering by default — the proxy accumulates the backend's response before forwarding it to the client, which is fine for a normal request/response payload but defeats the entire purpose of a streaming response, since the client ends up receiving the whole answer at once right before the connection would otherwise have timed out, rather than incrementally as tokens are generated. The fix is disabling response buffering for that specific route (an NGINX Ingress annotation like nginx.ingress.kubernetes.io/proxy-buffering: "off") and separately raising the proxy's read timeout well beyond its default, since a long-running generation can otherwise be killed by the proxy well before the model has actually finished producing output.

STAFFQ46. Why is load-balancing gRPC traffic on Kubernetes trickier than load-balancing plain HTTP/1.1 traffic, and how does that show up for AI services that use gRPC?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

gRPC runs over HTTP/2, which multiplexes many logical requests over a single long-lived TCP connection — a plain Kubernetes Service's default load balancing happens at the connection level (via kube-proxy's iptables/IPVS rules), so once a client's HTTP/2 connection is established to a given pod, every subsequent RPC on that connection keeps going to the same pod regardless of how many replicas exist behind the Service, producing badly skewed load distribution rather than the even spread you'd get with short-lived HTTP/1.1 connections. This is exactly the scenario where AI services exposing gRPC endpoints (many model-serving frameworks default to gRPC alongside REST) need either client-side load balancing (each client independently resolves and round-robins across pod IPs, which requires a headless Service) or an L7-aware proxy in front (a service mesh sidecar, or an Ingress/gateway that terminates and re-balances HTTP/2 request-by-request) rather than relying on the plain Service's connection-level balancing.

STAFFQ47. What's the actual cost/benefit trade-off of putting a service mesh like Istio in front of your AI inference services, versus leaving that traffic unmeshed?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

A mesh solves exactly the gRPC/HTTP-2 load-balancing problem above out of the box, plus gives you mTLS between services, fine-grained traffic-shifting for canaries independent of the CRD-level mechanisms individual serving frameworks provide, and uniform request-level observability (latency histograms, error rates) across every service without each one instrumenting itself. The cost is a sidecar proxy injected into every pod, adding both a fixed per-hop latency overhead and non-trivial memory/CPU overhead per pod — which matters more, proportionally, for a latency-sensitive, high-throughput inference path than it does for a typical internal microservice, and multiplies out to a real cost at the fleet level when you're running hundreds of GPU pods each carrying a sidecar. Whether it's worth it usually comes down to whether you actually need the mesh's traffic-management and cross-cutting security features, or whether you're adopting it mainly for observability that a lighter-weight, application-level tracing setup could give you at a fraction of the overhead.

SENIORQ48. Does enabling mTLS on the mesh meaningfully hurt latency for a token-by-token streaming inference endpoint?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

mTLS's real cost is almost entirely paid once, at connection establishment (the TLS handshake), not per request or per streamed token — so for a long-lived streaming connection carrying many tokens over its lifetime, that one-time handshake cost is amortized to near-nothing relative to the total data transferred. The place it actually matters is a workload pattern that opens many short-lived connections rather than reusing long-lived ones, where the repeated handshake overhead is paid over and over; for that reason, connection reuse/keep-alive settings matter more to streaming-inference latency under a mesh than the mTLS overhead itself does.

MIDQ49. A multi-turn chat application caches conversation state (KV-cache or session context) in the memory of whichever pod handled the first turn. What Kubernetes networking feature do you need to make sure later turns land on the same pod?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

A plain Kubernetes Service load-balances every new connection across the replica set with no memory of where a given client landed before, which breaks a serving pattern that relies on per-conversation state being pinned to one pod. The fix is session affinity — either the Service's own sessionAffinity: ClientIP (coarse, and unreliable behind a shared NAT/proxy where many clients appear to share one IP), or, more robustly for HTTP traffic, a cookie-based or header-based affinity rule configured at the Ingress/gateway layer that consistently routes a given session identifier to the same backend pod. Either way, session affinity needs to be an explicit, deliberate choice — the default Service behavior actively works against a stateful-per-session serving pattern.

SENIORQ50. What role do NetworkPolicies play in isolating GPU workloads on a shared multi-tenant cluster, and what's commonly missed when writing them?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

By default, any pod on a Kubernetes cluster can reach any other pod's network address — NetworkPolicies are the mechanism for restricting that, letting you scope, say, a tenant's inference pods to only be reachable from that tenant's own gateway/ingress path and only able to initiate connections to their own model registry or feature store, not another tenant's. The commonly missed part is that NetworkPolicies are additive-allow and default-deny only once *any* policy selects a pod — a namespace with zero NetworkPolicies applied is fully open, which teams sometimes discover only when an audit reveals that "isolated" tenant namespaces were never actually covered by a policy in the first place, because nobody had explicitly denied the default-allow state.

MIDQ51. Why would a model-weight PVC typically use the ReadOnlyMany access mode instead of ReadWriteOnce?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

ReadWriteOnce restricts a volume to being mounted read-write by pods on a single node at a time (or, depending on the CSI driver, to a single pod), which is fine for a volume one pod writes to, but is exactly wrong for a set of model-serving replicas spread across many nodes that all just need to read the same weights. ReadOnlyMany allows the same underlying volume to be mounted simultaneously, read-only, by pods across many nodes — letting a whole fleet of replicas share one copy of the model weights without each one needing its own local copy or a write-lock contention problem, since none of them ever need to write to it.

SENIORQ52. Compare pulling model weights via an init container from object storage at pod startup versus baking the weights directly into the container image. What would push you toward one or the other?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Baking weights into the image gives you the strongest reproducibility guarantee (the image digest fully pins the model version) and the fastest pod startup once the image is already cached on a node, at the cost of a very large image to build, push, store, and pull, and a full image rebuild-and-redeploy cycle for every model update even when nothing about the serving code changed. An init container that pulls weights from object storage at startup keeps the image small and decouples model updates from code deploys — you can roll out a new model version by changing a referenced path/tag without touching the image at all — at the cost of a startup-time network pull adding to cold-start latency and an extra external dependency (the object store) that startup now relies on. Most large-model serving setups lean toward the init-container pull specifically because image sizes in the tens-of-gigabytes range become genuinely painful to build and distribute through a normal container registry pipeline.

SENIORQ53. If every pod restart triggers a fresh multi-gigabyte model download from object storage, what's a Kubernetes-native way to avoid re-paying that cost on every restart?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A node-local cache — a hostPath-backed or local-NVMe-backed volume that persists across pod restarts on the same node, populated once and then reused by subsequent pods scheduled there — turns the download into a one-time cost per node rather than a per-pod-restart cost, since the init container can check whether the weights are already present locally before pulling. The trade-off is that the cache is now tied to a specific node, so a pod rescheduled to a different node still pays the full download cost, and the cache needs its own eviction/cleanup policy so old, unused model versions don't quietly fill up node-local disk over time; some platforms address this with a dedicated caching layer (a shared, node-adjacent cache service) rather than relying purely on per-node local disk.

SENIORQ54. What's the trade-off between using a cloud-provider CSI-backed network volume versus a node's local NVMe ephemeral storage for model weight storage?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

A CSI-backed network volume (EBS, Persistent Disk, and similar) is durable independent of any specific node's lifecycle, can be provisioned with the exact size and access mode you need, and survives a node being terminated entirely — but its read throughput is bounded by network attachment bandwidth, which can bottleneck loading a very large model into GPU memory as fast as the GPU itself could otherwise consume it. Local NVMe storage physically attached to the node offers dramatically higher read throughput, which matters when model-load time is a meaningful fraction of cold-start latency, but is inherently ephemeral — the moment the node terminates or the pod is rescheduled elsewhere, that data is gone — so it only makes sense as a cache layer fed from a durable source, never as the sole copy of anything you can't afford to lose or re-fetch.

MIDQ55. Describe a clean pattern for integrating a model registry (where model artifacts are versioned and stored) with Kubernetes deployment, so that "which model version is running" is always unambiguous.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A common pattern is to have the Deployment's pod spec reference an immutable, versioned artifact path (an object storage URI containing a specific model version identifier or content hash, not a mutable "latest" pointer) as an environment variable or init container argument, so the exact model version running is fully determined by the Deployment spec itself and visible in kubectl get deployment -o yaml without needing to inspect the running container. Deploying a new model version then becomes an ordinary Deployment update (a rolling update to a new pod template referencing the new artifact path), which gets you the existing rollout, rollback, and audit-trail machinery Kubernetes already provides for free, rather than needing a bespoke model-versioning system layered separately on top.

SENIORQ56. A GPU-serving pod's process gets OOM-killed, but `kubectl describe pod` shows the container's memory (RAM) usage was nowhere near its limit. What's actually happening?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Kubernetes' resource accounting and OOM-killing mechanism governs host RAM, cgroup-enforced — it has no visibility into or control over GPU memory (VRAM) at all, which is managed entirely by the NVIDIA driver and CUDA runtime independently of the kubelet. A CUDA out-of-memory error is a driver-level failure, distinct from a Linux OOM-kill, and it typically surfaces as the application process crashing with a CUDA allocation error rather than the pod being terminated by the kubelet's OOM killer — so "the pod died and RAM looked fine" is usually the tell that the actual exhausted resource was GPU memory, not host memory, and the fix is in application-level batch sizing or KV-cache configuration, not in the pod's resources.limits.memory.

SENIORQ57. Why must GPU resource requests and limits always be set equal in a pod spec, when for CPU and memory it's common and often beneficial to set a limit higher than the request?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

For CPU and memory, a request-below-limit gap lets the scheduler pack pods more densely on the (correct) bet that not every pod will simultaneously use its full limit, with the kernel able to throttle or reclaim the difference gracefully if contention does occur. GPUs have no equivalent kernel-level mechanism for graceful sharing or reclaiming a fraction of a device mid-use — a device is either allocated whole to a pod or it isn't, so a request lower than the limit would be asking the scheduler to guarantee less than what the pod might actually consume, with no way to enforce or reclaim the gap if it does. Kubernetes' extended resource model reflects this by requiring GPU requests and limits to match exactly; setting them differently is rejected or, depending on version, simply has no meaningful effect since there's nothing partial to allocate.

MIDQ58. Beyond the GPU count itself, what CPU and memory sizing mistakes commonly bottleneck a GPU-serving pod that on paper "has enough GPUs"?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Under-provisioned CPU starves the data-loading, tokenization, request-batching, and pre/post-processing work that happens on the CPU side of the pipeline before and after the actual GPU compute — a GPU that's frequently idle waiting on a CPU-bound preprocessing step is wasted expensive capacity regardless of how correctly its own request/limit was set. Under-provisioned pod memory can similarly bottleneck host-side buffering (staging input batches, holding intermediate results before they're moved to GPU memory), and is a separate failure mode entirely from the GPU-memory OOM discussed above — both need sizing that reflects the actual CPU/memory footprint of the surrounding pipeline, not just "enough for the container to start."

SENIORQ59. How would you use a ResourceQuota to stop one team from monopolizing a shared cluster's GPU budget, and what's the operational downside of doing so?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A ResourceQuota scoped to a team's namespace can cap the total requests.nvidia.com/gpu (and CPU/memory) that all pods in that namespace may collectively request, which the API server enforces at admission time — any pod creation that would push the namespace over its GPU budget is rejected outright, giving a hard, cluster-enforced ceiling rather than a soft convention teams are trusted to respect. The downside is that a hard per-namespace quota is a static allocation that doesn't flex with actual demand: a team with an urgent, legitimate burst need for more GPU capacity than their quota allows is simply blocked until a human raises the quota, so quotas need to be paired with a reasonably fast process for requesting temporary increases, or with a PriorityClass-based preemption scheme instead of, or in addition to, hard quotas, to handle genuine cross-team burst contention more gracefully.

MIDQ60. What does a LimitRange accomplish for GPU pods that a ResourceQuota alone does not?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

A ResourceQuota caps the aggregate resource consumption across an entire namespace but says nothing about any individual pod's request — a LimitRange fills that gap by defining default requests/limits applied automatically to any pod spec in the namespace that doesn't specify its own, and can also enforce a minimum/maximum per-pod bound. For GPU workloads, this matters because a pod submitted without an explicit GPU request wouldn't request one at all (there's no meaningful "default" GPU quantity the way there is a default CPU/memory), so a LimitRange is sometimes used to enforce a sane maximum-per-pod GPU count in a shared namespace, preventing one misconfigured deployment from requesting far more GPU than any single workload in that namespace should reasonably need.

MIDQ61. How does a Prometheus Operator ServiceMonitor get GPU metrics from a DCGM exporter DaemonSet into Prometheus, concretely?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

The DCGM exporter runs as a DaemonSet (one per GPU node, matching the same per-node-coverage logic as the device plugin) and exposes GPU telemetry on an HTTP metrics endpoint in Prometheus's text exposition format. A ServiceMonitor is a Prometheus-Operator-specific CRD that declares which Services to scrape and on what port/interval, using a label selector rather than a hardcoded target list — the Operator's reconciler watches for ServiceMonitor objects matching its configuration and automatically regenerates Prometheus's actual scrape configuration to include them, so adding GPU metric collection to a new namespace or cluster is a matter of applying a ServiceMonitor object rather than hand-editing Prometheus's static config and restarting it.

SENIORQ62. Beyond raw utilization percentage, what other GPU metrics from a DCGM exporter are worth alerting on for a production inference cluster?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

GPU memory utilization (distinct from compute utilization) catches the case of a workload approaching an OOM before it actually crashes; SM (streaming multiprocessor) occupancy gives a more precise read on genuine compute saturation than the coarser overall utilization figure; ECC error counts (correctable and uncorrectable) surface hardware degradation before it causes silent data corruption or a hard crash; and power draw/thermal throttling metrics catch a GPU that's silently running below its rated performance because it's hitting a thermal or power ceiling, which looks like a mysterious throughput regression if you're only watching utilization percentage and don't know to check for throttling specifically.

STAFFQ63. What does distributed tracing add for a multi-pod inference pipeline (say, a gateway, a retrieval service, and a model-serving pod) that GPU/pod-level metrics alone don't give you?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

Per-pod metrics tell you each component's own latency and error rate in isolation, but not how a single end-to-end request's time budget is actually split across the hops it traversed, or where in a multi-service call chain a specific slow or failed request actually stalled — a request that's slow "somewhere in the pipeline" is much harder to root-cause from four separate dashboards than from one trace showing exactly which span consumed the time. Distributed tracing (propagating a trace context header through the gateway, retrieval call, and inference call, and reporting spans to a collector) reconstructs that end-to-end path per request, which becomes essential once the pipeline has enough hops that "which service is actually the bottleneck for this specific slow request" stops being answerable by eyeballing aggregate dashboards.

MIDQ64. Why is a sidecar-per-pod logging pattern often overkill for a high-throughput GPU inference fleet, compared to node-level log collection?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

A logging sidecar running in every pod multiplies its own CPU/memory overhead by the number of replicas, which is a real cost on a fleet of expensive GPU pods where you'd rather that headroom go to the workload itself. Since container stdout/stderr is already captured by the container runtime and available on-node, a node-level log collector (a DaemonSet running one collector per node that tails every container's logs on that node and ships them centrally) achieves the same end result — centralized, queryable logs — with a single shared collection process per node rather than one per pod, making it the more resource-efficient default; a sidecar only earns its keep when a pod's logging needs genuinely differ from the fleet norm (a nonstandard log format needing in-pod transformation before shipping, for instance).

SENIORQ65. What namespace isolation strategy would you use for multiple teams sharing one GPU-equipped cluster, and what does namespace boundary alone fail to enforce?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A per-team namespace gives you a natural boundary for RBAC (who can create/modify what), ResourceQuotas (how much GPU/CPU/memory the team may collectively request), and NetworkPolicy scoping (which pods can talk to which) — a solid default starting point for logical isolation. What namespace boundaries alone don't enforce is anything at the node or kernel level: without additional taints/tolerations or node affinity rules routing each team's workloads to dedicated node pools, two teams' pods can still be co-scheduled onto the very same physical GPU node, and namespace boundaries do nothing to prevent noisy-neighbor resource contention or, in a hard multi-tenancy requirement (mutually distrusting tenants), the deeper isolation gaps in the container sandbox itself — namespaces are an administrative and scheduling boundary, not a hardware or kernel isolation boundary.

STAFFQ66. How do PriorityClass and ResourceQuota work together to give a shared GPU cluster something like fair-share scheduling across teams?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

ResourceQuota alone only gives you a hard static ceiling per namespace, with no mechanism for one team to temporarily use another's unused capacity — which tends to leave clusters either overprovisioned to cover everyone's worst case, or under-provisioned and constantly hitting quota walls even when the cluster as a whole has spare GPU sitting idle in another team's quota. Layering PriorityClass on top changes the picture: instead of (or in addition to) hard quotas, teams share a common pool, but latency-critical, high-priority workloads can preempt lower-priority ones (typically labeled as explicitly preemptible batch/best-effort work) when the cluster is under real contention — giving the cluster the elasticity to let any team temporarily use spare capacity, while guaranteeing that a genuine priority conflict resolves in favor of the workload that actually needs to keep running. Getting this right requires real discipline about which workloads are honestly marked low-priority-preemptible, since a team that marks everything high-priority defeats the entire scheme.

SENIORQ67. What RBAC design pitfalls come up specifically for an AI platform team, where data scientists need to deploy models but shouldn't necessarily be able to modify cluster-wide GPU node configuration?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

The common mistake is granting a broad namespace-admin ClusterRole out of convenience, which technically lets data scientists deploy their own InferenceServices and Jobs but also lets them modify ResourceQuotas, NetworkPolicies, or RBAC bindings within their own namespace — enough rope to accidentally (or deliberately) grant themselves more GPU budget than intended, or weaken their own namespace's isolation. A tighter design scopes a custom Role to exactly the verbs and resource types data scientists actually need (create/get/list/delete on Deployments, Jobs, and the relevant CRDs like InferenceService, plus read-only on ConfigMaps/Secrets they depend on) while leaving quota, network policy, and RBAC management itself to a separate, platform-team-only Role — the principle being that "can deploy workloads" and "can change the guardrails those workloads operate within" should never be granted by the same binding.

STAFFQ68. How would you implement GPU cost attribution/chargeback across teams sharing one Kubernetes cluster, given that the cloud bill itself doesn't know which pod used which GPU-hour?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

The raw cloud bill is scoped to instances, not to Kubernetes objects, so attributing cost down to a team or workload requires joining two data sources: node-level cost (instance type, hourly rate, spot vs. on-demand) and pod-level resource consumption over time, keyed by namespace/label, pulled from the cluster's own metrics (actual GPU utilization, not just requested allocation, ideally, since a team requesting but under-utilizing a GPU is a distinct cost problem from one running it flat out). Purpose-built tools (Kubecost and similar) automate exactly this join, allocating a node's cost across the pods that ran on it proportionally to their resource requests or actual usage, and rolling that up by namespace/label into a per-team GPU-hour and dollar figure — the underlying principle being that cost attribution has to be built on the same label/namespace taxonomy the rest of your multi-tenancy model already uses, or it can't be automated at all.

MIDQ69. When would you use a Kubernetes Job versus a CronJob for a fine-tuning workflow?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

A Job is the right primitive for a fine-tuning run triggered on demand — someone kicks off training against a specific dataset/config, the Job runs pods to completion (with retry semantics on failure up to a configured backoff limit), and it's done; that's a one-shot execution model, not a recurring one. A CronJob wraps that same Job semantics with a schedule, appropriate for fine-tuning that genuinely needs to happen on a fixed cadence — say, a nightly retrain against the day's newly collected data — where the CronJob controller is responsible for creating a fresh Job object on schedule rather than a human or external pipeline triggering it each time.

SENIORQ70. A long-running fine-tuning Job keeps hitting its `backoffLimit` and giving up, even though each individual failure was a transient, recoverable GPU driver hiccup. What's misconfigured?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A Job's default backoffLimit (six, by default) and its exponential backoff between retries are tuned for workloads that fail fast and cheaply — for a training job where each restart means re-provisioning a GPU node, re-pulling a large image, and potentially re-loading a large dataset, a small number of retries can be exhausted by transient infrastructure hiccups alone, long before the job has had a fair chance to actually make training progress. The fix usually involves both raising backoffLimit to reflect the realistic number of transient failures expected over a multi-hour or multi-day run, and — more importantly — combining it with checkpointing so that a restarted pod resumes from its last saved state rather than re-starting the entire training run from scratch on every retry, which is what actually makes a higher retry count useful rather than just wasteful.

SENIORQ71. What does an Indexed Job give you for a distributed, data-parallel batch inference workload that a plain parallel Job does not?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A plain parallel Job creates N interchangeable pods with no inherent identity, which is fine when the work is homogeneous and any pod can pick up any unit of work from a shared queue, but awkward when you specifically want to statically partition a fixed dataset into N shards, one per pod, ahead of time. An Indexed Job assigns each pod a stable completion index (exposed as an environment variable, JOB_COMPLETION_INDEX) that the pod can use to deterministically compute which shard of the dataset it's responsible for — letting you partition a batch-inference dataset into exactly N slices up front and have pod index 3 always process shard 3, without needing an external work-queue coordination layer just to hand out which piece of data goes to which pod.

MIDQ72. What's the purpose of a Job's `ttlSecondsAfterFinished`, and why does forgetting to set it matter more for AI batch pipelines than for typical short CI jobs?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

By default, completed Job objects and their pods stick around indefinitely until manually cleaned up, which for a pipeline that kicks off frequent fine-tuning or batch-inference Jobs (nightly, or per-dataset-update) accumulates a steadily growing pile of completed-but-not-deleted Job and Pod objects that bloats etcd and clutters kubectl get output. ttlSecondsAfterFinished tells the Job controller to automatically garbage-collect the Job (and its pods) some time after it completes, which matters more for AI pipelines specifically because their Jobs tend to be both frequent and often carry sizeable per-pod metadata (large environment variable sets, sizeable log volumes referenced by the pod spec) compared to a typical lightweight CI job.

STAFFQ73. A long-running fine-tuning Job runs on spot GPU capacity, and the node gets reclaimed mid-training. What has to be true about the Job's design for that reclaim to not lose all the training progress made so far?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Spot reclaim delivers a termination notice with a short warning window, not a graceful "finish what you're doing" signal — so the training process itself needs to periodically checkpoint its state (model weights, optimizer state, and current step/epoch) to durable storage on its own schedule, independent of whether a reclaim is imminent, since there usually isn't enough warning to checkpoint reactively once the notice arrives. On restart (whether via the Job's own retry or a re-submitted run), the training entrypoint needs to check for and resume from the latest available checkpoint rather than always starting from step zero, which is an application-level responsibility the Job/Kubernetes layer doesn't provide for you — Kubernetes will happily reschedule the pod, but resuming from where it left off is purely a function of whether the training code was written to expect exactly this kind of interruption.

STAFFQ74. What problem does Kueue's job-queueing and suspend/resume model solve for a cluster running many fine-tuning and batch-inference Jobs competing for limited GPU capacity?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

Without a queueing layer, every submitted Job's pods are created immediately and left to the default scheduler to place — if GPU capacity is currently full, those pods simply sit Pending, competing chaotically for whatever capacity frees up next with no notion of priority, fairness, or admission ordering across teams. Kueue introduces an admission-control layer above the Job: a submitted workload is queued and only "admitted" (its pods actually created, or an existing suspended Job unsuspended) once its resource requirements can be satisfied under the cluster's configured quota and fairness policy, and it can suspend a running Job's pods and requeue them if a higher-priority workload needs the capacity. This turns "which Job gets to run right now" from an emergent property of the base scheduler's opportunistic placement into an explicit, governable queueing policy — much closer to how a traditional HPC batch scheduler manages a shared GPU cluster.

STAFFQ75. What is gang scheduling, and why does a multi-node distributed training job specifically need it when the default Kubernetes scheduler doesn't provide it?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

The default scheduler places pods one at a time, independently — for a stateless service, that's fine, since each replica works whether or not its siblings have started yet. A distributed training job using data or tensor parallelism across, say, 8 nodes is fundamentally different: none of the 8 worker processes can make progress until *all 8* are running and can establish their collective-communication group, so if the scheduler places 5 of the 8 pods and then the cluster runs out of capacity for the remaining 3, those 5 sit consuming GPU capacity indefinitely while doing zero useful work, waiting on peers that may never be scheduled — and worse, this can deadlock against another job's partial allocation competing for the same remaining capacity. Gang scheduling (implemented via schedulers like Volcano or Kueue's equivalent mechanism) solves this by making the placement decision all-or-nothing at the level of the whole job: either enough capacity exists to place every required pod simultaneously, or none of them are scheduled at all, avoiding the wasted-partial-allocation deadlock entirely.

STAFFQ76. You're designing a self-serve internal platform so that data science teams can deploy their own models to production without needing a platform engineer in the loop for every deploy. What Kubernetes-level abstraction would you build, and what do you deliberately hide from them?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

The right shape is usually a thin, opinionated CRD (or a Helm chart with a tightly-scoped values schema) that exposes only the handful of things a data scientist actually needs to decide — model artifact location, a small enum of instance/GPU sizes, a scaling range, maybe a small set of allowed base runtime images — while the controller reconciling that CRD generates the full underlying Deployment, Service, HPA, ServiceMonitor, and NetworkPolicy objects with all the platform team's hardening baked in (probe timings tuned correctly, resource limits within sane bounds, mandatory labels for cost attribution, egress restricted by default). What's deliberately hidden is everything that's either dangerous to get wrong (raw pod-spec-level security context, node affinity/taints) or purely an implementation detail the requester shouldn't need to reason about (the exact Service/Ingress wiring) — the design goal is that the self-serve surface is small enough to be safe to expose broadly, while all the accumulated platform-team judgment about "how to run this correctly" lives in the controller, not in each team's own copy-pasted YAML.

SENIORQ77. When rolling out a new model version, what's the practical difference on Kubernetes between doing a blue/green swap and a gradual canary, and which failure modes does each protect against?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

A blue/green swap runs the new model version fully scaled up alongside the old one and then flips all traffic over atomically (a Service selector change, or a 100% traffic-weight flip in a traffic-splitting CRD) — it gives you an instant, complete rollback (flip the selector back) if something's wrong, at the cost of needing double the capacity briefly and exposing 100% of traffic to a problem the moment the flip happens, with no chance to catch it on a small fraction of traffic first. A gradual canary instead shifts a small percentage of traffic to the new version and watches its error rate/latency before incrementally increasing that percentage, which catches a broken model version while it's only affecting a small fraction of users — better for catching subtle regressions (a marginally worse hallucination rate, a slight latency increase) that a blue/green swap's instant full cutover wouldn't give you time to notice before every user is affected.

SENIORQ78. What's the specific pain point of pulling a very large (tens-of-GB) GPU-serving container image, and what Kubernetes/registry-level techniques mitigate it?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A huge image (CUDA libraries, a deep learning framework, plus baked-in weights if you've gone that route) means a cold node pulling it for the first time can spend minutes just downloading and unpacking layers before the container even starts — directly inflating both first-deploy latency and, worse, autoscaling responsiveness, since a freshly-provisioned node scaling up to meet load has to eat that same pull cost before it can serve any traffic. Mitigations include structuring the image so rarely-changing, large layers (the CUDA/framework base) are cached separately from frequently-changing application code layers so most deploys only pull a small delta; pre-pulling images onto nodes ahead of when they're needed (some clusters run a warm-pool of nodes with the image already cached, or use a DaemonSet purely to pre-pull); and, where the registry/runtime supports it, lazy/on-demand image pulling that starts the container before the entire image has finished downloading, streaming in the remaining layers as they're accessed.

SENIORQ79. What does a PodDisruptionBudget protect against for a GPU inference Deployment, and what's a mistake teams make when setting it?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A PodDisruptionBudget (PDB) bounds how many replicas of a Deployment can be voluntarily disrupted at once — node drains for maintenance, cluster upgrades, cluster-autoscaler consolidation — by refusing an eviction that would take the running/available replica count below the PDB's configured minimum, which matters enormously for GPU pods specifically since replacing one is slow (image pull, weight load) compared to a typical stateless service, so an uncontrolled mass eviction during a node upgrade can leave a serving fleet badly under-capacity for minutes. The common mistake is setting minAvailable (or maxUnavailable) as an absolute count that made sense at the replica count the Deployment happened to run at when the PDB was written, and then never revisiting it — a PDB with minAvailable: 3 on a Deployment that later got scaled down to 3 replicas total makes the Deployment fully un-drainable, blocking node maintenance indefinitely until someone notices and fixes the mismatch.

MIDQ80. Why would you add pod anti-affinity rules to a GPU-serving Deployment's spec even when the scheduler would otherwise spread replicas reasonably on its own?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

The default scheduler's spreading behavior is a soft preference, not a guarantee — under resource pressure it will happily co-locate multiple replicas of the same Deployment on one node if that's where the available GPU capacity happens to be, which means a single node failure or drain can take out multiple replicas simultaneously rather than just one. An explicit pod anti-affinity rule (preferably a hard, requiredDuringScheduling rule keyed on the Deployment's own label, targeting node-level topology) forces replicas onto distinct nodes, trading some scheduling flexibility (a replica might stay Pending slightly longer waiting for a distinct node to free up) for a real availability guarantee that a correlated single-node failure can only ever take out one replica, not several.

Interview Gold Point: Point out that for a multi-AZ cluster, the same logic extends to a topology-key anti-affinity across availability zones, not just nodes — protecting against an entire zone outage, not merely a single-node failure, at the cost of needing enough GPU capacity available in every zone you want to spread across.
SENIORQ81. When does it make more sense to scale a single GPU-serving pod vertically (a bigger, more capable GPU) versus scaling horizontally (more replicas on smaller GPUs)?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Vertical scaling is forced, not optional, once a single model instance's memory footprint exceeds what any single GPU in your fleet offers — there's no horizontal way around needing a bigger device (or tensor-parallel sharding across several) when the model itself doesn't fit. Once the model does comfortably fit on a given GPU, horizontal scaling (more replicas) is usually the better lever for handling increased request volume, since it composes cleanly with the HPA, gives you finer-grained capacity increments, and improves availability (losing one of many replicas is a small capacity dip, losing your one large vertically-scaled instance is a full outage) — vertical scaling within Kubernetes is comparatively clumsy since it typically means recreating the pod against a different node/GPU-type selector rather than a smooth in-place resize.

SENIORQ82. A pod requesting `nvidia.com/gpu: 1` has been stuck in `Pending` for twenty minutes. Walk through your diagnostic steps.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

kubectl describe pod is the first stop — its Events section directly states the scheduler's rejection reason, most commonly "Insufficient nvidia.com/gpu" across all nodes. From there: check whether any node currently reports nonzero allocatable GPU at all (kubectl describe node per GPU node, or a quick query against the node's status.allocatable) — zero allocatable GPU on nodes that physically have them points at the device plugin being unhealthy or not yet running there. If nodes do show available GPU capacity, check for a taint the pod's spec lacks a matching toleration for, or a nodeAffinity/nodeSelector rule that unintentionally excludes every node that actually has free GPU. If genuinely no node anywhere has spare capacity, check whether a Cluster Autoscaler or Karpenter node group exists that could scale up to satisfy the pending pod, and whether its scale-from-zero GPU-resource annotation (if scaling from an empty node group) is correctly configured — a missing annotation there is a very common reason a GPU pool never scales up even though the autoscaler is otherwise healthy.

MIDQ83. A model-serving pod is stuck in `CrashLoopBackOff`. What's different about root-causing this for a GPU model server compared to a typical stateless web service crash-looping?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

A stateless web service crash-looping is usually a straightforward application bug or missing config, visible in the first few lines of logs. A GPU model server has a wider, more specific set of likely causes worth checking in order: a driver/CUDA-toolkit version mismatch between the node and the container image (often surfacing as an obscure CUDA initialization error rather than a clear message); a liveness probe configured with startup-time assumptions from a stateless-service default that kills the pod mid-weight-load before it's ever had the chance to become ready (distinguishable by checking whether the container's own logs show it was still loading when the kill happened, versus it having genuinely crashed on its own); or a GPU memory OOM during model load itself if the requested/limited GPU doesn't actually have enough free memory for the model's footprint. Checking kubectl describe pod for which probe (if any) triggered the restart, alongside the container's own logs immediately before termination, usually disambiguates between these quickly.

STAFFQ84. A stakeholder asks why you'd build model serving on Kubernetes at all instead of just using a managed serverless inference product. Focusing purely on the Kubernetes-object-level trade-offs (not general build-vs-buy strategy), what would you say?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

A managed serverless product hides essentially all of the object-level machinery this chapter covers — device plugins, taints/tolerations, HPA/KEDA wiring, PVC-based model caching, PodDisruptionBudgets — behind a much smaller API surface, at the cost of losing direct control over exactly how any of it behaves: you can't tune a readiness probe's grace period, can't add a custom NetworkPolicy, can't run a bespoke sidecar for preprocessing, and are bound by whatever GPU types and scaling behavior the provider happens to expose. Building on Kubernetes directly gives you all of that control, plus the ability to run mixed batch and serving workloads side by side on the same shared capacity with your own scheduling policy — real advantages if your workloads are heterogeneous or you're running at a scale where GPU utilization efficiency meaningfully moves the cost needle, and correspondingly not worth the added operational surface if your needs are simple and homogeneous enough that the managed product's defaults already fit.

STAFFQ85. How would a mutating admission webhook be used to enforce that every GPU-requesting pod on the cluster automatically gets a correctly-sized shared memory volume, without relying on every team remembering to add it themselves?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

A mutating admission webhook intercepts every pod creation request at the API server before it's persisted, and can programmatically rewrite the pod spec — so a webhook configured to trigger on any pod requesting nvidia.com/gpu can inject a /dev/shm volume sized proportionally to the GPU count requested (needed for multi-GPU tensor-parallel inter-process communication, discussed earlier), or enforce that GPU requests equal limits, or inject standard labels needed for cost attribution — all without needing every team's Deployment YAML to remember to include it correctly by hand. The trade-off is that admission webhooks are a powerful, somewhat opaque point of cluster-wide behavior — a bug in the webhook can block or silently mutate every pod creation cluster-wide, so they need to be built with strict timeouts, a well-defined failure policy (typically failing open for non-critical mutations rather than blocking all pod creation if the webhook itself is briefly unavailable), and careful test coverage before being deployed as a mandatory gate on the whole cluster.

SENIORQ86. How does using a standard Deployment rolling update for a new model version give you rollback "for free," and where does that story break down for AI-specific serving?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A Deployment retains a revision history of its previous ReplicaSets, so kubectl rollout undo can revert to the prior pod template (and therefore the prior model version, if that's what the template referenced) essentially instantly at the Kubernetes-object level — genuinely useful, free machinery. Where it breaks down for AI serving specifically is that "roll back the Deployment" only reverts which model artifact the pods are configured to load — it says nothing about any external state that may have silently drifted alongside the model (a feature store schema the new model version expected, an updated tokenizer/vocabulary file, a changed prompt template stored elsewhere), so a rollback that only reverts the Deployment while those dependencies have moved on can leave you running the old model against now-incompatible surrounding infrastructure — meaning a genuinely safe rollback plan for a model change needs to account for everything the model version depends on, not just the Deployment's own revision history.

Interview Gold Point: This is a good moment to mention model/feature/prompt version coupling as a first-class concern — interviewers at the staff level are often listening for whether you treat "the model" as one atomic deployable unit or naively assume the Deployment object alone captures everything that needs to roll back together.
STAFFQ87. A chat-serving fleet relies on session affinity to keep a conversation's KV-cache pinned to one pod. How does that interact badly with horizontal autoscaling, and how would you address it?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Session affinity actively works against even load distribution: as new conversations start, they get pinned to whichever pods happen to be selected by the affinity mechanism, but existing long conversations stay pinned to their original pod for the conversation's full lifetime — so a scale-up event adds new pods that only ever receive *new* sessions, while long-running sessions keep loading the older pods, which can leave the fleet unevenly loaded even after scaling out, and a scale-down event risks evicting a pod that's mid-conversation for an active session, forcibly dropping its cached context. Addressing this well usually means decoupling the cache from pod lifetime: externalizing conversation state to a shared, fast key-value store (so any pod can serve any conversation's next turn by fetching its context rather than needing it resident in that specific pod's memory) trades a network fetch for cross-request state for the ability to load-balance and scale freely, which is often the better trade once conversation volume is high enough that affinity-driven imbalance becomes a real capacity-planning headache.

SENIORQ88. Why do GPU workloads often need Pod Security Standards or admission configuration explicitly relaxed from a cluster's default "restricted" baseline, and what's the actual security trade-off being made?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A cluster-wide "restricted" Pod Security Standard typically disallows things like running as root, mounting host device files, or using certain capabilities — but the device plugin framework and the NVIDIA Container Toolkit's runtime hook, in some configurations, need to mount host GPU device files and driver libraries into the container, which a strictly restricted policy would block outright. The trade-off is real, not cosmetic: broadening what a GPU-workload namespace is allowed to do (typically via a separate, more permissive Pod Security Standard applied only to a dedicated GPU-workload namespace, not cluster-wide) does widen that namespace's attack surface compared to the general baseline, so the mitigating practice is scoping the relaxation as narrowly as possible — applied only to the specific namespaces actually running GPU workloads, paired with tighter RBAC and network policy on exactly those namespaces to compensate for the loosened pod-level restriction.

STAFFQ89. Why might a pod need to request `ephemeral-storage` explicitly for an inference workload that spills KV-cache or intermediate attention state to local disk, and what happens if you don't?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Some inference setups, when GPU memory is the binding constraint, offload part of the KV-cache to local node disk rather than keeping it entirely in GPU or host memory — that disk usage draws from the node's shared ephemeral storage pool the same way container logs and any other unrequested scratch writes do, and if the pod spec doesn't declare an ephemeral-storage request/limit reflecting that usage, the kubelet has no basis for admission-time capacity planning around it and, separately, no basis for evicting the *right* pod first if the node's ephemeral storage does fill up — an unrequested, unbounded consumer can starve or trigger eviction of unrelated, well-behaved pods on the same node rather than being throttled or evicted itself. Declaring the request accurately gives the scheduler a true picture of the node's remaining capacity and makes the kubelet's node-pressure eviction logic target the actual offender rather than whichever innocent pod happens to look worst by comparison.

MIDQ90. How would `volumeClaimTemplates` on a StatefulSet be used to give each replica of a model-serving fleet its own dedicated, persistent local model cache?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Unlike a Deployment's shared pod template, a StatefulSet's volumeClaimTemplates provisions a distinct PersistentVolumeClaim per ordinal replica (model-cache-0, model-cache-1, and so on), and — critically — that same PVC is reattached to the same-ordinal pod across restarts and rescheduling, rather than a fresh volume being created each time. For a fleet where each replica maintains its own local cache of recently-used model versions (relevant in a model-mesh-style multi-model setup), this means a restarted replica comes back up with its cache already warm rather than starting cold, which a plain Deployment's ephemeral, non-identity-tied pod template can't provide, since a Deployment has no concept of "this specific replacement pod is a continuation of that specific prior replica."

STAFFQ91. Pulling everything in this chapter together: you're asked to design the Kubernetes-object-level architecture for a platform serving several LLMs to both interactive chat users and overnight batch-embedding jobs, on a shared GPU fleet. What are the core decisions, and how do they fit together?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

The GPU node layer needs separate, taint-isolated node pools (or PriorityClass-based preemption on a shared pool) so batch work can never starve latency-critical chat capacity, provisioned via Karpenter or Cluster Autoscaler with correctly configured scale-from-zero GPU annotations. The chat-serving tier runs as Deployments (or InferenceServices, if standardizing on KServe) behind a Service with session affinity or, better, externalized conversation state, scaled by a KEDA or HPA setup driven by request queue depth rather than GPU utilization percentage alone, fronted by an Ingress with buffering disabled and generous timeouts for streaming responses, and protected by a PodDisruptionBudget and anti-affinity rules for availability. The batch-embedding tier runs as Indexed Jobs (or is queued through Kueue if contention with other batch work needs fair-share governance), on spot-capacity-tolerant node pools, with checkpointing built into the job itself so spot reclaim doesn't lose progress. Model weights for both tiers are pulled via init containers from a versioned model registry into a shared, node-cached or ReadOnlyMany-backed volume rather than baked into images, given how large modern model weights are. Observability threads through all of it via a DCGM exporter and Prometheus Operator ServiceMonitors feeding both the autoscalers and alerting on GPU-specific failure modes (thermal throttling, ECC errors, memory saturation), with namespace-scoped RBAC, ResourceQuotas, and NetworkPolicies enforcing the multi-tenancy boundary between the chat and batch teams' workloads. The unifying theme across every one of these decisions is the same: Kubernetes' general-purpose primitives (Deployment, Job, HPA, Service, PVC) are all still the right foundation, but every one of them needs an AI-workload-specific adaptation — a different autoscaling signal, a different storage pattern, a different node-pool isolation strategy — layered on top of the generic default.

STAFFQ92. If you had to name the single most common mistake teams make when moving AI/model-serving workloads onto Kubernetes for the first time, what would it be?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Treating a GPU-backed model-serving pod as if it were just a slightly-bigger stateless web service — copy-pasting probe timings, HPA metrics, and resource-request conventions that were tuned for CPU-bound, fast-starting, easily-shared workloads directly onto a workload whose startup is slow (weight loading), whose bottleneck resource behaves nothing like CPU (no fractional sharing, no kernel-level throttling, a separate OOM domain from host memory), and whose real capacity signal is rarely the metric copy-pasted from a generic dashboard template. Nearly every specific failure mode covered in this chapter — crash-looping on a too-aggressive liveness probe, an HPA that never fires because it's watching CPU on a GPU-bound workload, a node pool that silently never scales from zero — ultimately traces back to that same root cause: assuming Kubernetes' generic defaults, tuned for the median stateless workload, transfer unchanged to AI-specific ones. The fix is never a single setting; it's the habit of re-deriving each of these choices (probe timing, scaling signal, storage pattern, isolation strategy) from first principles for the actual workload shape, rather than inheriting them from whatever the last non-GPU service in the cluster happened to use.

16. SUMMARY

  • Kubernetes became the default AI infrastructure substrate because it already solved general scheduling, reconciliation, and lifecycle-management problems that AI workloads also need — it was extended for GPUs via device plugins, not redesigned from scratch.
  • The core mental model is a continuous control loop: you declare desired state, and controllers perpetually reconcile reality toward it — this explains almost every Kubernetes behavior you will encounter, from Deployments to autoscalers.
  • GPUs are fundamentally different from CPU/memory in Kubernetes' resource model — indivisible by default, requests must equal limits, and this single fact explains most of the operational pitfalls unique to AI workloads on this platform.
  • Readiness and liveness probes are the single most important, most frequently misconfigured mechanism for AI workloads, because model servers have startup profiles wildly different from typical web services.
  • Autoscaling must be keyed on signals that actually correlate with the real bottleneck — GPU utilization or queue depth for model-serving tiers, not CPU — or the autoscaler will confidently fail to react when it matters most.
  • Your Spring AI application code stays decoupled from all of this through the ChatModel/ChatClient abstraction talking to a stable Service DNS name; the Kubernetes-specific engineering lives in manifests, probes, taints, and autoscaling policy, not in your business logic.

Next chapter: Kubernetes gives you the substrate to run and scale individual AI workloads reliably on one cluster, but real production AI systems rarely stay confined to a single cluster or a single point of coordination — model servers, vector stores, caches, and orchestration layers end up spread across nodes, zones, and sometimes regions, each with its own latency, consistency, and partial-failure behavior. Distributed Systems for AI (Chapter 73) picks up exactly there: the theory and patterns you need once "reliable" has to mean reliable across a network, not just reliable on one machine.
