CHAPTER 53 — EVALUATION

Volume 3 — Embeddings & RAG · Chapter 13 of 13

Enterprise RAG (Chapter 52) gave you a hardened, multi-tenant, access-controlled retrieval pipeline — chunking strategy, hybrid search, reranking, guardrails, all wired together into something you could actually put in front of paying customers. But you shipped it on the strength of a demo and your own judgment about which answers "looked right." That is not a foundation you can build a career, a team, or a production incident review on. This chapter closes out Volume 3 by giving you the missing discipline: how to measure, numerically and repeatably, whether a RAG system is actually working — before your users find out for you, and before your next pull request silently makes retrieval quality worse.

1. MOTIVATION

Every chapter in this volume has built toward a single deliverable: a system that retrieves relevant context and generates grounded answers. Nothing in that pipeline, however, tells you whether it is good. You chunked documents (Chapter... in this volume), you embedded them, you built hybrid search, you added a reranker, you wrapped it in guardrails — and at every one of those steps you made a choice: chunk size 512 or 1024, top-k 5 or 10, cosine similarity or dot product, one reranker model or another. Each of those choices moved system quality up or down by some amount you almost certainly did not measure. That is the gap this chapter closes.

As a Java/Spring engineer, you already have a name for what is missing: a test suite. You would never merge a pull request that changes a payment calculation without running the tests that pin down expected totals. Yet it is astonishingly common — even at well-funded companies — to ship a change to a chunking strategy, a prompt template, or a model version with nothing more rigorous than "I tried a few questions and it seemed fine." The reason this happens is not laziness; it is that the tests you already know how to write (exact-match assertions) do not apply to a system whose correct output is not a single fixed string. Evaluation, in the AI engineering sense, is the discipline of building a test suite for a system that has no single correct answer.

Concretely, evaluation is what lets you answer four questions that will come up in every single RAG project you ever ship: "did this change make things better or worse?", "which failures are retrieval's fault versus generation's fault?", "are we safe to ship this to production?", and — the one that ends careers when answered wrong — "how do we know the system is still working three months from now, after the underlying model provider silently updated their model?" Without evaluation, every one of those questions gets answered by gut feel, and gut feel does not survive a postmortem.

This chapter is also a hinge point in the book. Everything before it in Volume 3 was about building capability — making the system able to retrieve and generate. Everything from here forward, starting with Agent Fundamentals in the next chapter, assumes you can measure whether a capability actually works, because agents compound RAG's non-determinism with multi-step decision-making, and an unmeasured agent is unmanageable. Evaluation is the discipline that makes the rest of this book buildable in a responsible way.

2. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

2.1 Evaluation Before Language Models: Classical ML Metrics

Long before LLMs, machine learning practitioners already had a mature evaluation discipline — because a classifier or a ranking model also does not produce a single deterministic correct answer for every input, it produces a distribution of outcomes across a test set. Precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC, and mean average precision (MAP) were all developed to answer one question: across many examples, how often does the system get it right, and what kind of wrong does it get? Information retrieval specifically — the direct ancestor of the "R" in RAG — built an entire sub-field around metrics like Precision@k, Recall@k, and NDCG (Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain) decades before vector databases existed, evaluating search engines by having human judges label which documents were relevant to a query and then scoring ranked result lists against those judgments.

This history matters directly: RAG evaluation did not need to invent retrieval metrics from scratch. It inherited them wholesale from classical information retrieval, then had to solve a genuinely new problem layered on top — evaluating the generated text that gets produced from that retrieved context, which is where things got harder.

2.2 Evaluation for Text Generation: The BLEU/ROUGE Era

Machine translation and summarization systems faced the generation-evaluation problem first. Their answer, starting in the early-to-mid 2000s, was n-gram overlap metrics: BLEU (for translation) and ROUGE (for summarization) both work by comparing a generated text against one or more human-written "reference" texts and counting how many overlapping word sequences (n-grams) appear in both. If your machine translation shares 80% of its 4-word sequences with a professional human translation, BLEU scores it highly.

These metrics were a genuine advance — they let researchers compare systems without paying human judges for every single evaluation run — but they had a well-documented and important flaw for this book's purposes: they measure surface lexical overlap, not meaning. "The cat sat on the mat" and "A feline rested upon the rug" mean nearly the same thing and share almost no n-grams. A RAG system's generated answer can be completely correct and helpful while scoring poorly on BLEU/ROUGE simply because it phrased things differently than a reference answer — which made these metrics a poor fit for open-ended, free-form answers, exactly the kind an LLM produces.

2.3 The LLM-as-Judge Turn

The pivot that defines modern RAG and LLM evaluation, roughly from 2022 onward, is the realization that a capable LLM can itself be used to grade another model's output against a rubric — reading a question, a generated answer, and (for RAG) the retrieved context, and producing a judgment ("is this answer supported by the context? yes/no/partially") along with reasoning. This is called LLM-as-judge, and it is now the dominant technique underlying most modern RAG evaluation frameworks, including the ones you will use directly in Java projects (RAGAS-style metrics, and Spring AI's own evaluation abstractions covered later in this chapter). It is not a magic bullet — Section 11 covers exactly where it fails — but it solved the core problem that BLEU/ROUGE could not: it evaluates for semantic correctness and faithfulness rather than string overlap, at a cost and speed that scales to continuous production monitoring instead of only expensive one-off human studies.

EraCore TechniqueGood ForBlind Spot
Classical IR (pre-2010s)Precision/Recall/NDCG against human relevance judgmentsRanking quality of retrieved documentsSays nothing about generated text quality
MT/Summarization era (2000s–2010s)BLEU, ROUGE (n-gram overlap)Cheap, automatic, reproducible generation scoringPenalizes correct paraphrases; rewards surface overlap over meaning
Embedding similarity era (2018+)BERTScore and similar (compare embeddings, not tokens)Captures paraphrase/semantic closeness better than n-gramsStill needs a reference answer; no notion of "grounded in this context"

Why this history matters to you: notice the same pattern from the "What Is Intelligence" chapter's history table — each era's bottleneck relocated rather than disappeared. BLEU solved "we can't pay humans to grade every run" but created "surface overlap isn't meaning." LLM-as-judge solved "we need semantic, reference-free grading" but created "who evaluates the evaluator?" — a question this chapter answers directly in Section 11.

3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

Strip away the tooling and vendor names, and RAG evaluation reduces to one precise engineering problem:

Given a system with two independently failable stages — retrieval and generation — build a repeatable, automatable process that tells you (a) how good the retrieved context is, (b) how good the generated answer is given that context, and (c) whether a proposed change made either of those better or worse, without requiring a human to manually re-read every output on every change.

This decomposition into "retrieval quality" and "generation quality" is the single most important structural idea in this chapter, and it is worth dwelling on why it must be decomposed rather than measured as one end-to-end blob. If a RAG system gives a wrong answer, there are exactly two possible root causes, and they demand completely different fixes:

  • Retrieval failure: the correct information was never retrieved (or was retrieved but ranked too low to make it into the context window). The generator never had a chance — no amount of prompt engineering fixes a model that was never shown the right document. The fix lives in chunking, embedding model choice, hybrid search weighting, or reranking (all covered in earlier chapters of this volume).
  • Generation failure: the correct information WAS retrieved and present in the context, but the model ignored it, misread it, or hallucinated something else on top of it anyway. The fix lives in prompt design, model choice, or output constraints — retrieval is innocent here and re-tuning it would be wasted effort.

An evaluation approach that only measures "was the final answer correct?" (an end-to-end score) cannot tell these two failure modes apart. You could spend a sprint re-tuning your reranker to fix a bug that was actually in your prompt template, or vice versa. This is precisely analogous to a Spring engineer debugging a slow HTTP endpoint without distinguishing "the database query is slow" from "the serialization is slow" — you need per-stage instrumentation, not just an end-to-end latency number, to know where to spend engineering effort. Evaluation is the AI-engineering equivalent of that per-stage instrumentation, and RAGAS-style frameworks are built explicitly around this retrieval/generation split.

A second, equally practical dimension of the problem: evaluation must work at two different tempos. Offline evaluation runs against a curated, labeled test set before you ship a change — this is your CI test suite equivalent, and it must be fast, cheap enough to run on every pull request, and stable enough that a score change means a real quality change, not noise. Online evaluation runs continuously against real production traffic, where you have no labels and no ground truth, and must instead rely on proxy signals (user feedback, LLM-judge scoring of live traffic samples, and behavioral signals like reformulation rate or session abandonment). A mature evaluation strategy needs both, because offline sets can never fully represent the long tail of real user queries — the same generalization problem from the "What Is Intelligence" chapter reappears here as "your eval set does not generalize to production traffic," and it is a genuine, recurring failure mode.

4. THEORY

4.1 The Core Mental Model: Evaluation Is Measurement Under Uncertainty, Not a Pass/Fail Gate

The single biggest misconception engineers new to this bring from traditional testing is expecting evaluation to produce a boolean: "does it work, yes or no?" It does not, and trying to force it into that shape leads to brittle, gameable evaluation suites. The correct mental model, borrowed directly from classical ML, is a scorecard across multiple, sometimes competing dimensions, measured against a representative sample, with an explicit acceptable threshold you decide as an engineering and product judgment call — not a truth handed down by the metric itself. A RAG system might score 0.91 on faithfulness and 0.68 on answer relevance; whether that is "good enough to ship" is a decision your team makes, informed by the number, not dictated by it.

4.2 The Core Dimensions of RAG Quality

Four dimensions recur across every serious RAG evaluation framework. Learn these as vocabulary — they are used identically across RAGAS, Spring AI's evaluator interfaces, and most vendor evaluation products:

DimensionQuestion It AnswersStage It MeasuresAnalogy
Context PrecisionOf the chunks retrieved, how many were actually relevant/useful?RetrievalLike precision in a search engine: are we drowning the model in noise?
Context RecallOf all the relevant information that existed in the corpus, how much did we actually retrieve?RetrievalLike recall in a search engine: did we miss the one document that mattered?
Faithfulness (a.k.a. Groundedness)Is every claim in the generated answer actually supported by the retrieved context?GenerationFact-checking a journalist's article against their cited sources
Answer RelevanceDoes the generated answer actually address the user's question (even if faithful, it can be off-topic)?GenerationA technically accurate but unhelpful answer to a different question than was asked

Notice that faithfulness and factual correctness are not the same thing, and conflating them is one of the most consequential mistakes in this entire discipline. Faithfulness asks "is this claim supported by the context I gave the model?" — it says nothing about whether the context itself was true. If your vector store contains an outdated document claiming your product supports a feature it no longer supports, a perfectly faithful model will confidently repeat that stale, wrong information, scoring high on faithfulness while being factually incorrect in the real world. This is why context recall/precision and faithfulness must be evaluated together, and why "evaluate the RAG system" always implicitly includes "evaluate the corpus you are retrieving from," which is a data quality problem, not a model problem.

4.3 Reference-Based vs. Reference-Free Evaluation

A second theoretical axis, orthogonal to the four dimensions above, is whether a metric needs a human-written "gold" answer to compare against:

  • Reference-based metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore, exact-match, and "LLM-judge comparing against a gold answer") require you to have already written down a correct answer for each test question. They are precise but expensive to build and cannot scale to arbitrary live production traffic where no one has written the "correct" answer in advance.
  • Reference-free metrics (faithfulness-against-context, answer relevance-against-question, LLM-judge rubrics with no gold answer) need no pre-written correct answer — they check internal consistency (does the answer follow from the context? does it address the question?) rather than comparing to a ground truth. This is precisely what makes them usable in production, on live traffic, where no gold answer exists.

The practical takeaway: build a small, high-quality, reference-based "golden set" for offline regression testing (Section 8's CI pipeline), and rely on reference-free metrics for continuous production monitoring, where writing gold answers for every query is impossible.

5. INTERNAL WORKING

This section walks through exactly how an LLM-as-judge evaluation actually executes, mechanically, because "an LLM grades another LLM's output" sounds circular and mysterious until you see the concrete steps.

5.1 Step-by-Step: Evaluating Faithfulness

  1. Collect the triple. For a single evaluation instance you need three things: the user's question, the context chunks that were actually retrieved and passed to the generator, and the generated answer. This is why evaluation must be wired into your RAG pipeline at the point where all three are simultaneously available — bolting evaluation on after the fact, with only the final answer logged, throws away the information needed to diagnose retrieval versus generation failures (Section 3).
  2. Decompose the answer into atomic claims. A well-built faithfulness evaluator does not judge the whole answer as one blob. It first asks a judge LLM to break the generated answer into a list of individual factual statements — this is a generation step, not a scoring step. For example, "Spring AI supports OpenAI, Anthropic, and Ollama through a common ChatModel interface" decomposes into three atomic claims: (1) Spring AI supports OpenAI, (2) Spring AI supports Anthropic, (3) Spring AI supports Ollama — each independently checkable.
  3. Verify each claim against the context. For each atomic claim, the judge LLM is asked a narrow, binary-leaning question: "is this claim directly supported by the following context: [retrieved chunks]?" This step is deliberately narrow in scope — a single true/false-style judgment per claim is far more reliable than asking a judge to holistically score an entire paragraph, because narrow judgments are less prone to the judge being swayed by fluency or tangential correctness.
  4. Aggregate into a score. Faithfulness is then computed as (number of claims supported by context) / (total number of claims). An answer with 4 out of 5 claims grounded in context scores 0.8. This aggregation step is why faithfulness is described as a ratio metric, not a binary pass/fail — a mostly-faithful answer with one unsupported embellishment is meaningfully different from a completely fabricated one, and the score should reflect that gradation.

5.2 Step-by-Step: Evaluating Context Precision and Recall

These two retrieval metrics work differently because they don't need a judge to read generated text — they need a judge (or, if you have them, human labels) to assess relevance of retrieved chunks against the question, independent of what the generator did with them:

  1. For each retrieved chunk, judge relevance. Ask: "is this chunk relevant to answering this question?" — yes/no per chunk, in retrieved rank order.
  2. Compute Context Precision by weighting relevant chunks by their rank position (a relevant chunk at position 1 counts more than one buried at position 8) — this rewards not just retrieving relevant material, but ranking it near the top, which matters because context windows and reranking cutoffs mean low-ranked chunks may never even reach the generator.
  3. Compute Context Recall by comparing retrieved chunks against a reference answer (this metric is necessarily reference-based): for each factual statement in the reference answer, check whether it can be attributed to something in the retrieved context. If the reference answer contains a fact that no retrieved chunk supports, that is a recall miss — the corpus may have had the answer, but retrieval failed to surface it.

5.3 Edge Cases a Careful Engineer Must Reason Through

  • The "unanswerable question" case. If the correct behavior is "I don't know, this isn't in my knowledge base" and the model correctly says so, a naive answer-relevance metric may score this low (it doesn't "answer" the question) when it should score highly (correct behavior for an unanswerable query). Evaluation sets must explicitly include unanswerable questions with an expected "refusal" behavior, scored by a different rubric than answerable ones.
  • The "partially faithful but still useful" case. An answer that is 90% grounded with one minor unsupported flourish is very different in risk profile from one that is 90% grounded with one fabricated statistic — claim-level severity weighting, not just claim-count ratios, matters for high-stakes domains (health, finance, legal).
  • Context that contradicts itself. If two retrieved chunks disagree (a common real-world case with stale documents mixed into a corpus), a faithful model might reasonably pick either side, and a naive faithfulness check might penalize a correct-but-selective answer. This is a genuine open edge case; the practical mitigation is corpus hygiene (deduplication and staleness detection, covered under Enterprise RAG) rather than a purely evaluation-side fix.

6. MATHEMATICS

RAG evaluation math is intentionally light — the field deliberately favors interpretable ratio metrics over complex statistics, precisely because interpretability lets a whole engineering team reason about a score without a statistics background. This section covers the handful of formulas you will actually see in tooling and papers.

6.1 Precision, Recall, and F1 — Recap With RAG-Specific Framing

                  relevant chunks retrieved
Context Precision = ─────────────────────────────
                     total chunks retrieved

                  relevant chunks retrieved
Context Recall    = ─────────────────────────────
                     total relevant chunks that EXIST in the corpus

                   2 · Precision · Recall
F1                = ────────────────────────
                     Precision + Recall

Intuition: precision punishes noise (retrieving junk alongside good chunks — this costs context window space and can distract the generator), recall punishes blind spots (missing the one chunk that had the answer — this costs correctness outright, and no downstream generation step can recover a fact retrieval never surfaced). F1 is the harmonic mean, useful as a single number when you must compare configurations but need both dimensions represented — remember, though, that collapsing to one number always discards information, which is why dashboards should show precision and recall separately, not just F1.

6.2 Faithfulness as a Ratio

                 number of claims in the answer supported by context
Faithfulness  =  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
                 total number of claims in the answer

This is the atomic-claim-verification process from Section 5.1 expressed as a formula — nothing more exotic than a supported-claims count divided by a total-claims count. The apparent simplicity is deliberate; the hard part of this metric is entirely in Section 5's decomposition-and-verification mechanism, not in the arithmetic.

6.3 NDCG — Why Rank Position Matters, Not Just Presence

Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain formalizes the intuition that a relevant document at rank 1 is worth more than the same document at rank 10, because a reranker or top-k cutoff may never let a low-ranked chunk reach the generator at all: 

                 relevance of item at rank i
DCG@k  =  Σ  ─────────────────────────────────    for i = 1 to k
                 log2(i + 1)

NDCG@k =  DCG@k / IDCG@k     (IDCG = DCG of the ideal, perfectly-ranked order)

The logarithmic denominator is the whole trick: it discounts the contribution of a relevant item more and more heavily the further down the ranking it sits, which is exactly the shape of real user (and real generator) attention — items near the top matter disproportionately. Normalizing by IDCG (the best possible ranking's score) turns the raw number into a 0-to-1 scale comparable across queries with different numbers of relevant documents, which is what makes it usable as a dashboard metric rather than a research curiosity.

You will rarely hand-compute any of this — evaluation libraries compute it for you — but knowing what the number represents is what lets you distinguish "our retriever found the right chunk but ranked it 9th" (an NDCG/reranking problem) from "our retriever never found the right chunk at all" (a recall problem), which again require entirely different fixes.

Without this loop, your offline golden set stays frozen at whatever it looked like on day one while real user traffic drifts further and further from it — the classic "eval set doesn't generalize" failure from Section 3, made worse over time instead of better.

9. JAVA / SPRING PERSPECTIVE

Spring AI treats evaluation as a first-class concern rather than an afterthought bolted onto testing, via its Evaluator abstraction in the org.springframework.ai.evaluation package. The core contract is simple and idiomatically Spring: an Evaluator accepts an EvaluationRequest (bundling the user query, the retrieved context/documents, and the generated response) and returns an EvaluationResponse carrying a pass/fail-style result plus supporting metadata. Spring AI ships a RelevancyEvaluator and a FactCheckingEvaluator out of the box, both implemented by delegating the actual judgment to a ChatModel call — which is precisely the LLM-as-judge mechanism from Section 5, wired into a familiar Spring interface rather than a bespoke evaluation DSL.

This design choice matters for the same reason the "What Is Intelligence" chapter's Java/Spring table matters: it lets you swap the judge model independently of the model doing the actual generation. A common, cost-conscious production pattern is generating answers with a faster/cheaper model and judging faithfulness with a stronger, more careful model — because a judge's mistakes silently corrupt every downstream decision made from its scores, so judge quality deserves its own budget line, distinct from generation cost. 

// Conceptual sketch — evaluating a RAG answer with Spring AI's evaluator abstraction

@Service
public class RagFaithfulnessCheck {

    private final ChatClient judgeClient;   // built from a ChatModel, e.g. a
                                             // stronger model reserved for judging
    private final FactCheckingEvaluator factCheckingEvaluator;

    public RagFaithfulnessCheck(ChatClient.Builder builder, ChatModel judgeModel) {
        this.judgeClient = builder.build();
        this.factCheckingEvaluator = new FactCheckingEvaluator(
                ChatClient.builder(judgeModel));
    }

    public EvaluationResponse checkFaithfulness(
            String userQuestion,
            List retrievedContext,
            String generatedAnswer) {

        EvaluationRequest request = new EvaluationRequest(
                userQuestion,
                retrievedContext,
                generatedAnswer);

        return factCheckingEvaluator.evaluate(request);
        // response.isPass() == false  ->  route to fallback / human review,
        //                                 log for offline golden-set triage
    }
}

In a typical Spring Boot testing setup, this same Evaluator gets invoked from a JUnit 5 test that iterates your golden test set — not asserting exact string equality against a generated answer (which would flake constantly, as the "What Is Intelligence" chapter's Common Mistakes section warned), but asserting that the evaluator's pass rate across the whole set stays above your team's agreed threshold: 

@SpringBootTest
class RagRegressionTest {

    @Autowired RagPipeline ragPipeline;
    @Autowired FactCheckingEvaluator evaluator;

    @Test
    void faithfulnessStaysAboveThreshold() {
        List cases = GoldenSetLoader.load("golden-set-v7.json");
        long passed = cases.stream()
                .map(c -> ragPipeline.answer(c.question()))
                .filter(result -> evaluator
                        .evaluate(new EvaluationRequest(
                                result.question(), result.context(), result.answer()))
                        .isPass())
                .count();

        double passRate = (double) passed / cases.size();
        assertThat(passRate).isGreaterThanOrEqualTo(0.90);  // team-agreed bar
    }
}

This is the direct, concrete answer to Section 3's "how do you build a CI test suite for a system with no single correct answer" — you don't assert on individual outputs, you assert on an aggregate pass rate across a representative set, exactly the way a classical ML team asserts "accuracy must stay above 92%" rather than "example #47 must classify as exactly 'spam'."

10. BEST PRACTICES

  • Build your golden set from real production data, not invented questions. Engineer-invented test questions are systematically biased toward well-phrased, textbook-style queries; real users ask messy, ambiguous, multi-part questions, and your golden set must reflect that or it will pass in CI and fail in production.
  • Always evaluate retrieval and generation separately, even when you also track an end-to-end score. Section 3 and Section 7.2's decision tree exist because a single blended number cannot tell you where to fix the system.
  • Use a stronger, separately-budgeted model as the judge whenever feasible. A judge that is weaker than the model it is grading tends to be an unreliable judge — it may not even understand the nuance it is asked to evaluate.
  • Version your golden set and your evaluation prompts together, and track scores against that version over time. A faithfulness score of 0.85 is meaningless without knowing which test set and which judge prompt produced it — treat both as artifacts under version control, exactly like application code.
  • Set explicit, written thresholds before you see the numbers. Deciding "0.8 faithfulness is our bar" after already seeing your system scores 0.79 is a well-documented bias trap; agree on the bar with stakeholders in advance, the same discipline good SLOs require.
  • Feed every production incident back into the golden set (Section 8's feedback loop). An evaluation suite that never grows from real failures calcifies and stops reflecting reality.
  • Include explicitly unanswerable and adversarial questions in your golden set, not just answerable ones — correct refusal behavior is a real, gradeable capability (Section 5.3), and skipping it means you never notice a system that has started confidently fabricating answers to questions it should decline.

11. COMMON MISTAKES

  • Treating a single blended "accuracy" score as sufficient. As covered in Section 3, this hides whether failures are retrieval's fault or generation's fault, wasting engineering effort on the wrong fix.
  • Confusing faithfulness with factual correctness. A model can be perfectly faithful to a stale or wrong document and score well on faithfulness while being completely wrong in the real world (Section 4.2) — corpus quality must be evaluated as its own concern.
  • Using the same model as both generator and judge with no independent check. A model is prone to rating its own output favorably (a documented bias in LLM-as-judge literature), and errors it systematically makes as a generator, it will often fail to catch as a judge for the same underlying reason.
  • Overfitting the system to the golden set. If engineers iterate prompts by staring at golden-set failures and patching each one individually, the system starts memorizing the test set's specific phrasings rather than improving general quality — the classical ML overfitting problem, reborn in prompt engineering. Hold out a portion of your golden set that is never used for iteration, only for final validation.
  • Never updating the golden set after it is built. Corpora change, user behavior changes, and models get upgraded by providers — a golden set frozen at launch silently drifts out of relevance exactly as described in Section 8.
  • Trusting LLM-judge scores as ground truth without any human spot-checking. Judges have their own failure modes — verbosity bias (favoring longer answers), position bias (favoring whichever answer appears first when comparing two), and susceptibility to confidently-worded wrong answers exactly like the underlying hallucination problem. Periodic human audits of a sample of judge decisions are not optional if the eval suite gates production deploys.
  • Evaluating only "golden path" happy queries. Skipping edge cases like empty retrieval results, contradictory context, or multi-hop questions requiring synthesis across several chunks leaves exactly the failure modes most likely to embarrass you in production untested.

12. PERFORMANCE CONSIDERATIONS

Evaluation has its own cost and latency profile, and treating it as free is a common early-stage mistake:

  • LLM-as-judge is not free. Faithfulness evaluation via claim decomposition (Section 5.1) issues multiple additional model calls per evaluated example — one to decompose the answer into claims, and typically one per claim (or one batched call) to verify each against context. Running this over a 1,000-example golden set on every pull request adds real, recurring token cost and CI time; many teams run a smaller "fast" subset on every PR and the full set nightly or before release.
  • Offline evaluation should be parallelized the same way you would parallelize an integration test suite — each golden-set example is independent, so batch/concurrent execution against the judge model materially shortens CI feedback loops.
  • Online (production) evaluation must sample, not evaluate every request, both for cost reasons and because judge-model latency would otherwise sit on your critical request path if run synchronously — always run production evaluation asynchronously, off the response path, against logged data.
  • Cache judge results for identical (question, context, answer) triples when re-running the same golden set repeatedly during rapid local iteration — this is the same caching instinct the "What Is Intelligence" chapter's Java/Spring table already named as transferable.
  • Retrieval-only metrics (precision/recall/NDCG) are far cheaper than generation metrics because they don't require an LLM call at all if you have pre-existing relevance judgments — prefer running these on every commit, and reserve the more expensive LLM-judge generation metrics for a slightly less frequent cadence if cost is a genuine constraint.

13. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

Evaluation's security surface is narrower than, say, retrieval's or tool-calling's, but it is not zero, and it is worth being precise about where it actually matters rather than padding this section artificially.

  • Judge-model prompt injection via evaluated content. If your evaluation harness ever scores live, untrusted production content (for instance, evaluating an answer that incorporated retrieved content from a user-editable knowledge base), that content reaches the judge model's context the same way it reaches the generator's — meaning the same prompt injection risk flagged in the "What Is Intelligence" chapter's security section applies to your judge, not just your generator. A cleverly crafted document could attempt to instruct the judge to always score "pass" regardless of actual faithfulness.
  • Evaluation data as a sensitive asset. Golden sets built from real production queries can contain personally identifiable information or sensitive business data pulled directly from user traffic (Section 10's best practice). Treat golden-set storage and access with the same controls as production data, not as disposable test fixtures — a leaked golden set can leak real customer questions and internal document contents.
  • Gaming the evaluation suite is a real organizational risk, not just an academic one. If compensation, launch decisions, or team OKRs are tied directly to a specific eval score, there is a structural incentive to tune the system (or worse, the eval prompts themselves) to that specific metric rather than to genuine quality — a well-known phenomenon (Goodhart's Law: "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"). Mitigate by rotating/refreshing golden sets, holding out validation subsets (Section 11), and pairing automated scores with periodic human review before high-stakes launch decisions.

Beyond these, evaluation is largely a measurement and observability concern rather than an attack surface in its own right — most of the substantive security engineering for a RAG system lives in the retrieval, tool-calling, and guardrail layers covered elsewhere in this book, and this chapter will not manufacture additional security depth where the topic genuinely does not carry much.

14. INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

JUNIORQ1. Why can't you evaluate a RAG system's answers with simple exact-match unit tests the way you would test a normal Java method?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

An LLM generates free-form text, and the same correct meaning can be phrased in countless different ways — there is no single fixed "correct string" to assert equality against, unlike a deterministic function like reverse("hello"). Instead, RAG systems are evaluated statistically: metrics like faithfulness and relevance are scored per example (often by another LLM acting as a judge) and then aggregated into a pass rate or average score across a representative test set, with an agreed threshold determining whether the system is "good enough," analogous to accuracy thresholds in classical ML testing.

JUNIORQ2. What is the difference between context precision and context recall in RAG evaluation?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Context precision measures how much of what you retrieved was actually relevant (noise vs. signal in your retrieved set) — low precision means the generator is being distracted by irrelevant chunks. Context recall measures how much of the relevant information that exists in the corpus was actually retrieved — low recall means the retriever missed information the answer needed, and no amount of good generation can recover from that, since the model never saw the missing fact.

MIDQ3. Explain what "faithfulness" measures in RAG evaluation, and why it is not the same as factual correctness.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Faithfulness measures whether every claim in the generated answer is actually supported by the retrieved context — it is typically computed by decomposing the answer into atomic claims and checking each one against the context, then scoring the ratio of supported claims to total claims. It is not the same as factual correctness because it says nothing about whether the retrieved context itself was true or current — a model can be perfectly faithful to a stale or wrong document in the corpus, scoring high on faithfulness while being wrong about the real world. This is why corpus quality (freshness, accuracy of source documents) must be evaluated as a separate concern from faithfulness.

MIDQ4. How does LLM-as-judge evaluation actually work, mechanically?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

A capable LLM is prompted to grade another model's output against a specific rubric, rather than being asked to holistically judge quality in one shot. For faithfulness specifically: the judge first decomposes the generated answer into individual atomic factual claims, then for each claim asks a narrow, focused question — "is this claim supported by the given context?" — and the final score is the ratio of supported claims to total claims. Narrow, per-claim judgments are more reliable than one holistic judgment because they reduce the judge's susceptibility to being swayed by overall fluency or tangential correctness.

SENIORQ5. A production RAG system's answers are wrong. Walk through how you would determine whether the root cause is retrieval or generation, and how your fix would differ.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

First check whether the correct information was even present in the retrieved context for that query. If it was absent, this is a retrieval (context recall) failure — the generator never had a chance, and the fix lives upstream: chunking strategy, embedding model choice, hybrid search weighting, or reranker tuning. If the correct information WAS present in the context but the answer still contradicts or ignores it, this is a generation (faithfulness) failure — the fix lives in prompt design, model choice, or output constraints, and re-tuning retrieval would be wasted effort. A third case worth checking: if the context was present but itself factually wrong or stale, this is a corpus freshness/quality failure, distinct from both retrieval and generation, requiring data pipeline fixes rather than model or search tuning. Diagnosing which of the three applies before touching any code is the entire point of separating retrieval and generation metrics rather than relying on a single end-to-end accuracy score.

Interview Gold Point: Explicitly name all three failure categories (retrieval recall miss, generation faithfulness miss, corpus staleness/quality) rather than just the two obvious ones — most candidates stop at retrieval-vs-generation and miss that faithful-but-wrong answers are a distinct, common production failure mode requiring data-layer fixes, not model or search retuning.
SENIORQ6. How would you design an offline evaluation pipeline that runs on every pull request touching your RAG system, without making CI unbearably slow or expensive?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Maintain a versioned golden test set built from real production queries, including answerable, unanswerable, and adversarial cases. Run cheaper, non-LLM retrieval metrics (precision/recall/NDCG against pre-existing relevance judgments) on every commit since they require no model calls. Run the more expensive LLM-judge generation metrics (faithfulness, answer relevance) on a smaller, fast subset on every PR for quick feedback, executed concurrently across examples to shorten wall-clock time, and run the full golden set nightly or pre-release. Gate merges on regression against a tracked baseline (e.g., fail if faithfulness drops more than an agreed threshold versus the last known-good score) rather than an absolute bar alone, since absolute thresholds can mask meaningful regressions that stay above the bar. Feed every production incident discovered post-deploy back into the golden set to keep it representative over time.

SENIORQ7. What are the known failure modes of using an LLM as a judge, and how do you mitigate them?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Known failure modes include: self-bias (a model favoring its own outputs when used as both generator and judge), verbosity bias (favoring longer answers regardless of actual quality), position bias (favoring whichever option appears first in a pairwise comparison), susceptibility to confidently-worded but wrong content (the judge inherits the same plausibility-over-truth weakness as any LLM), and vulnerability to prompt injection from evaluated content that instructs the judge to score favorably. Mitigations: use a separate, typically stronger model as judge rather than the generator itself; use narrow, per-claim binary judgments instead of holistic scoring to reduce susceptibility to sway; randomize ordering in pairwise comparisons to cancel position bias; periodically human-audit a sample of judge decisions, especially before high-stakes launch gates; and treat any content reaching the judge's context as a potential injection vector requiring the same sanitization discipline as the generator's input.

STAFFQ8. Your team's RAG evaluation dashboard has shown a stable 92% faithfulness score for months, yet customer complaints about wrong answers have been rising. How do you investigate, and what structural issue might this reveal?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

A stable offline score alongside rising real-world complaints is the textbook signature of an evaluation set that no longer represents production traffic — the golden set has calcified while user behavior, the underlying corpus, or the upstream model provider have drifted. Investigation should pull a fresh, unbiased sample of recent production traffic (not cherry-picked complaint tickets alone, which are themselves a biased sample), run it through both retrieval and generation metrics, and compare distributions against the golden set's query patterns — differences in question complexity, topic coverage, or multi-hop reasoning demand are common culprits. It is also worth checking whether faithfulness is silently being preserved against an increasingly stale or incorrect corpus (Section 4.2's faithfulness-vs-correctness gap) — a corpus quality regression would not move faithfulness scores at all while genuinely degrading real-world correctness. The structural fix is establishing the production feedback loop from Section 8: every substantiated complaint becomes a new golden-set case, and the golden set is refreshed on a cadence tied to observed traffic drift, not left frozen since initial launch.

16. SUMMARY

  • RAG evaluation exists because generated text has no single correct answer — it replaces exact-match testing with statistical scoring across a representative test set, aggregated into pass rates against explicitly agreed thresholds.
  • Retrieval and generation must always be evaluated separately (context precision/recall vs. faithfulness/answer relevance) because they fail independently and demand entirely different fixes — collapsing them into one end-to-end score hides which stage actually broke.
  • Faithfulness (is the answer grounded in the retrieved context?) is not the same as factual correctness (is the context itself true?) — a system can be perfectly faithful to a stale or wrong document, meaning corpus quality must be tracked as its own concern.
  • LLM-as-judge is the dominant modern technique, working by decomposing answers into atomic claims and verifying each narrowly against context, but it inherits real biases (self-favoritism, verbosity, position bias, injection susceptibility) that require independent judge models and periodic human audits.
  • Mature evaluation runs at two tempos — offline golden-set regression testing gating CI/CD, and online reference-free monitoring of live production traffic — connected by a feedback loop where every real production failure graduates into a new offline test case.
  • Spring AI operationalizes all of this through its Evaluator abstraction (RelevancyEvaluator, FactCheckingEvaluator), letting evaluation be wired into ordinary Spring Boot tests as aggregate pass-rate assertions rather than exact-match assertions.
