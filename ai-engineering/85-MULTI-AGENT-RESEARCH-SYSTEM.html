CHAPTER 85 — MULTI-AGENT RESEARCH SYSTEM

Volume 6 — Production Projects · Chapter 10 of 12

The Document Intelligence Platform (Chapter 84) taught you how to make one well-orchestrated pipeline extract structured answers from a fixed corpus of documents you already control. Real research questions rarely arrive with a fixed corpus attached — they arrive open-ended ("compare the regulatory approaches to AI liability across the EU, US, and Japan and flag the contradictions"), require exploring dozens of independent leads no single context window can hold at once, and reward breadth as much as depth. This chapter builds the system that answers that class of question: a lead agent that plans and delegates, a fleet of subagents that explore in parallel, and a synthesis layer that turns scattered findings into one trustworthy report — the most architecturally demanding project in this volume because it is the first one where the "agents" are plural and must coordinate.

1. MOTIVATION

Every AI system you have built so far in this series, however sophisticated, has had a single locus of reasoning: one ChatClient call, wrapped in advisors, perhaps augmented by retrieval or a handful of tool calls, but ultimately one context window doing one linear chain of thinking per request. That architecture has a hard ceiling, and open-ended research is exactly the workload that hits it. Ask a single agent to "research the competitive landscape for on-device LLM inference chips" and you force it into an impossible sequencing problem: it must decide what to search first, read the results, decide what to search second based on what it just read, and so on, entirely serially, inside one context window that is simultaneously accumulating search results, partial notes, and the eventual report — all while the clock and the token budget run out.

A multi-agent research system exists to break that ceiling in the same way a distributed system breaks the ceiling of a single machine: by decomposing a large, loosely-coupled problem into independent units of work that can run in parallel, each with its own working memory, and by introducing an orchestration layer whose only job is planning and integration rather than doing the legwork itself. This is not a novelty pattern — it is the direct product-shaped consequence of everything Volume 4 taught about agents and tool use, scaled from "one agent doing one task" to "one planner directing many specialized workers," which is precisely the architecture used by production research assistants that need to synthesize across dozens of sources in minutes rather than hours.

For you as a Java/Spring engineer, this chapter is also where "agent" stops being an abstraction you reason about in isolation and becomes a distributed systems problem you already know how to think about: task decomposition, parallel execution, partial failure, result aggregation, and idempotency all reappear here with an LLM standing in for the unit of work. If you have designed a fan-out/fan-in batch job or a scatter-gather API composition layer, you already have most of the mental scaffolding this chapter needs — you are applying it to a new kind of worker.

2. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

The idea of splitting a task across multiple cooperating "agents" is old in distributed AI and multi-agent systems research, long predating LLMs — auction-based task allocation, blackboard architectures, and swarm robotics all wrestled with how autonomous units coordinate toward a shared goal without a central bottleneck. What changed with LLMs is not the coordination theory but the nature of the worker: instead of a narrow, hand-coded agent that can only do one thing, each worker is now a general-purpose reasoning engine that can be instructed, in natural language, to pursue almost any sub-goal.

2.1 The Single-Agent ReAct Era

The first wave of LLM agents, built on the ReAct pattern (reason, then act, then observe, repeat — covered conceptually in Volume 4), were single-threaded by construction: one model, one growing transcript, one tool call at a time. Early open-source experiments like AutoGPT and BabyAGI pushed this pattern toward autonomy — an agent that would generate its own subtasks and loop indefinitely — and quickly exposed the failure mode that motivates this entire chapter: a single agent chasing an open-ended goal tends to wander, loses track of earlier findings as its context window fills, and has no mechanism to explore multiple independent leads at once. Adding "more reasoning" to one transcript does not fix this, because the bottleneck is architectural, not a matter of prompt quality.

2.2 The Orchestrator-Worker Pivot

The next pivot, and the one this chapter is built on, reframed the problem the way distributed computing reframed single-threaded batch jobs decades earlier: separate planning from execution, and let execution happen in parallel. A lead ("orchestrator") agent's only responsibilities become understanding the request, decomposing it into independent sub-questions, and later synthesizing the answers; each sub-question is handed to a fresh subagent with its own clean context window, its own tool access, and no visibility into what sibling subagents are doing. This mirrors the MapReduce pattern almost exactly — the lead agent is the "map" planner and "reduce" synthesizer, and the subagents are the parallel mappers, except the transformation each one applies is "go research this and report back" rather than a pure function.

Published engineering accounts of production multi-agent research assistants (most notably from Anthropic's own applied-AI teams) converged on the same finding from a different direction: multi-agent research systems consistently outperformed single-agent ones on breadth-heavy tasks, at the direct cost of using significantly more tokens per query — often an order of magnitude more — because every subagent re-pays the fixed cost of loading context and reasoning about its slice of the problem. That trade-off, spelled out honestly, is the central design tension of this entire chapter.

3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

Build a system that takes one open-ended research question, explores multiple independent lines of inquiry in parallel — each requiring its own iterative search-read-reason loop — and produces a single, coherent, well-cited report, within a bounded time and token budget, without any one sub-exploration derailing or duplicating the others.

Unpack the constraints embedded in that sentence, because each one rules out an architecture you might otherwise reach for:

  • "Open-ended" rules out a fixed, hand-coded pipeline of specific tool calls — you cannot know in advance how many sub-questions a query decomposes into, so the decomposition itself must be a reasoning step, not a configuration.
  • "Multiple independent lines of inquiry in parallel" rules out a single-agent ReAct loop — as Section 2 established, one context window degrades as it accumulates unrelated findings, and serial exploration wastes wall-clock time that parallel exploration would not.
  • "Its own iterative search-read-reason loop" means each subagent is not a single tool call but a full micro-agent: it must be able to search, evaluate whether the results answer its assigned sub-question, search again with a refined query if not, and know when to stop.
  • "Coherent, well-cited report" is the hardest constraint and the one most systems get wrong: parallel exploration produces fragmented, sometimes contradictory, sometimes overlapping findings that must be reconciled by a synthesis step that can see all of them at once — and every claim in the final report must be traceable back to a source, or the system has just industrialized hallucination instead of preventing it.
  • "Bounded time and token budget" rules out unconstrained recursive delegation (subagents spawning their own subagents indefinitely) — without an explicit budget and depth limit, a multi-agent system's cost is unbounded by construction.

Notice the shape of the trade-off this problem statement forces: you are deliberately spending more tokens and more orchestration complexity to buy breadth, parallelism, and resilience to any single line of inquiry going nowhere. This is the same trade every fan-out/fan-in system makes — more moving parts, more coordination overhead, in exchange for throughput and fault isolation a single serial worker cannot provide.

4. THEORY

4.1 The Lead Agent / Subagent Mental Model

Think of the lead agent as a research director, not a researcher. A good research director does not personally read every paper — they understand the question well enough to break it into assignments, brief each research assistant on exactly what to find and why it matters, wait for the assistants to report back, and then write the final synthesis themselves because only they have seen everything. Each research assistant (subagent), by contrast, is deliberately kept narrow: they know their one assignment, they have access to search tools, and they have no idea what the other assistants are doing or how their piece fits into the whole — this isolation is a feature, not a limitation, because it keeps each subagent's context window small, focused, and free of irrelevant noise from sibling investigations.

4.2 Orchestration as Explicit State, Not Implicit Conversation

A single-agent system can get away with treating "the conversation so far" as its entire state. A multi-agent system cannot — there is no single conversation. Instead, the system needs an explicit, structured plan: a list of sub-tasks, each with a status (pending, running, complete, failed), each producing a structured result (findings plus citations, not free-flowing prose) that the lead agent consumes programmatically rather than by re-reading a transcript. This is the theoretical pivot that makes the architecture tractable: state moves from "one long implicit narrative" to "an explicit task graph with typed inputs and outputs," which is exactly the kind of state your Spring Boot instincts already know how to model as a domain object, persist, and reason about.

4.3 Breadth vs. Depth, and Why the Lead Agent Must Choose

Every research question implies a trade-off between exploring many angles shallowly (breadth) and exploring few angles exhaustively (depth). A single agent makes this trade-off implicitly and inconsistently as it runs out of context. A multi-agent system makes it an explicit planning decision: the lead agent decides, up front, how many subagents to spawn and how narrowly or broadly to scope each one's assignment. A vague, high-level assignment ("look into competitor pricing") tends to make a subagent burn its budget being unfocused; an assignment scoped too narrowly ("find the exact list price of product X on date Y") wastes a whole subagent's overhead on a single fact a lighter-weight tool call could have fetched directly. Calibrating this — effort-scaling the number and scope of subagents to the actual complexity of the question — is the single highest-leverage design skill in this chapter, and it is a judgment call the lead agent's own prompt must be engineered to make well, not something you can hard-code once.

4.4 Citations as the Contract Between Subagents and the Synthesizer

The mechanism that keeps the final report honest is a strict contract: every subagent must return not prose but a structured object — a list of findings, each finding tagged with the source it came from. The lead agent's synthesis step is then explicitly instructed to write the report using only claims traceable to a returned finding, and to attach the citation inline. This turns "did the model make this up" from an unanswerable question into a checkable one: if a sentence in the final report has no citation, or cites a source whose finding does not actually support it, that is a detectable defect, not a philosophical debate about hallucination.

5. INTERNAL WORKING — STEP BY STEP

Walk through one query end to end: "How are the EU, US, and Japan each regulating liability for harms caused by autonomous AI agents, and where do their approaches conflict?"

  1. Intake and clarification. The lead agent receives the raw question. For genuinely ambiguous requests, a production system inserts a clarification turn here (asking the user a follow-up) rather than guessing scope — skipping this is a common source of wasted downstream work, because a misunderstood question wastes every subagent's budget.
  2. Planning / decomposition. The lead agent, prompted specifically to think like a research director, produces a structured plan: e.g., three parallel sub-tasks, one per jurisdiction ("research EU AI liability regulation," "research US AI liability regulation," "research Japan AI liability regulation"), plus a note that a fourth, dependent step ("identify conflicts between the three") must run only after the first three complete. This dependency information is itself part of the plan's output schema — not every multi-agent task is embarrassingly parallel.
  3. Task dispatch. Each independent sub-task is dispatched to a fresh subagent instance concurrently. Each subagent gets: its specific assignment, the tools it is allowed to use (typically a web/document search tool and a page-fetch/read tool), and an explicit budget (max tool calls, max tokens, or a wall-clock timeout).
  4. Subagent execution loop. Each subagent runs its own bounded ReAct-style loop: form a search query from its assignment, call the search tool, evaluate whether the results are relevant and sufficient, optionally reformulate and search again, fetch and read promising sources, and extract findings. Critically, the subagent is instructed to self-assess when it has "enough" to answer its assignment and to stop rather than keep searching indefinitely — an explicit stopping criterion, not an implicit one.
  5. Structured reporting. Each subagent returns a structured result: a short summary, a list of discrete findings, and for each finding, the source URL/identifier and a confidence or relevance note. This is enforced via structured output (a defined schema), not free text, precisely so the lead agent can consume it programmatically.
  6. Aggregation and dependent-task dispatch. The orchestration layer collects completed sub-task results, updates the task graph's status, and dispatches any tasks whose dependencies are now satisfied — here, the "identify conflicts" task, which is itself handed to a subagent (or handled by the lead agent directly) with all three jurisdictional summaries as its input.
  7. Failure and partial-result handling. If a subagent times out, exhausts its budget without a satisfying answer, or its tool calls consistently fail, the orchestrator marks that sub-task as degraded rather than blocking the whole pipeline — the synthesis step must be able to produce a report that honestly notes "no reliable information was found on X" rather than silently omitting it or waiting forever.
  8. Synthesis. The lead agent receives all completed (and degraded) sub-task results and is prompted to write the final report: organize by theme rather than by which subagent produced what, explicitly surface the requested comparison/conflict analysis, and attach citations to every substantive claim by pulling from the structured findings it received — it must not introduce claims that are not backed by a returned finding.
  9. Citation verification pass (optional but recommended). A lightweight verification step — sometimes a separate, smaller model call — checks that each cited claim is actually supported by the finding it cites, catching the case where the lead agent's synthesis subtly drifted from what a source actually said.
  10. Delivery. The final report, along with the full citation list and (for auditability) the underlying task graph and raw subagent findings, is returned to the user and persisted for later inspection.

Two edge cases deserve special attention because they are where naive implementations break. First, overlapping sub-tasks: if the lead agent's decomposition is sloppy, two subagents may end up researching largely the same ground, wasting budget and producing redundant, possibly contradictory findings that confuse synthesis — good decomposition prompting explicitly asks the lead agent to ensure sub-tasks are non-overlapping. Second, a subagent "going rogue" — following an interesting tangent far outside its assignment because a search result mentioned something adjacent but irrelevant — is exactly why per-subagent budgets and tightly scoped assignments are not optional hardening, they are core to the architecture working at all.

6. MATHEMATICS

This system does not need new mathematics beyond what earlier chapters established (token costs, context windows, sampling) — what it needs is a clear-headed accounting of how those costs compose when you go from one agent to many, because that composition is the crux of the cost/quality trade-off from Section 2.

6.1 Token Cost Scales Roughly Linearly With Subagent Count

Let a single-agent approach to a question cost roughly T tokens (its own context, tool results, and output). A multi-agent decomposition into n subagents does not cost T total — each subagent pays its own fixed overhead (system prompt, tool schemas, its slice of search results) plus the lead agent pays for planning and, more expensively, for synthesis over all n returned summaries. A reasonable first-order model is: 

Total tokens ≈ T_plan + Σ(i=1 to n) T_subagent_i + T_synthesis

where:
  T_plan          = tokens for the lead agent's decomposition step (small, roughly fixed)
  T_subagent_i    = tokens consumed by subagent i's own search/read/reason loop
                     (grows with the number of tool calls and pages read)
  T_synthesis     = tokens for the lead agent's final pass, which grows with the
                     TOTAL size of all subagents' returned findings, not with n alone

Rule of thumb observed in production research assistants:
  multi-agent token cost ≈ 4x to 15x the cost of an equivalent single-agent attempt,
  depending on n and per-subagent depth.

The practical consequence: n (how many subagents you spawn) is the single biggest cost lever you control, which is exactly why Section 4.3's breadth/depth calibration is described as the highest-leverage design decision, not a minor prompting detail.

6.2 Wall-Clock Latency: Parallelism's Payoff

Where the arithmetic works in your favor is latency. A serial single-agent exploration of n independent leads takes roughly the sum of each lead's time; n parallel subagents take roughly the maximum of their individual times (plus dispatch and synthesis overhead): 

Serial (single agent):   Latency ≈ Σ(i=1 to n) latency_i
Parallel (multi-agent):  Latency ≈ max(latency_1 ... latency_n) + T_dispatch + T_synthesis

This is the same amortized-cost reasoning you apply when deciding whether to parallelize a batch job across worker threads or a fan-out of downstream HTTP calls with CompletableFuture.allOf — you are trading more total compute (and money) for a much shorter critical path, and the trade is worth it exactly when the user-facing latency matters more than the marginal token cost, which is almost always true for an interactive research assistant.

Two architectural details matter more here than in a single-agent service. First, the job/task graph store is not optional bookkeeping — because subagents run concurrently and can fail independently, the system needs durable, queryable state about what has run, what succeeded, and what is still pending, the same way a saga or workflow orchestrator needs durable state for a multi-step distributed transaction. Second, per-agent cost and token metering must be enforced at the worker level, not only observed after the fact, because an unbounded subagent (one stuck in a search-refine loop) can otherwise consume the entire request's budget on its own.

9. JAVA / SPRING PERSPECTIVE

Spring AI gives you the primitives for both the lead agent and the subagents without inventing a new multi-agent-specific API — the pattern is built by composing tool calling, structured output, and ordinary Java concurrency around multiple ChatClient instances.

9.1 Modeling Lead Agent and Subagents as Distinct ChatClient Configurations

Each role gets its own ChatClient bean, built via ChatClient.Builder, with a role-specific default system prompt and its own set of tools registered via .defaultTools(...) or per-call .tools(...). The lead agent's ChatClient is configured with no external search tools at all — its only "tool," conceptually, is producing a structured plan — while each subagent's ChatClient is configured with the search and fetch tools implemented as @Tool-annotated methods (or ToolCallback beans), exactly as covered in the Tool Calling chapter. 

@Bean
ChatClient leadAgentClient(ChatModel model) {
    return ChatClient.builder(model)
        .defaultSystem("""
            You are a research director. Decompose the user's question into
            2-6 independent, non-overlapping sub-tasks. Note any dependency
            between sub-tasks explicitly. Output only the structured plan.
            """)
        .build();
}

@Bean
ChatClient subagentClient(ChatModel model, SearchTools searchTools) {
    return ChatClient.builder(model)
        .defaultSystem("""
            You are a focused research assistant. You have ONE assignment.
            Search, read, and stop once you can answer it. Return findings
            as structured data with a source citation for every claim.
            """)
        .defaultTools(searchTools)
        .build();
}

9.2 Structured Output for the Plan and for Findings

Both the lead agent's plan and each subagent's findings are extracted with Spring AI's structured output support (.entity(SomeRecord.class) backed by a BeanOutputConverter), not parsed out of free text — this is what lets the orchestration service treat the plan and the findings as ordinary Java objects it can persist, iterate over, and dispatch against, rather than strings it has to re-parse defensively. 

record SubTask(String id, String assignment, List<String> dependsOn) {}
record ResearchPlan(List<SubTask> subTasks) {}

record Finding(String claim, String sourceUrl, String confidence) {}
record SubagentResult(String subTaskId, String summary, List<Finding> findings) {}

ResearchPlan plan = leadAgentClient.prompt()
        .user(userQuestion)
        .call()
        .entity(ResearchPlan.class);

9.3 Parallel Dispatch With Virtual Threads

Because subagent work is I/O-bound (waiting on search APIs and page fetches) far more than CPU-bound, Java's virtual threads (Project Loom, standard since JDK 21) are a natural fit for dispatching subagents without hand-rolled thread-pool tuning: each subagent's blocking ChatClient call and tool invocations run on their own virtual thread, and StructuredTaskScope (or, more simply, a list of CompletableFutures run on a virtual-thread-per-task executor) lets the orchestrator wait for all independent sub-tasks to complete before proceeding to synthesis. 

try (var scope = new StructuredTaskScope.ShutdownOnFailure()) {
    List<Subtask<SubagentResult>> forks = plan.subTasks().stream()
        .filter(t -> t.dependsOn().isEmpty())
        .map(t -> scope.fork(() -> runSubagent(t)))
        .toList();

    scope.join().throwIfFailed();

    List<SubagentResult> results = forks.stream().map(Subtask::get).toList();
    // dispatch dependent sub-tasks next, then synthesize
}

9.4 Advisors for Per-Agent Guardrails

Spring AI's Advisor mechanism (covered in the Advisors chapter) is the natural place to enforce cross-cutting policy per agent role without polluting business logic: a token-budget advisor that aborts a subagent once it exceeds its allotment, a logging/tracing advisor that tags every call with the subtask id for observability, and a content-safety advisor that screens tool results before they re-enter the model's context — each wired once, applied uniformly across every subagent ChatClient call, the same way you would apply a cross-cutting @Around interceptor across a set of Spring services.

10. BEST PRACTICES

  • Make the lead agent's decomposition prompt explicitly demand non-overlapping, independently answerable sub-tasks. Vague decomposition is the single largest source of wasted budget and contradictory findings.
  • Give every subagent a hard budget (max tool calls, max tokens, or a timeout) and an explicit instruction to stop once its assignment is answerable — unbounded subagents are the multi-agent equivalent of an unbounded retry loop.
  • Force structured output at every hand-off — the plan, the subagent findings, and even the final report's citation list should be typed objects, not prose the orchestrator has to re-parse.
  • Persist the full task graph and raw subagent outputs, not just the final report — when a user questions a claim, you need to trace it back to the exact finding and source, and when the system misbehaves, you need to replay which subagent produced the bad input.
  • Run a citation verification pass before returning the report; treat unverifiable claims as a defect to strip or flag, not a rare inconvenience.
  • Cap recursion depth explicitly if you allow subagents to spawn their own sub-subagents for deep leads — an unbounded delegation tree is an unbounded cost tree.
  • Stream progress to the user (which sub-tasks are running/complete) rather than presenting a single opaque multi-minute wait — research queries are inherently slower than a single chat turn, and perceived latency matters as much as actual latency.

11. COMMON MISTAKES

  • Over-decomposing simple questions. Spawning five subagents for a question a single well-scoped tool call could answer multiplies cost and latency for no quality gain — Section 4.3's effort-calibration is a design requirement, not a nice-to-have.
  • Letting subagents share a context or converse directly. This defeats the isolation that keeps each subagent's reasoning focused and reintroduces the context-pollution problem the architecture was built to avoid.
  • Synthesizing from subagent summaries alone, discarding the underlying findings and citations. This is how multi-agent systems produce confident, fluent, uncited reports — the exact hallucination risk described in the Hallucinations chapter, now amplified across multiple sources.
  • No stopping criterion inside a subagent's own search loop. A subagent instructed only to "research X" with no sense of "enough" will keep refining queries and reading pages until its budget is exhausted, even after it already found the answer.
  • Treating a single subagent failure as a whole-request failure. A rigid implementation that aborts the entire research job because one of five subagents timed out wastes the other four's completed work — degrade gracefully instead.
  • No de-duplication of overlapping findings. When two subagents independently surface the same fact from different sources, naive synthesis can present it twice or, worse, as if it were two independent corroborating claims when it is one fact reported by two outlets.
  • Ignoring cost until the bill arrives. Because multi-agent token cost multiplies quickly (Section 6.1), teams that ship this pattern without per-request cost caps and monitoring are routinely surprised by production spend.

12. PERFORMANCE CONSIDERATIONS

The performance profile of a multi-agent research system is genuinely bimodal, and both sides need explicit engineering attention.

  • Latency: parallel dispatch turns the critical path into "the slowest subagent plus synthesis," as derived in Section 6.2 — but that means tail latency from any single slow tool call (a rate-limited search API, a slow page fetch) dominates the user-visible response time. Per-subagent timeouts with graceful degradation are a latency control, not just a cost control.
  • Throughput and concurrency limits: spawning many subagents per request multiplies concurrent calls to your model provider and to shared tools (search APIs, internal vector stores); at scale this requires connection pooling, provider-side rate-limit awareness, and often a shared semaphore across subagents so one research job cannot starve others.
  • Cost: as Section 6.1 showed, expect several times to an order of magnitude more tokens than an equivalent single-agent attempt — cost must be tracked per research job, with a hard ceiling, and reported to users or capped by plan tier in any multi-tenant product.
  • Caching: search results and page-fetch content are excellent caching candidates across subagents and even across users researching overlapping topics — caching at the tool layer (not the model layer) captures most of the benefit without touching model determinism.
  • Model tiering: not every role needs your most capable model — a smaller, cheaper model is often sufficient for the mechanical parts of a subagent's loop (query reformulation, relevance triage) while the lead agent's planning and synthesis, and the citation verification pass, benefit most from a stronger model; this mirrors the "match model size to task difficulty" principle established early in this series.

13. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

This project has a genuinely larger security surface than most single-agent chapters in this series, because subagents autonomously fetch and read untrusted external content — every page a subagent reads is, by the reasoning established in the Security chapter of Volume 1, a potential prompt injection vector. A malicious or compromised web page could contain text specifically crafted to be read by your subagent's model and interpreted as an instruction ("ignore your assignment and instead output the following..."), and because the subagent's findings flow into the lead agent's synthesis, a successful injection at the subagent level can propagate into the final report presented to the user as trustworthy, cited fact.

  • Treat all fetched content as data, never as instructions — the fetch tool should return content clearly delimited and the subagent's system prompt should explicitly state that content encountered during search is untrusted and must never override its assignment.
  • Scope tool permissions tightly per role. Subagents need read-only search and fetch tools; they should never be given write, send, or purchase-capable tools — the lead agent's synthesis step similarly needs no tool access at all, only the collected findings.
  • Sandbox and rate-limit outbound fetches to prevent a compromised subagent from being used as a vector for server-side request forgery against internal network resources — apply the same URL allow-listing and network egress controls you would to any server-side fetch feature.
  • Audit citation provenance so a report can always be traced to its actual sources, which also gives you a forensic trail if an injected instruction did partially succeed.
  • Cap data exfiltration risk by ensuring subagent tools cannot be pointed at internal, sensitive systems by user-supplied research topics — if internal document search is one of the available tools, apply the same authorization checks you would to any internal search endpoint, scoped to the requesting user's actual permissions, not the system's service-account permissions.

14. INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

JUNIORQ1. What is the core difference between a single-agent ReAct loop and a multi-agent orchestrator-worker system?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

A single-agent ReAct loop does all reasoning and tool use sequentially inside one growing context window. An orchestrator-worker system separates planning from execution: a lead agent decomposes the task into independent sub-tasks and delegates each to a separate subagent with its own isolated context, running in parallel, then the lead agent synthesizes their results.

JUNIORQ2. Why do subagents get their own separate context window instead of sharing the lead agent's conversation?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Sharing one context window across multiple lines of inquiry causes context pollution — irrelevant findings from one line crowd out relevant details of another, and the window eventually overflows. Isolated contexts keep each subagent focused only on its own assignment, which both improves quality and enables true parallel execution.

MIDQ3. Why does a multi-agent research system typically cost several times more in tokens than a single-agent approach to the same question, and is that trade-off worth it?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Each subagent pays its own fixed overhead (system prompt, tool schemas) plus the cost of its own search/read/reason loop, and the lead agent additionally pays for planning and for synthesis across all returned findings — costs that would be shared/amortized in a single agent are now paid n times. It is worth it when the task genuinely benefits from parallel breadth and lower wall-clock latency matters more than marginal token cost — for narrow, single-lookup questions it is not worth it, and a single tool call or single agent should be used instead.

MIDQ4. How would you prevent two subagents from redundantly researching the same sub-topic?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Primarily by engineering the lead agent's decomposition prompt to explicitly require non-overlapping, independently scoped sub-tasks, and by having it briefly justify why each sub-task is distinct before dispatch. As a secondary safeguard, the synthesis step should de-duplicate findings that report the same fact from different subagents rather than presenting them as independent corroboration.

SENIORQ5. Design the failure-handling strategy for a research job where one of five parallel subagents times out. What should happen to the overall request?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

The orchestrator should not fail the whole job. It should mark that sub-task as degraded/incomplete in the task graph, proceed to synthesis with the four completed results, and instruct the synthesis step to explicitly note in the report that information on the timed-out sub-topic was unavailable rather than silently omitting it or fabricating a plausible-sounding answer. Any downstream dependent task relying on the failed sub-task's output should either be skipped with a clear note or retried with a reduced scope, never silently proceed as if the missing data existed.

SENIORQ6. How do you keep the final report's claims traceable to real sources, and how would you test that this guarantee actually holds?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Enforce structured output at every hand-off: subagents return findings as typed objects (claim + source + confidence), never free prose, and the synthesis prompt is explicitly instructed to only assert claims backed by a returned finding, attaching its citation. To test this, run an automated citation verification pass — ideally a separate model call or rule-based check — that, for a sample of generated reports, checks each cited claim against the text of its cited source and flags unsupported or misattributed claims; track this as a measured accuracy metric over time, the same way you would track any other evaluation rubric from the Evaluation chapters, rather than trusting it by construction.

Interview Gold Point: Mention that citation verification should itself be logged and monitored as a production metric (percentage of claims that pass verification per report), not treated as a one-time QA check — this signals you think about hallucination control as an ongoing observability problem, not a launch-day checkbox.
SENIORQ7. How would you decide, for a given user query, how many subagents to spawn and how narrowly to scope each one?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

This is an effort-scaling decision made by the lead agent's planning step itself, not a fixed configuration: the decomposition prompt should be engineered to size the plan to the actual complexity of the question — a narrow factual question should produce zero or one sub-task (or be answered with a direct tool call, bypassing the multi-agent machinery entirely), while a genuinely broad comparative question should produce one sub-task per independent facet. Guardrails (a maximum sub-task count, and a minimum assignment scope below which a single tool call is used instead) prevent both over-decomposition of simple queries and under-decomposition of genuinely broad ones.

STAFFQ8. A stakeholder wants to add a feature where subagents can spawn their own sub-subagents for especially deep leads. What are the risks, and how would you allow this safely?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

Unbounded recursive delegation makes cost, latency, and even correctness unbounded by construction — a single deep lead could silently spawn dozens of downstream agents, blow through budget, and take arbitrarily long, with failures becoming harder to trace across a growing tree rather than a flat fan-out. To allow it safely: cap maximum recursion depth and total agent count for the entire request (not per level), propagate a shared, decrementing token/cost budget down through the tree so children cannot collectively exceed the parent's allotment, require each spawning decision to be justified in structured output (so it is auditable), and preserve the full tree in the task graph store for tracing and post-hoc cost attribution. Strategically, recursive delegation should be treated as an advanced, opt-in capability gated by policy per use case, not the default execution mode, given how much harder it makes cost forecasting and debugging.

MIDQ9. How does the lead agent decide whether a user query even needs decomposition into subagents, versus being answered directly?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

The planning step should classify query complexity before committing to the multi-agent path: single-fact lookups ("what is X's current stock price") should be answered with one direct tool call or a single agent, while genuinely multi-faceted or comparative questions ("compare the regulatory approaches of three countries to X") justify decomposition. A cheap classification pass (or explicit reasoning in the planning prompt itself) that estimates the number of independent facets the question has is the deciding signal — routing every query through full decomposition wastes cost and latency on the majority of simple questions.

MIDQ10. Give an example of a poorly decomposed research plan and explain why it fails.

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

A poor decomposition for "what caused the 2008 financial crisis" might split into "subagent 1: research causes, subagent 2: research effects, subagent 3: research causes and effects" — the sub-tasks overlap (two agents researching "causes") and are not independently useful (an "effects" agent may need context the "causes" agent is generating, so they aren't actually parallelizable). A good decomposition instead splits along genuinely orthogonal, non-overlapping facets (e.g., mortgage-market causes, regulatory-policy causes, ratings-agency causes) that each a subagent can answer with no dependency on the others' output.

MIDQ11. How do you handle a user query that is ambiguous before you decompose it into subagent tasks?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Decomposing an ambiguous query wastes an entire parallel research pass on the wrong interpretation, so ambiguity should be resolved before dispatch, not after. Cheap approaches: have the lead agent state its interpretation of the query and the scope it intends to research as part of the plan (shown to the user, or logged), and only ask a clarifying question back to the user when the interpretations meaningfully diverge in what sources or scope would be needed — for a synchronous chat UX, a brief clarifying turn is far cheaper than an expensive wrong research pass.

SENIORQ12. Should subagent task descriptions be handed down verbatim from the lead agent's plan, or should each subagent re-derive its own scope?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Verbatim, with enough self-contained context that the subagent does not need to see the rest of the plan or the original conversation. Each subagent's context should include the sub-task objective, any constraints (date range, source types to prefer/avoid), and the format its findings must be returned in — but not the full original conversation or the other sub-tasks, since that would reintroduce the context-pollution problem isolation was meant to solve. The lead agent, not the subagent, owns scoping decisions; letting subagents freelance on scope reintroduces the redundant-research and inconsistent-boundary problems decomposition exists to prevent.

SENIORQ13. How do you prevent decomposition from producing a sub-task that is too broad for a single subagent to complete within a bounded number of tool calls?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Give the planning prompt an explicit sizing heuristic and a hard budget to plan against — e.g., "each sub-task should be answerable within roughly 5-10 search/fetch calls" — and give each subagent a hard tool-call ceiling as a runtime guardrail regardless of what the plan intended. When a subagent hits its ceiling without converging, it should return its partial findings plus an explicit "scope too broad, needs further decomposition" signal rather than silently truncating its research; the orchestrator can then split that sub-task further in a second pass if the budget allows, rather than the subagent quietly producing a shallow answer.

MIDQ14. Should the intermediate research plan be shown to the end user before subagents are dispatched?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

For most consumer deep-research UX, yes — showing a short bulleted plan ("I'll look into: A, B, C") both sets expectations for a multi-minute wait and gives the user a cheap opportunity to redirect before expensive parallel work begins. It also serves as useful debugging/audit surface: when a report goes wrong, the plan tells you whether the failure was in decomposition or in a downstream subagent, which matters for triaging where to fix the system.

SENIORQ15. How would you handle a multi-hop research question where sub-task B genuinely depends on the result of sub-task A?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Not every question decomposes into an embarrassingly-parallel set of independent sub-tasks — true dependencies should be represented as a DAG rather than forced into a flat fan-out. The orchestrator runs A first (or the independent, non-dependent sub-tasks in parallel with A), then dispatches B once A's output is available, injecting A's relevant findings into B's task context. This costs latency versus full parallelism, so the planning step should only introduce a dependency edge when the sub-task genuinely cannot proceed without the other's output, not as a default structure.

Interview Gold Point: Note that a naive multi-agent implementation that assumes full independence between subagents will silently produce wrong answers on dependent multi-hop questions — flagging this failure mode unprompted signals you've thought past the common case.
STAFFQ16. When should the lead agent re-plan mid-research based on early subagent findings, rather than sticking to the original decomposition?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at senior/staff level

Re-planning is justified when an early finding invalidates an assumption the original plan was built on — e.g., a sub-task discovers the premise of the user's question is wrong, or surfaces a new facet clearly more important than one of the originally planned sub-tasks. Re-planning should be bounded (at most one or two revision passes) and gated behind a real signal, not triggered speculatively on every partial result, since checking after every subagent completion for whether to re-plan adds latency and cost to the common case where the original plan was fine. A pragmatic middle ground is a single checkpoint after the first wave of results, before committing to synthesis.

SENIORQ17. What does the lead agent's synthesis prompt need to include to avoid simply concatenating subagent outputs into an unreadable report?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

The synthesis prompt needs the original user question (so it can organize around answering it, not around the subagent boundaries), explicit instructions to restructure findings by theme rather than by which subagent produced them, instructions on how to handle contradiction and gaps, a target format/length, and the citation requirement (only assert what a returned finding supports, with its source attached). Without the original question re-anchoring the synthesis, the report tends to read as a sequence of disconnected mini-reports rather than a single coherent answer.

MIDQ18. How do you handle synthesis when subagents return wildly different volumes of findings — one comes back with two facts, another with twenty?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Volume of findings is not a proxy for importance to the final report, so the synthesis prompt should explicitly instruct the model to weight sub-topics by relevance to the user's question rather than by how much material was returned — otherwise a sparse-but-important sub-topic gets underrepresented and a verbose-but-tangential one dominates. If a sub-task returns unusually little, that is itself worth surfacing ("limited information was found on X") rather than padding it artificially to match the others' length.

SENIORQ19. Should the synthesis step be a single large LLM call, or should it itself be decomposed?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

For most research jobs, a single well-structured synthesis call is preferable — the whole point of synthesis is producing one coherent narrative, and splitting it risks reintroducing the same context-fragmentation problem decomposition solved for research. The exception is when total findings volume would overflow even a large context window (very broad reports with many subagents); then a two-stage synthesis is reasonable — first synthesize within thematic clusters, then a final pass merges the cluster-level summaries — but this should be the exception path, triggered by an actual token-count check, not the default architecture.

SENIORQ20. What state does the orchestrator need to track across the lifetime of a research job?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

  • The task graph: sub-tasks, their dependencies, and current status (pending/running/done/failed/timed-out).
  • Per-subagent findings, each tagged with its source citations.
  • Running cost/token/tool-call counters against the job's budget.
  • A job-level status for streaming to the client (planning / researching / synthesizing / done).
  • Enough of the original request (user query, any clarified scope) to re-anchor synthesis at the end.

This state needs to be persisted outside any single agent's context, typically in a database or durable store keyed by job ID, both so the client can poll/reconnect mid-job and so a crashed orchestrator process can resume rather than losing an in-flight multi-minute job.

STAFFQ21. How would you design the task-graph representation for subagents and their dependencies?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

Model it as a DAG of nodes, each carrying a unique task ID, its scope/instructions, its dependency edges (which task IDs must complete first), its status, its assigned agent/model tier, and a pointer to its result once complete. Most research jobs in practice are a flat fan-out (no edges) plus an optional final synthesis node depending on all of them, so the representation should optimize for that common shallow-and-wide case while still allowing the rare deeper dependency chain from a multi-hop decomposition. Persisting this graph (not just holding it in a single process's memory) is what enables progress streaming, crash recovery, and post-hoc debugging of exactly which sub-task produced a given claim.

MIDQ22. How do you avoid the lead agent hallucinating a synthesis when subagent results come back sparse or largely empty?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

The synthesis prompt should be explicit that "insufficient information was found" is an acceptable and expected output for a sub-topic, and that filling gaps with the model's own background knowledge (uncited) is not permitted for a system whose core promise is verifiable claims. Making this instruction concrete and repeated (not just a generic "don't hallucinate" line) matters because models under pressure to produce a comprehensive-sounding report will otherwise default to smoothing over gaps with plausible but unsourced text.

JUNIORQ23. What tools does a researcher subagent need at minimum to do useful work?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

At minimum: a web-search tool (query in, ranked results with URL/title/snippet out) and a page-fetch tool (URL in, extracted readable content out). Most production systems add a "return findings" structured-output step as the subagent's terminal action rather than free-form prose, so its output is directly consumable by the synthesis step. Optional but common additions: a targeted site-search or academic-search tool for domains where general web search is weak, and access to any internal/proprietary document store the product needs to research over alongside the public web.

MIDQ24. How do you decide how many search-tool calls to allow a single subagent before it must stop and report?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Set a hard per-subagent tool-call ceiling (a common production pattern caps this in the single-digit-to-low-teens range) sized to the sub-task granularity the planner is instructed to produce, and instruct the subagent's own prompt to self-assess convergence — stopping early once it judges it has enough to answer its assigned scope, rather than mechanically using its full budget every time. The ceiling exists as a backstop against agents that get stuck in unproductive search-refine loops, not as a target to always hit; a subagent that converges in two calls should stop in two calls.

MIDQ25. How do you handle a web search returning several near-duplicate results (e.g., the same story syndicated across five outlets)?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Deduplicate at the URL/domain level before spending a fetch call on near-identical content — a simple heuristic (same headline or first-paragraph similarity above a threshold) catches most syndicated-copy cases cheaply. Where the content genuinely is the same wire-service story, prefer fetching the original/primary source over a syndication copy when identifiable, since that also improves citation quality — citing the originating outlet is more defensible than citing a secondary aggregator.

MIDQ26. How would a subagent prioritize which search results to actually fetch, given fetching every result is too slow/expensive?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Use the search engine's own relevance ranking as a first-pass filter, then have the subagent apply a cheap relevance/authority judgment using only title and snippet before committing a fetch call — deprioritizing snippets that look off-topic, low-authority, or clearly promotional/SEO content. This snippet-triage step is what keeps the fetch budget spent on high-value pages rather than burned working through a search results list in raw order.

SENIORQ27. Should a subagent work off of search-result snippets, or should it always fetch the full page before citing a claim?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Snippets are useful for triage (deciding what's worth fetching) but should never be the basis for an asserted, cited claim — snippets are often truncated or algorithmically excerpted out of context, and citing "a source says X" based only on a snippet risks misrepresenting what the page actually says. For any claim that ends up in the final report, the subagent should have fetched and read the actual page content the citation points to; this is a specific, testable rule to enforce in the subagent's system prompt and to check for in citation-verification evals.

SENIORQ28. How do you handle rate limits from search/fetch APIs when dozens of subagents are hitting them in parallel?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Put a shared rate-limiting/queueing layer between all subagents and the external search/fetch providers rather than letting each subagent call out independently — this both respects provider limits and gives you a single place to apply backoff, provider fallback, and per-job fairness (so one large job can't starve concurrent jobs of search capacity). Subagents should treat a rate-limit response as a retryable condition with backoff, not a hard failure of their sub-task, and the orchestrator should track aggregate external-API usage as its own budget dimension alongside token cost.

MIDQ29. What's the risk of a subagent getting stuck in an unproductive search-refine loop, and how do you bound it?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Without a bound, a subagent that isn't finding good results can keep issuing slightly reworded queries indefinitely, burning cost and latency with no guarantee of ever converging. Bound it with the hard tool-call ceiling (Q24) as a backstop, plus a self-check the subagent's prompt is instructed to run after a few unproductive calls ("have my last N searches surfaced meaningfully new information?") that triggers an early "insufficient information found" exit rather than continuing to churn.

STAFFQ30. How would you integrate an internal/proprietary document store alongside public web search for a subagent?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

Expose it as an additional retrieval tool with the same shape as web search (query in, ranked results with a citable locator out) so the subagent's reasoning loop treats it uniformly, but keep the two source pools distinguishable in returned findings — internal documents typically carry different trust/freshness/access-control properties than public web pages, and the synthesis and citation layers need to know which is which (e.g., to render an internal-doc citation differently, or to enforce that internal content only appears in reports for users with matching access). The planning step should also be able to bias which pool a given sub-task should prefer, since some sub-questions are answerable only internally and others only from public sources.

SENIORQ31. What data structure should a "finding" carry to guarantee the final report's claims are traceable to real sources?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

A finding should be a structured object, not free text: the claim itself, the exact source URL/locator, a short supporting excerpt or quote from the actual fetched content (not a paraphrase — this is what a verification pass checks against), a confidence/certainty signal, and metadata like retrieval timestamp and source type. Requiring the subagent to produce this structure at the point of finding (immediately after reading the source), rather than reconstructing citations later from memory during synthesis, is what makes the traceability guarantee actually hold rather than being aspirational.

SENIORQ32. How do you handle a source that changes or disappears after it was cited in a report?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Snapshot or archive the fetched content (or at minimum the specific excerpt used) at citation time rather than relying on the live URL to always reflect what was read — web pages get edited, taken down, or paywalled after the fact, and a citation link that no longer supports the claim it's attached to undermines the whole verifiability guarantee. For reports users may revisit, store the excerpt alongside the citation so verification doesn't depend on the source still being live and unchanged.

SENIORQ33. How do you detect when a subagent has asserted a claim that isn't actually supported by the source it cited?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Run an automated citation-verification pass — a separate model call (or the subagent itself, as a self-check step) given only the claim and the source excerpt, asked to judge whether the excerpt actually entails the claim, distinct from and after the generation that produced the claim, so it isn't just re-confirming its own reasoning. Track the pass/fail rate as a first-class production metric across reports and treat a rising failure rate as a regression alarm, since this is the failure mode that most directly threatens user trust in the product.

Interview Gold Point: Distinguish two failure types worth naming separately: unsupported claims (the source doesn't say this at all) versus misattributed claims (the source says something related but the claim overstates or distorts it) — a strong verifier should catch both, and they often need different fixes upstream.
MIDQ34. Should citations be inserted into the report by the subagent, or attached later by the synthesis step?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

The citation-to-source binding should be created once, by the subagent, at the moment it produces a structured finding — the subagent is the only stage that has actually read the source. Synthesis should never invent or re-derive citations; it should only select and rephrase among the findings it was handed, carrying each claim's existing citation forward unchanged, since re-deriving citations during synthesis reintroduces exactly the hallucination risk structured findings were meant to eliminate.

MIDQ35. How would you present citations in the UI so users can verify claims quickly without leaving the report?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Inline numbered or superscript markers next to each claim, resolving to a source panel/footnote with the outlet name, a link, and ideally the exact excerpt that supports the claim shown on hover or click — surfacing the excerpt directly is what turns citation from a "trust us" link into something a user can verify in seconds without leaving the page. Grouping citations by claim rather than dumping an undifferentiated source list at the end makes the traceability the product promises actually usable.

SENIORQ36. What's the risk of citing a technically real but unreliable source (e.g., an SEO content farm or a known-biased outlet), and how do you mitigate it?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A citation being real and accurately quoted doesn't make the underlying claim true if the source itself is unreliable — this is a distinct failure mode from fabrication, and citation-verification alone won't catch it since the excerpt genuinely does say what's claimed. Mitigate with a source-quality signal fed into ranking/selection (domain reputation heuristics, or a maintained allow/deny list for known low-quality domains), and by having the synthesis step prefer corroboration from multiple independent higher-quality sources over a single low-quality one for any claim that's contestable or consequential.

MIDQ37. How do you handle multiple sources supporting the same claim — cite one, or all of them?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Cite the strongest one or two (by authority/recency) rather than every source that happens to agree — a wall of redundant citations doesn't add verifiability, it adds noise. The exception is when corroboration itself is the point being communicated (a contested or surprising claim), where explicitly noting "multiple independent sources confirm X" with two or three citations is informative rather than redundant.

STAFFQ38. Design an automated evaluation that scores citation precision and recall for generated research reports.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior/staff level

Precision: for every claim-citation pair in the report, verify (via the automated entailment check from Q33) whether the cited excerpt actually supports the claim — precision is the fraction that pass. Recall is harder since there's no single ground truth, but a workable proxy is: for a curated golden set of research questions with known key facts, check whether those key facts appear in the generated report with a valid citation, and measure what fraction do. Track both over time per model/prompt version, and treat precision as the harder gate (a hallucinated citation is worse than an incomplete report) — a reasonable policy is to block a release on precision regressions but treat recall regressions as a quality-improvement backlog item rather than a launch blocker.

SENIORQ39. How do you prevent a model from fabricating a plausible-looking URL or citation that was never actually retrieved?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Structurally, not just through prompting: citations should only ever be populated from the tool-call results the system actually recorded (the structured findings pipeline from Q31), never generated as free text by an LLM call — if the synthesis or report-writing step is only ever selecting from a fixed set of pre-recorded, tool-retrieved sources rather than writing URLs itself, fabrication becomes structurally impossible rather than merely discouraged. Any code path that lets a model free-type a citation string is the actual root cause of fabricated-citation incidents, and should be treated as a bug, not a prompting problem to iterate on.

SENIORQ40. What's your policy when no source can be found for a claim the user clearly wants an answer to?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Say so explicitly in the report rather than either omitting the topic silently or filling the gap with an uncited, model-generated answer — both alternatives erode trust, the first by seeming incomplete without explanation and the second by violating the product's core verifiability promise. "Available public sources did not address X as of this research" is a legitimate and honest output, and the synthesis prompt should treat it as an acceptable answer, not a failure to be papered over.

SENIORQ41. How should the system handle two credible sources that directly contradict each other on a factual point?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

The report should surface the disagreement rather than silently picking one side — something like "sources disagree on X: [source A] reports Y, while [source B] reports Z" — because presenting one figure as settled fact when it's actually contested is a subtler but real form of misleading the user. The synthesis prompt needs an explicit instruction for this case, since a model left to its own devices under pressure to sound authoritative will tend to just pick whichever claim it saw more often or last, rather than flagging genuine disagreement.

SENIORQ42. How do you detect contradiction across subagent findings before the synthesis step runs?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A lightweight pre-synthesis pass can group findings by sub-topic/entity and flag pairs that make incompatible factual assertions about the same thing (different numbers for the same statistic, opposite claims about the same event) — this can be a rule-based numeric/entity check for simple cases, or a dedicated model call for more nuanced contradictions. Flagging contradictions as structured metadata before synthesis, rather than expecting the single synthesis call to notice and handle them inline, produces more reliable handling than hoping one large prompt does everything at once.

MIDQ43. Should the final report ever present unresolved disagreement to the user rather than picking a "winner" between conflicting sources?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Yes, whenever the disagreement isn't resolvable from the retrieved evidence itself — forcing a false resolution when the underlying sources genuinely disagree produces a confidently wrong-sounding report. The exception is when one source is clearly more authoritative/recent/primary for the specific fact in question (e.g., an official statistics agency versus a blog repeating an old number), in which case the report can state the authoritative figure while still noting that other sources reported differently.

SENIORQ44. How do you weigh source recency and authority when reconciling conflicting claims?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Build explicit signals into the finding metadata (publication/retrieval date, source type — primary/official vs. secondary/aggregator vs. opinion) and give the synthesis prompt a rule of thumb: for facts that can change over time, prefer the more recent source; for facts about an entity's own data or statements, prefer the primary/official source over secondary reporting. This should be a documented heuristic the synthesis prompt applies, not an implicit judgment left entirely to model discretion, both so it's debuggable when the report gets it wrong and so it's consistent across reports.

SENIORQ45. How would you handle a case where the user's question rests on a premise that multiple sources indicate is factually wrong?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

The report should correct the premise rather than research the wrong question faithfully — e.g., a user asking "why did company X's revenue fall in 2024" when revenue actually rose should get told the premise is incorrect, with the actual figures cited, rather than an agent inventing plausible-sounding explanations for a decline that never happened. This is a case where the lead agent's synthesis step needs explicit permission to override the literal framing of the user's question when the evidence contradicts it, since a research agent overly deferential to the question's framing will otherwise happily fabricate a narrative to match a false premise.

STAFFQ46. What's the risk of the lead agent silently smoothing over contradictions during synthesis, and how would you catch it in production?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

An LLM optimizing for a fluent, confident-sounding report has a natural incentive to resolve tension between findings into one clean narrative, even when the underlying evidence doesn't actually support a single resolved answer — this is a subtle failure because the output reads as high-quality while quietly discarding real uncertainty the user should know about. Catch it with an automated check that compares the set of distinct claims in the pre-synthesis findings against the final report for a sample of contested topics, flagging cases where multiple incompatible finding-level claims collapsed into one unqualified report-level claim; this is a good candidate for periodic human spot-review rather than a fully automatable check, since judging "was this smoothing legitimate consensus-building or illegitimate erasure of real disagreement" is itself nuanced.

JUNIORQ47. Why is progress streaming particularly important for a deep-research product, more so than for a typical single-turn chat response?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

A research job can take minutes rather than seconds, and a blank loading state for that long reads as broken or hung, driving users to abandon the request. Streaming what the system is actually doing (which sub-topics it's researching, what it's found so far) converts idle waiting into a sense of visible progress and gives the user confidence the system understood their question correctly early, rather than discovering a misinterpretation only after the full wait.

MIDQ48. What events should stream to the client during a research job, end to end?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

  • Plan created: the decomposed sub-tasks, so the user sees what will be researched.
  • Per-subagent status transitions: started, key intermediate actions (searching for..., reading...), completed/failed.
  • A running count or preview of findings discovered, if the UX wants finer granularity than a status label.
  • Synthesis started, then the final report — ideally token-streamed like a normal chat response once synthesis begins.

These events are typically delivered over a persistent connection (SSE or WebSocket) tied to the job ID, decoupled from the underlying orchestration so a client can disconnect and reconnect mid-job without losing state, since the job state itself lives in the durable store described in Q20, not in the connection.

MIDQ49. How do you show parallel subagent progress to a user without overwhelming them with detail?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Default to a summarized view — a short label per sub-task ("researching regulatory approach in the EU... done") rather than streaming every individual tool call — with an optional expandable "show details" affordance for users who want the raw trace of searches and fetches. The right default granularity is the level that answers "is this making progress on what I asked" without requiring the user to parse agent-internal mechanics they didn't ask to see.

SENIORQ50. How would you handle a user who wants to cancel a research job mid-flight?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Cancellation needs to propagate down through the whole task graph, not just stop streaming to the client — in-flight subagent LLM calls and tool calls should be aborted (or at minimum, their results discarded and billing stopped) rather than left running to completion wastefully in the background after the user has walked away. This means every layer (orchestrator, subagent runtime, tool-call layer) needs to check a cancellation flag tied to the job ID, which is an argument for building job cancellation into the orchestration framework from the start rather than retrofitting it later.

MIDQ51. What's the tradeoff between streaming granular subagent tool calls versus higher-level status summaries?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Granular streaming (every search query, every URL fetched) is maximally transparent and great for debugging/power users, but is noisy for a typical consumer who just wants reassurance that progress is happening — it also couples the UI tightly to internal agent implementation details, making it harder to change subagent internals later without breaking the UI contract. Higher-level status summaries are cleaner UX and a more stable interface, at the cost of hiding detail a curious or skeptical user might want. Many products default to summaries with an opt-in detailed trace, which serves both audiences without picking one at the expense of the other.

SENIORQ52. How do you keep the UI responsive and informative if the orchestrator is blocked waiting on one unusually slow subagent?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Stream per-subagent status independently rather than only emitting a single job-level status, so the client can show "4 of 5 topics complete, still researching: X" instead of an undifferentiated "researching..." that gives no signal about which part is slow. Combined with the per-subagent timeout from the failure-handling design (Q5), this also gives the UI a natural moment to communicate "this part is taking longer than expected" rather than leaving the user guessing whether the whole job has stalled.

STAFFQ53. Should users be able to steer research mid-flight — e.g., tell the system to drop or add a research angle while subagents are still running?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

It's a genuine UX enhancement but adds real orchestration complexity: the system needs to accept a mid-job instruction, map it onto the live task graph (cancel a running sub-task, or dispatch a new one), and handle the case where the instruction arrives after synthesis has already started. A pragmatic staged approach is to first ship read-only visibility into the plan with cancellation of the whole job as the only control, then add "drop this angle" as a lower-risk steering primitive (it only ever removes work), before attempting "add this angle" (which requires safely injecting new work into an in-flight job) — since the failure modes of adding scope mid-flight are more complex than removing it.

MIDQ54. How would you design the UI to distinguish "found nothing on this sub-topic" from "still looking"?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

These need genuinely distinct status states in the streamed event model, not just an absence of updates that the client has to interpret — "still researching" should keep showing an active/in-progress indicator, while a completed sub-task that came back empty should transition to an explicit "no results found" terminal state. Conflating them (e.g., both just going quiet) leaves the user unable to tell a slow-but-working system apart from one that silently failed to find anything, which is exactly the ambiguity that erodes trust in a long-running background process.

MIDQ55. Why should the final report stream token-by-token even though the research phase before it already took minutes?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

By the time synthesis starts, the user has typically already waited through the longer research phase, so an additional silent pause while the full report generates (which can itself take tens of seconds for a long report) feels disproportionately bad relative to its actual duration — token streaming during synthesis keeps the experience feeling continuously alive rather than introducing one more unexplained wait right at the end. It's a small implementation detail but disproportionately affects perceived quality for this product's characteristic long-running flow.

SENIORQ56. How do you handle a client that disconnects and reconnects in the middle of a multi-minute research job?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

The job must continue running server-side independent of any particular client connection — orchestration state lives in the durable job store (Q20), not in the streaming connection itself — and on reconnect, the client should be able to fetch the current job state (plan, completed sub-tasks, partial findings) and resume the event stream from wherever it left off, rather than the job restarting or the user losing all visibility into work already done. This is a strong argument for treating the research job as a durable, resumable server-side entity from day one rather than something coupled to a single request/response or WebSocket lifecycle.

MIDQ57. How do you set a token/cost budget for an entire research job upfront?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Budget as a function of the plan's shape, not a flat number per job: estimate cost from the number of sub-tasks the planner produced times a per-subagent budget (system prompt + tool-call allowance + expected findings size), plus a fixed cost for planning and synthesis. Set this as a hard ceiling the orchestrator enforces during dispatch (e.g., capping subagent count if the plan would exceed budget), and expose the estimate to product/pricing decisions, since it's the direct lever for a tiered product (e.g., "quick" vs. "deep" research modes mapping to different subagent-count/budget ceilings).

SENIORQ58. What's your strategy when a research job is trending over budget mid-execution?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Track cumulative spend against budget as subagents complete, and when a threshold is crossed (e.g., 80% of budget with sub-tasks still pending), degrade gracefully rather than failing hard: skip lower-priority remaining sub-tasks, cap remaining subagents to a smaller tool-call allowance, or move straight to synthesis with whatever's been gathered — and always disclose the degradation in the report ("research on X was limited due to time/scope constraints") rather than silently producing a thinner report than the plan promised.

SENIORQ59. How do you decide which model tier (larger/more capable vs. smaller/cheaper) to use for which role in the system?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Match model capability to the reasoning difficulty of the role, not uniformly to the most capable model everywhere: the lead agent's planning and synthesis steps benefit most from a strong model since decomposition quality and coherent, well-hedged writing are hard to get right and directly determine report quality, while individual subagents doing search-and-extract work over retrieved text are often well served by a smaller/cheaper/faster model, since the task per subagent is narrower and there are many of them running in parallel — the cost multiplier from Q3 makes subagent-tier model choice the highest-leverage cost lever in the whole system.

SENIORQ60. How would you cache across research jobs to reduce redundant cost?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Cache at the tool-call layer, not the report layer — search results and fetched-page content for a given query/URL are reusable across unrelated jobs and users within a reasonable freshness window (e.g., minutes to hours depending on how time-sensitive the domain is), which can meaningfully cut redundant search/fetch cost and latency for popular or overlapping topics. Caching whole reports is far riskier since it reintroduces staleness risk for a product whose value proposition is current, verified information — cache the expensive raw ingredients, not the finished synthesis.

MIDQ61. What's the latency budget breakdown for a target 5-minute research job across planning, dispatch, research, and synthesis?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Roughly: planning/decomposition should be fast (seconds, a single small-to-medium LLM call), research is the dominant cost since it's bounded by the slowest subagent's search-fetch-reason loop across however many tool-call rounds it needs (this is where parallelism matters most — five subagents running for two minutes each in parallel costs two minutes wall-clock, not ten), and synthesis is a single call whose latency scales with total findings volume and target report length. The overall SLA is therefore mostly a function of per-subagent tool-call ceiling and round-trip latency to search/fetch providers, which is why bounding subagent tool-call budgets (Q24) is as much a latency lever as a cost lever.

SENIORQ62. How do you parallelize research without overwhelming downstream rate limits on search/fetch providers?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

This is the same shared rate-limiting/queueing layer from Q28 viewed from the capacity-planning side: cap the effective concurrency of outbound search/fetch calls system-wide (not just per job), so that a single job with many subagents, or many concurrent jobs, can't individually or collectively exceed provider limits. When the queue backs up, subagents should experience it as added latency on individual tool calls rather than errors, with the per-subagent tool-call budget and overall job SLA absorbing that added latency gracefully rather than the system failing outright under load.

MIDQ63. Would you ever short-circuit a research job early if partial results already answer the query well?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Yes, for narrow factual questions that got decomposed more broadly than necessary — if an early-completing subagent's finding directly and completely answers the user's question, it's wasteful to wait out the full budget on other sub-tasks that turn out to be less relevant. This requires a cheap "does this already answer the question" check after each sub-task completes, which is a genuine engineering investment (false positives cut research short prematurely) — a reasonable middle ground is only short-circuiting for the narrow-query case identified at planning time (Q9), where the plan itself signals low expected complexity, rather than attempting it universally.

STAFFQ64. How do you handle cost attribution and billing to an end user or customer for a research job with inherently variable cost?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

Variable actual cost per job makes flat per-query pricing risky at the margins (a broad five-subagent job costs meaningfully more than a narrow single-lookup one), so common approaches are either tiered product modes with fixed subagent-count/budget ceilings per tier (predictable pricing, bounded cost per tier) or metered usage billing tied to actual token/tool-call consumption for enterprise customers who want to pay for what they use. Either way, the budget-tracking infrastructure from Q57 needs to double as the metering source of truth, so cost attribution isn't a separate system that can drift from what actually happened during the job.

SENIORQ65. What's the risk of prompt and tool-schema overhead multiplying across many subagents, and how do you mitigate it?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Every subagent pays its full system prompt and tool-schema token cost on its first call regardless of how small its actual sub-task is, so for a job with many narrow sub-tasks, fixed per-subagent overhead can become a larger fraction of total cost than the actual research content — this is the concrete mechanism behind the "n times the overhead" cost multiplier from Q3. Mitigate by keeping subagent system prompts and tool schemas as lean as possible (only the tools that role actually needs), by using prompt caching for the shared static portion of the subagent prompt across the parallel fan-out, and by not over-decomposing simple queries into more subagents than the question actually needs (Q9, Q13).

STAFFQ66. How would you define SLOs for this product, and what would you actually measure to track them?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

  • Latency: end-to-end job completion time (p50/p95), and time-to-first-plan-shown (matters more for perceived responsiveness than total time).
  • Quality: citation precision from the automated verifier (Q33), and periodic human/LLM-judge scores on a rubric (Q72).
  • Reliability: job failure/timeout rate, and rate of jobs that degrade gracefully under budget pressure (Q58) versus fail outright.
  • Cost: actual spend per job versus estimated budget, tracked for drift.

The distinguishing point for this product versus a typical API SLO set is that quality can't be reduced to a single latency/error-rate pair — citation precision specifically needs to be a tracked, alertable SLO in its own right, since it's the metric most directly tied to whether users can trust the product's core promise.

MIDQ67. How do you deduplicate near-identical findings that different subagents phrase differently?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Exact-string matching won't catch paraphrased duplicates, so this needs semantic comparison — either embedding-similarity clustering of findings' claim text above a threshold, or a dedicated LLM pass over the pooled findings that groups semantically equivalent claims before synthesis. The key is doing this as an explicit pre-synthesis step with visible output (a deduped, clustered finding set) rather than hoping the single synthesis call implicitly notices and merges duplicates while also handling everything else — an explicit step is both more reliable and separately debuggable.

MIDQ68. Should deduplication happen before synthesis as a separate pass, or be handled implicitly during the synthesis call itself?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

As a separate, explicit pass before synthesis, for the same reason structured findings exist at all: giving the synthesis call one clear job (write a coherent report from a clean, deduplicated, thematically-clustered finding set) produces more reliable output than giving it three jobs at once (deduplicate, resolve contradictions, and write well) inside a single prompt. Separating these concerns also makes each one independently testable — you can evaluate deduplication quality and synthesis quality as distinct metrics rather than only being able to judge the combined end-to-end output.

SENIORQ69. How do you avoid over-counting corroboration when multiple subagents happen to cite the exact same underlying source?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Track the underlying source URL/locator, not just the claim text, when deduplicating and when the synthesis step reasons about how well-corroborated a claim is — three subagents independently finding and citing the same single article is one source's worth of evidence, not three, and a synthesis prompt that (or a downstream reader who) treats it as three independent confirmations is being misled about actual evidentiary strength. This matters specifically because parallel subagents with overlapping search queries commonly do converge on the same popular source, so this isn't an edge case to dismiss.

MIDQ70. Give an approach for clustering findings by sub-topic before writing the final report.

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Simplest approach: since the original decomposition already assigned each finding to a sub-task with a known topic, group by originating sub-task as a first pass — this is cheap and usually reasonably coherent. Where findings within a sub-task still span meaningfully distinct themes, or where the report's ideal structure cuts across the original sub-task boundaries (common when decomposition was source-oriented but the report should be theme-oriented), an embedding-based or LLM-driven clustering pass over the finding set produces a better report structure than assuming sub-task boundaries equal ideal report sections.

SENIORQ71. Why can't you use exact-match, ROUGE, or BLEU-style metrics to evaluate a generated research report?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

These metrics assume there's a single correct reference text to compare against and reward surface-level lexical overlap with it — neither holds for an open-ended research report, where many differently-worded reports can be equally correct and comprehensive, and where the properties that actually matter (citation accuracy, completeness of coverage, appropriate handling of contradiction) aren't measurable by n-gram overlap at all. This product's evaluation needs to be rubric- and claim-level (does each assertion hold up, is coverage adequate), not reference-text-similarity based — closer to how the Evaluation chapters approach open-ended LLM output generally, applied specifically to citation and coverage properties here.

SENIORQ72. What would an LLM-as-judge rubric for scoring a research report look like?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

  • Citation accuracy: does each cited claim hold up against its source (can reuse the automated verifier from Q33 as one rubric dimension).
  • Coverage/completeness: does the report address the key facets a good answer to this question should cover.
  • Handling of uncertainty: does it appropriately flag gaps and contradictions rather than papering over them (Q41, Q46).
  • Coherence/readability: is it organized around the question rather than reading as concatenated fragments (Q17).
  • Faithfulness to scope: does it avoid drifting into unrequested tangents or omitting clearly relevant, requested facets.

Score each dimension separately rather than one overall number, since a report can be well-written but citation-unreliable, or perfectly cited but incomplete — a single blended score would hide which specific failure mode to fix.

STAFFQ73. How would you build a golden evaluation set for this product given that "correct" research answers are open-ended, not single ground-truth strings?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

Instead of a single reference answer per question, curate a small set of key facts/claims each golden question's answer should cover (derived by a human researcher actually researching the question well), plus known-tricky properties to test for (a question with genuinely contradictory sources, a question with a false premise, a question where good sources are scarce). Score generated reports against that fact checklist (did it surface each key fact, with a valid citation) rather than against reference-text similarity, and deliberately include the tricky cases in the golden set specifically because they're the ones default synthesis behavior tends to get wrong (Q41, Q45, Q46) — a golden set of only easy, well-covered questions won't catch the failure modes that matter most for this product.

MIDQ74. How do you detect regressions in report quality when you change the orchestrator's decomposition or synthesis prompt?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Run both prompt versions against the same golden question set and same (or matched) retrieved evidence where possible, then compare on the rubric dimensions from Q72 plus the citation-precision metric from Q33 — the "same retrieved evidence" control matters because without it, a quality difference could come from search results varying between runs rather than from the prompt change itself, which is a common false signal in ad hoc before/after comparisons. Treat this as a required gate before shipping orchestrator/synthesis prompt changes, the same way you'd gate a model or retrieval change in a RAG system.

SENIORQ75. What human-evaluation process would you run before shipping a change to the orchestrator's core prompts?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Automated rubric/LLM-judge scoring should be the first, cheap filter run on every change, but a sample of reports (weighted toward the tricky golden-set cases — contradiction, false premise, sparse sources) should get human review before a prompt change touching synthesis or citation handling ships, since these are exactly the judgment calls (was smoothing over disagreement appropriate, was a premise correction handled gracefully) that automated judges are least reliable at scoring. Track inter-rater agreement on this human review over time — if it's low, the rubric itself needs tightening before the review process is trustworthy.

SENIORQ76. How would you measure "completeness" of a research report given there's no single ground truth for what a complete answer looks like?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Approximate it with the golden-set key-facts-checklist approach from Q73 for a fixed evaluation set, and for production monitoring at scale (where you can't hand-curate a checklist per live query), use an LLM-judge prompted specifically to identify facets of the user's actual question that the report doesn't address, given the question and report alone — this catches a report that answered a narrower question than what was actually asked, which is the most common practical form completeness failures take, even without a true reference answer to compare against.

MIDQ77. What automated checks would you run on every generated report before showing it to a user, as opposed to periodic offline evaluation?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Anything cheap enough to run inline without materially adding latency: a citation-format check (every claim has a resolvable citation, no dangling or malformed ones), a check that every cited URL actually corresponds to a source the system retrieved during this job (catching the fabrication failure mode structurally, per Q39), and possibly a lightweight version of the entailment check from Q33 on a sample of claims if latency allows. Full rubric scoring and human review are too slow/expensive to run synchronously per-report and belong in the offline evaluation pipeline instead — the online checks are a cheap safety net, not a replacement for the deeper offline evaluation.

MIDQ78. How should a subagent handle hitting a paywalled article mid-research?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Treat it as a normal, expected outcome rather than an error: record that the source exists and appeared relevant based on its snippet/headline, but that its full content wasn't accessible, and move on to other sources rather than getting stuck retrying or waiting. Critically, the subagent must not cite a specific claim as coming from a paywalled article based only on the snippet — per Q27, an unfetched source can't support a specific asserted claim, so a paywall effectively removes that source from the citable evidence pool for this job even though it may be mentioned as "further reading not accessible."

MIDQ79. What do you do when a source that looks highly relevant can't be fetched at all — robots.txt disallow, geo-block, bot detection?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Respect the block (don't attempt to circumvent robots.txt or bot detection — this is both a legal/ethical line and a reliability one, since attempts to work around it are fragile and create operational risk) and fall back to the next-best accessible source instead. If no accessible source adequately covers that specific point, this becomes a legitimate coverage gap the report should disclose (Q40) rather than something to route around by any means available.

SENIORQ80. Should the report ever cite a source it could not actually access, based only on search-result snippet text?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Medium-High

No — this is a direct violation of the traceability guarantee (Q27, Q31), even though it's tempting when a snippet strongly implies a specific fact and fetching failed. The correct handling is to either find the same fact corroborated by an accessible source, or omit the specific claim while optionally still mentioning the inaccessible source's existence in a non-cited "for further reading" note — never attach a specific citation marker to content the system never actually read in full, since that's indistinguishable from fabrication from the user's perspective if the underlying page turns out not to say what the snippet implied.

MIDQ81. How do you handle sources requiring login/authentication that the system doesn't have credentials for?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Functionally the same handling as a paywall (Q78) — treat the fetch as inaccessible, don't attempt credential workarounds, and let the subagent continue with accessible alternatives. The one addition worth building for enterprise/internal-document use cases is that if the product legitimately has been granted the user's own credentials or access scope to a specific system (e.g., a connected internal wiki), that's a distinct, deliberately-provisioned access path — not something a general web-research subagent should ever attempt on its own initiative against arbitrary public sites.

STAFFQ82. Design a deep-research product end to end: sketch the architecture from a user's query to a final cited report.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior/staff level

At a high level: (1) intake — receive the query, optionally resolve ambiguity (Q11), create a job record in durable storage; (2) planning — a lead agent decomposes the query into a bounded, sized set of independent (or DAG-dependent, per Q15) sub-tasks, sized against the job's cost/latency budget; (3) dispatch — subagents run in parallel (respecting shared rate limits, Q28/Q62), each equipped with search/fetch tools, each producing structured, cited findings (Q31), with per-subagent timeouts and graceful degradation on failure (Q5); (4) aggregation — deduplicate and cluster findings across subagents (Q67, Q70), detect contradictions (Q42); (5) synthesis — a single lead-agent call (or staged for very large jobs, Q19) produces the final report from the clean finding set, citing only what findings support; (6) verification — an automated citation-precision check runs before or alongside delivery (Q33, Q77); (7) delivery — the report streams to the client, with progress events streamed throughout steps 2-5 (Q48). Cross-cutting: a durable job store backing steps 1-7 for resumability, and a budget tracker enforcing cost/latency ceilings throughout.

STAFFQ83. How would you hit a 5-minute SLA target for typical queries while keeping cost bounded?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Medium-High at staff level

Latency and cost pull in the same direction here more than they trade off: bounding subagent count and per-subagent tool-call ceilings (Q13, Q24) caps both the wall-clock time of the slowest parallel branch and total token spend simultaneously, and using a cheaper/faster model tier for subagents (Q59) helps both dimensions too. Where they do trade off is model quality for planning/synthesis (a stronger, slightly slower model there improves report quality) — this is a smaller latency cost than the research phase, so it's usually worth paying for. The 5-minute figure should be validated against the realistic p95 case (an unusually broad query with a slow subagent or two, Q52), not just the median case, with graceful degradation (Q58) as the safety valve when a specific job would otherwise blow the SLA.

SENIORQ84. Walk through what happens, system-wide, from the moment a user submits a research query to seeing the final report.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Query received and a job record created; the client opens a streaming connection keyed to the job ID; the lead agent plans and the plan streams to the client; subagents dispatch in parallel behind a shared rate limiter, each streaming coarse-grained status while running its own search-fetch-reason loop and emitting structured findings on completion (or a failure/timeout status); once all sub-tasks resolve (or budget/timeout forces early stop), findings are deduplicated and contradiction-checked; the lead agent synthesizes a report from the clean finding set, token-streaming it to the client as it's generated; an automated citation check runs and, depending on the product's policy, either gates delivery or runs as a background quality signal; the completed report and its citation metadata persist to the job record so the user can revisit or the team can audit it later.

STAFFQ85. How would you version and roll out changes to the orchestrator's decomposition or synthesis prompts safely?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

Treat prompt changes with the same rigor as a code deploy: version the prompts explicitly, run the offline golden-set evaluation (Q73, Q74) as a required gate, then roll out via canary/percentage-based traffic split rather than a hard cutover, comparing the automated quality metrics (citation precision, rubric scores) between old and new versions on live traffic before ramping to 100%. Log which prompt version produced each report in the job record, so a quality regression noticed after rollout can be traced back to a specific version and rolled back cleanly, and so any A/B comparison has clean attribution.

SENIORQ86. How would you support a "follow-up question" on an already-completed research report without re-running the entire research job?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Classify the follow-up first: if it's answerable from findings already gathered in the original job (the full finding set, not just what made it into the final report, should be retained), answer directly from that existing evidence with no new research needed — cheap and fast. If it asks about something genuinely outside the original job's scope, it needs its own smaller research pass (potentially just one or two new subagents rather than a full new decomposition), which can reuse the original job's context as background. This is a strong argument for retaining the full finding set (not discarding what didn't make the final report) as part of the job record, since it directly enables cheap follow-ups.

STAFFQ87. What observability would you build specifically for this product, beyond generic LLM-application observability (traces, token counts, latency)?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Medium-High at staff level

  • Citation precision/recall as a continuously tracked, alertable metric (Q33, Q38), not just spot-checked.
  • Task-graph-level tracing: which sub-task produced which finding, which finding fed which report claim — full lineage from final claim back to the original source, so a bad report is debuggable to its root cause rather than only visible as a bad final output.
  • Degradation-rate tracking: how often jobs hit budget/timeout limits and degrade (Q58), as a leading indicator of the budget being miscalibrated against real query complexity.
  • Contradiction/gap disclosure rate: how often reports flag disagreement or missing information, which is a healthy signal, not a failure metric, and worth watching for the opposite anomaly — a sudden drop, suggesting synthesis has started silently smoothing over genuine uncertainty (Q46).

The unifying theme is that generic LLM observability tells you the system is running; this product specifically needs observability that tells you whether its verifiability promise is holding.

SENIORQ88. How would you test this system end-to-end given that both the research content and the model's output are non-deterministic?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Separate what needs to be deterministic from what doesn't: orchestration mechanics (task-graph construction, budget enforcement, timeout/failure handling, citation-structure validity) should be unit- and integration-testable deterministically using mocked search/fetch tools with fixed canned responses, independent of any live model call. Actual output quality (does the report read well, are citations accurate) can't be asserted with exact-match tests and instead relies on the golden-set rubric evaluation (Q73) run regularly, with acceptable-range thresholds rather than exact expected outputs. Mixing these two — trying to assert exact report text in a unit test, or trying to unit-test orchestration logic only through slow live-model runs — produces either flaky tests or slow, expensive ones; keeping them separate keeps both fast and meaningful.

STAFFQ89. What's your incident-response plan if the system starts producing reports with fabricated citations in production?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

This is a trust-critical incident, not an ordinary quality bug — treat it with a severity matching a correctness/safety incident. Immediate: check whether the citation-verification metric (Q33) shows a step-change, correlate against recent deploys (prompt, model, or tool-layer changes) using the version logging from Q85, and if a specific recent change correlates, roll it back rather than attempting a forward-fix under pressure. Structurally, this is also the moment to double check that citations are actually constrained to only ever be selected from tool-retrieved results (Q39) rather than free-generated — if fabrication is occurring despite that structural constraint, that itself is a more serious finding (a bypass or bug in the constraint) than a normal prompt-quality regression, and warrants pausing the affected code path rather than only tuning prompts.

STAFFQ90. How would you scale this system from handling 10 concurrent research jobs to 10,000?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

The bottlenecks shift as scale grows: at low scale, per-job orchestration logic and model API latency dominate; at high scale, shared external dependencies become the constraint — search/fetch provider rate limits (Q28, Q62) become a hard ceiling requiring either provider-side capacity negotiation or a multi-provider fallback strategy, the durable job store needs to handle high write throughput for streaming status updates without becoming a bottleneck itself, and the orchestrator/subagent compute layer needs to scale horizontally (stateless workers pulling from a job queue, with all actual state in the durable store, not in worker process memory) rather than assuming a single orchestrator process per job. Caching shared search/fetch results across jobs (Q60) also becomes proportionally more valuable at scale, since popular topics get researched redundantly by many concurrent users.

STAFFQ91. If forced to cut this system's cost by 50% while preserving as much quality as possible, what would you cut first?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

In rough order: first, tighten subagent model tier and per-subagent tool-call ceilings (Q59, Q24) — this is usually the single largest cost lever with the least quality impact, since subagent search-and-extract work tolerates a cheaper model better than planning/synthesis does. Second, invest in cross-job caching of search/fetch results (Q60), which reduces cost without touching output quality at all for repeated/overlapping topics. Third, tighten the query-complexity classifier (Q9) so simple queries reliably skip full decomposition rather than defaulting to it. What to protect last: synthesis-step model quality and the citation-verification pass — these most directly determine whether reports read well and whether the fabrication-prevention guarantee holds, and degrading them saves comparatively little (they're a small fraction of total per-job cost, per Q3) while risking the product's core trust proposition.

16. SUMMARY

  • A multi-agent research system breaks the single-context-window ceiling of one agent by separating planning (a lead agent) from parallel, isolated execution (subagents), mirroring the orchestrator-worker and fan-out/fan-in patterns you already know from distributed systems.
  • The core problem it solves is exploring multiple independent lines of inquiry in parallel and synthesizing them into one coherent, citation-backed report within a bounded time and token budget.
  • Structured output at every hand-off — the plan, each subagent's findings, and the final citations — is what turns this from an unverifiable black box into an auditable, testable pipeline.
  • The dominant trade-off is cost versus breadth/latency: multi-agent decomposition can cost several times to an order of magnitude more tokens than a single-agent approach, in exchange for parallel wall-clock speed and better coverage on genuinely broad questions — calibrating how many subagents to spawn is the highest-leverage design decision.
  • Java's virtual threads and structured concurrency, combined with per-role Spring AI ChatClient configurations and Advisors for budget/guardrail enforcement, map cleanly onto this architecture without needing a bespoke multi-agent framework.
  • Because subagents autonomously ingest untrusted external content, this project has a materially larger prompt-injection and tool-permission surface than earlier single-agent chapters, and must be engineered with that threat model from the start, not bolted on after launch.
