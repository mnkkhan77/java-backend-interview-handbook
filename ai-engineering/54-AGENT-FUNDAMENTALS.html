CHAPTER 54 — AGENT FUNDAMENTALS

Volume 4 — AI Agents & MCP · Chapter 1 of 10

Evaluation (Chapter 53) gave you a way to measure whether a single model response is good enough to ship. But every evaluation technique in that chapter quietly assumed a system that produces one answer to one prompt and stops. The moment a task requires the model to look something up, run a calculation, call an API, check its own work, and only then answer — a single request/response pair can no longer represent what the system did. This chapter introduces the structure that makes multi-step, tool-using, self-directed behavior possible at all: the agent. Everything in the rest of this volume — planning, tool design, multi-agent orchestration, MCP — is built on the loop you are about to learn here.

1. MOTIVATION

Up to this point in the series, every interaction with a model has had the same shape: you build a prompt (possibly enriched with retrieved context, as in Volume 3), send it once, and receive one response. Even the most sophisticated ChatClient call with an Advisor chain and a memory-augmented prompt is still, mechanically, a single request answered by a single response. This shape is enormously useful, and it will remain the majority of what you build. But it has a hard ceiling: it cannot solve any task whose correct execution depends on information the model does not yet have at the moment you send the prompt.

Consider a deceptively simple request: "Check whether the customer with email jane@example.com has any open support tickets, and if the account is on the Enterprise plan, escalate the highest-priority one to the on-call engineer." No amount of clever prompting solves this in one shot, because the correct action depends on two facts — the account's plan, and whether tickets exist — that live in your systems, not in the model's training data or your prompt. A single-shot call can only ever produce a plausible-sounding guess at those facts, which is precisely the hallucination failure mode from earlier in this series, now with real operational consequences: escalating (or failing to escalate) an actual customer issue based on a guess.

The only way to close this gap is to give the model a way to act — to look something up, observe the result, and decide what to do next based on what it found — rather than to only speak. A system built this way is what this chapter calls an agent: a loop, not a call. This is not a cosmetic change to your architecture. It changes what "the request" even means (it now spans many model invocations), what "correct" means (a sequence of actions can be correct even if any single step alone looks strange), and what can go wrong (a bad decision at step 3 of 10 can compound, whereas a single-shot call fails once and is done). If you are moving from "I can call a model" to "I can build systems that get real work done," this loop is the single most consequential structure you will learn in this entire series — every later chapter in this volume is a refinement of it.

2. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

The word "agent" did not arrive with large language models — it is one of the oldest ideas in AI, and tracing its lineage explains why today's LLM agents look the way they do, and why certain design choices (a "scratchpad," an "observation," a "final answer" marker) exist at all.

2.1 Classical AI Agents (1980s–2000s)

Long before LLMs, AI textbooks already defined an agent formally: something that perceives its environment through sensors and acts upon that environment through actuators, in a continuous loop, in pursuit of a goal. This definition came out of robotics and classical planning research — a thermostat, a chess-playing program, and a warehouse robot are all "agents" in this sense, of wildly different sophistication. Classical planning systems (STRIPS and its descendants) formalized this further: given a starting state, a goal state, and a set of allowed actions with known preconditions and effects, search for a sequence of actions that provably reaches the goal. This is powerful and exact — but it required the entire world to be described in a formal, symbolic representation ahead of time, which runs straight into the knowledge acquisition bottleneck covered in the "What Is Intelligence" chapter. It worked beautifully for a robot arm on a fixed assembly line; it could not plan "handle this customer's ambiguous email," because natural language and open-ended real-world state cannot be exhaustively formalized.

2.2 Reinforcement Learning Agents (2000s–2010s)

The reinforcement learning (RL) tradition kept the perceive-act loop but replaced hand-authored plans with learned policies: an agent observes a state, takes an action, receives a reward signal, and gradually learns — through repeated trial and error — a policy that tends to choose good actions. This produced dramatic results in narrow, well-defined environments (Atari games, Go, robotic control) where the state space, action space, and reward function could all be precisely specified. It struggled badly outside those environments, because defining a reward function for something like "be a helpful customer support representative" is nearly impossible to do precisely, and training required millions of trial-and-error episodes that are cheap in a game simulator and dangerous or expensive in the real world.

2.3 The LLM-as-Reasoning-Engine Turn (2022–present)

The breakthrough that makes today's agents practical was not a new kind of agent architecture — it was realizing that a large language model, already trained to predict plausible continuations of text, could be used as the decision-making component inside the classical perceive-act loop, without any additional RL training. The pivotal published pattern is ReAct (Reason + Act, 2022): instead of asking a model only for a final answer, you ask it to interleave short pieces of reasoning ("Thought: I need to check the account plan first") with explicit actions ("Action: lookup_account(email=...)"), observe the real result of that action, and continue reasoning from there. This turned the LLM into a general-purpose policy that needs no environment-specific training — you only need to describe the available actions in natural language (or, later, in a structured schema) and let the model's already-learned reasoning ability decide which one to take.

2023 saw an explosion of experimental autonomous agent projects (AutoGPT, BabyAGI, and many similar systems) that pushed this loop to its logical extreme: give the model a high-level goal and let it decide its own sub-goals, tools, and stopping point with minimal human structure. These projects were instructive failures as much as successes — they demonstrated vividly that unconstrained autonomy loops burn enormous token budgets, get stuck in repetitive cycles, and confidently pursue wrong sub-goals with no one checking in. The lessons from that era directly shaped the more disciplined, bounded, tool-schema-driven agent designs (native function/tool calling from model providers, and later structured protocols like MCP, covered in a later chapter of this volume) that this book teaches you to build in production today.

You are not inventing caution from scratch; you are inheriting hard-won lessons. Treat "how much autonomy should this agent have" as a deliberate, bounded engineering decision, not a default setting to leave wide open.

3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

Precisely, this chapter solves the following engineering problem:

Given a goal expressed in natural language and a set of actions the system is allowed to take in the real world (querying data, calling APIs, running code), construct a control loop that lets a language model decide which actions to take, in what order, based on the results of previous actions, until the goal is satisfied or a safe stopping condition is reached.

Unpack the clauses of that statement, because each one rules out a tempting but wrong simplification:

  • "Decide which actions to take" rules out a hard-coded pipeline. If you already know the fixed sequence of steps ahead of time (always call service A, then service B, then format the result), you do not need an agent — you need a deterministic workflow, and building an agent for that task is unnecessary complexity and unnecessary non-determinism. Agents earn their cost only when the correct sequence of actions genuinely depends on information not known in advance.
  • "Based on the results of previous actions" rules out a single-shot call with tools attached but no loop. A model that picks one tool, gets a result, and must immediately produce a final answer without the option to act again cannot handle tasks that require multiple, dependent lookups.
  • "Until the goal is satisfied or a safe stopping condition is reached" rules out unbounded autonomy. Every production agent must have an explicit, engineered answer to "how does this loop know when to stop?" — this is not automatic, and getting it wrong is the single most common cause of the runaway-cost and infinite-loop failures described in Section 2.3.

Framed this way, "agent" is not a marketing term for "chatbot with extra steps." It is a specific control-flow pattern — a loop with a model-driven decision point at its center — that you reach for only when the task's branching structure cannot be known ahead of time by the engineer writing the code.

4. THEORY

4.1 What Actually Makes Something an "Agent"

Strip away the hype and an agent, in the sense this volume uses the word, has exactly four ingredients working together:

IngredientRoleAnalogy for a Java engineer
Reasoning engineDecides what to do next given the current situationThe controller/decision logic — except here it's the LLM, not code you wrote
ToolsThe concrete actions the agent is allowed to takeA set of injected services/beans exposed through a fixed interface
Memory / stateWhat has happened so far in this episode (past thoughts, actions, observations)Accumulated request context — think a growing audit log passed along with each call
EnvironmentThe real world the tools act on and observe (a database, an API, a filesystem)Everything outside your process boundary

An "assistant" that simply answers questions from its own training data has a reasoning engine and nothing else — no tools, no environment interaction. A fixed automation script (a nightly batch job, a Camunda/BPMN workflow) has tools, memory, and an environment, but its "reasoning" is code you wrote, not a model deciding dynamically. An agent is specifically the combination where the reasoning engine is a model, deciding dynamically, at run time, which tool to invoke next based on what it has observed so far.

4.2 The Agent Loop as a Mental Model

The clean way to hold this in your head, before any code, is a single repeating cycle with a well-known name: Thought → Action → Observation, repeated until a Final Answer is produced.

  • Thought — the model's own reasoning about what it currently knows and what it needs next. This is not shown to the end user; it is the model "thinking out loud" in a scratchpad that becomes part of its own context on the next turn.
  • Action — a structured decision to call a specific tool with specific arguments, rather than to answer directly.
  • Observation — the real result returned by executing that action, injected back into the conversation as if it were new information the model just perceived.

The loop terminates the moment the model produces a Thought followed by a final, direct answer instead of another Action. Everything else in this chapter — the internal mechanics, the math, the production architecture — is elaboration on making this three-step cycle safe, bounded, observable, and reliable enough to run unattended in a real system.

4.3 The Autonomy Spectrum

Not all agents grant the model the same amount of freedom, and conflating these levels is a common source of miscommunication on engineering teams. It helps to think of autonomy as a spectrum rather than a binary:

If you do not have a rock-solid mental model of a single bounded agent loop, adding planning or multi-agent delegation on top will only make failures harder to diagnose, not easier.

5. INTERNAL WORKING

Here is what actually happens, mechanically, inside a single execution of an agent loop — the level of detail you need to reason about edge cases yourself rather than treating it as a black box.

  1. Initialization. The system assembles an initial context: a system prompt describing the agent's role and constraints, a machine-readable description of every available tool (its name, its purpose, and the shape of its arguments — typically a JSON Schema), and the user's goal. This entire bundle is sent to the model as the first request.
  2. Model decision. The model produces one of two kinds of output: either a direct, final answer (the model believes it already has enough information), or a structured request to invoke a specific tool with specific arguments. Modern model providers support this natively as "tool calling" or "function calling" — the model does not have to be asked to format this as free text; the provider's API returns a distinct, structured tool-call object.
  3. Validation. Before any tool actually runs, the orchestrating application validates the requested call: does this tool exist, do the arguments satisfy the schema (correct types, required fields present), and — critically — is this application permitted to execute it given the current user's authorization? This step exists entirely outside the model; the model can request anything, but the application decides what is actually allowed to happen, exactly the way a REST controller never trusts client-supplied data without validation.
  4. Execution. The application executes the real action — a database query, an HTTP call to another service, a calculation — and captures the result, including any error.
  5. Observation injection. The result (or the error, if the call failed) is appended to the conversation history as a new message, in a role specifically meant for tool results, and the entire updated history is sent back to the model for another decision round.
  6. Repeat or terminate. Steps 2 through 5 repeat. The loop must terminate under one of several conditions: the model produces a final answer instead of another tool call; a maximum iteration count configured by the engineer is reached; a token or cost budget is exhausted; or an explicit safety check (discussed in Section 13) halts execution for human review.

Several edge cases become visible only once you trace this sequence carefully, and each one has a direct engineering consequence covered later in this chapter:

  • What if the model requests a tool that does not exist, or with malformed arguments? This happens routinely, especially with smaller or less capable models. The orchestrator must return a clear, structured error observation ("tool 'lookup_acount' not found; did you mean 'lookup_account'?") rather than crashing — the model can often self-correct on the next turn if the error is legible.
  • What if a tool call succeeds but returns an unexpected or empty result? The model must be able to reason about "no results found" as meaningfully different from "the tool failed," or it may hallucinate a plausible-sounding result instead of reporting the absence honestly.
  • What if the model calls the same tool with the same arguments repeatedly? This is the classic symptom of a stuck loop — either the model has not correctly registered the prior observation, or the task genuinely cannot be completed with the tools available. Detecting repeated identical calls is a cheap, effective guardrail (Section 10).
  • What if the goal is ambiguous or was already achieved two steps ago? The model must decide when to stop, and it can be wrong in both directions — stopping too early with an incomplete answer, or continuing to "double check" indefinitely, consuming budget for no benefit.

6. MATHEMATICS

Agent fundamentals do not require new mathematical machinery beyond what earlier volumes already gave you (next-token probability, attention), but one lightweight formalism is genuinely useful here because it explains both why the pattern works at all and why costs behave the way they do: treating the agent loop as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), borrowed directly from the reinforcement learning lineage discussed in Section 2.

An MDP is defined by a state, a set of possible actions from that state, and a transition to a new state after an action is taken. Map this onto the agent loop:

  • State (S) — the full conversation history so far: system prompt, goal, and every prior thought/action/observation.
  • Action (A) — the next tool call (or the decision to answer directly), chosen by the model.
  • Transition — executing the tool produces an observation that gets appended to the state, deterministically producing the next state.
  • Policy (π) — the function mapping a state to an action. In classical RL this is learned through millions of reward-driven trials; in an LLM agent, the "policy" is simply the language model's own learned next-token behavior, applied to a prompt that describes the state and the available actions. No additional training is required — this substitution is the entire reason ReAct-style agents became practical the moment sufficiently capable models existed.

The one piece of arithmetic worth internalizing early, because it directly drives the cost and latency discussion in Section 12, is that the state at step n contains the entire history of steps 1 through n−1. Since every model call re-sends the whole accumulated context, the total tokens processed across a full agent episode grows roughly with the square of the number of steps, not linearly: if each step adds a roughly constant number of new tokens, the total tokens billed across an n-step episode is proportional to 1 + 2 + 3 + ... + n, which is n(n+1)/2. A 3-step agent episode and a 12-step agent episode are not 4x apart in cost — they are closer to 16x apart. This single fact is why bounding the number of iterations (Section 10) is a cost control, not just a safety control. 

Tokens processed across an n-step agent episode (illustrative, constant tokens/step = k)

 Step 1:  k                       →  cumulative:  k
 Step 2:  2k  (history + new)     →  cumulative:  3k
 Step 3:  3k                      →  cumulative:  6k
 Step 4:  4k                      →  cumulative: 10k
   ...
 Step n:  nk                      →  cumulative: k · n(n+1)/2

Three architectural elements deserve emphasis because newcomers routinely underestimate them. First, the tool registry is not a convenience — it is the enforcement point for least-privilege: a given agent instance should see only the tools it is authorized to use for the current user/tenant, never a fixed global list. Second, the episode state store exists because a real agent episode can span seconds to minutes across several model round-trips and possibly several application-server instances behind a load balancer — the accumulating history must live somewhere durable, not just in a local variable. Third, per-step tracing is not optional observability polish; without a span per Thought/Action/Observation, diagnosing why an agent took a wrong turn three steps before the wrong final answer is nearly impossible after the fact.

A minimal illustration of what a single bounded tool the model can call looks like in practice: 

@Component
public class AccountTools {

    private final AccountRepository accountRepository;

    public AccountTools(AccountRepository accountRepository) {
        this.accountRepository = accountRepository;
    }

    @Tool(description = "Look up a customer account and its plan by email address")
    public AccountSummary lookupAccount(String email) {
        return accountRepository.findByEmail(email)
                .map(AccountSummary::from)
                .orElseThrow(() -> new ToolExecutionException(
                        "No account found for email: " + email));
    }
}

10. BEST PRACTICES

  • Always set a hard maximum iteration count and a token/cost budget per episode. Never rely on the model to decide, unassisted, when it has looped too long — this is an application-level control, not a model-level one.
  • Give every tool a narrow, precisely described responsibility. A tool named doAccountStuff with a vague description invites the model to misuse it; a tool named lookupAccountByEmail with a description of exactly what it returns and does not return reduces wrong invocations dramatically.
  • Design tools to fail loudly and legibly, not silently. Return a clear, structured error the model can reason about ("account not found" vs. a raw stack trace) so the loop can self-correct instead of hallucinating a substitute answer.
  • Treat tool authorization as independent of the model's request. The model deciding to call a tool is not authorization to run it — the same permission checks you would apply to a REST endpoint must run before execution, scoped to the actual end user of the request.
  • Log every Thought/Action/Observation as a structured trace, keyed by a single episode/request id, from day one — retrofitting this after an incident is far harder than building it in from the start.
  • Prefer a bounded agent over an unbounded one until you have strong evidence you need more autonomy. Most real business tasks fit comfortably inside a handful of iterations; reach for planning (Chapter 55) or multi-agent designs only when a bounded loop has demonstrably failed to cover the task's complexity.

11. COMMON MISTAKES

  • Building an agent when a deterministic pipeline would do. If the sequence of steps is always the same regardless of input, you have a workflow, not an agent — wrapping it in a model-driven loop only adds latency, cost, and non-determinism for no benefit.
  • Giving the agent too many overlapping tools. When two tools do nearly the same thing, models frequently pick the wrong one or alternate between them across steps, wasting iterations. Consolidate or clearly differentiate overlapping capabilities.
  • No stopping condition beyond "the model decides." This is the direct, recurring cause of the runaway-loop and runaway-cost incidents described in Section 2.3 — always add an engineered ceiling.
  • Trusting tool arguments the model supplies without validation. A model can request a tool call with an out-of-range id, a malformed date, or — in adversarial contexts — an injected value designed to manipulate a downstream system; validate exactly as you would validate any untrusted client input.
  • Not distinguishing "tool returned an error" from "tool returned an empty/negative result" in the observation. If both look the same to the model, it cannot tell "the lookup failed, try again" from "the lookup succeeded and found nothing," and it may either retry uselessly or hallucinate a result.
  • Testing an agent only on the happy path. Because the branching structure is decided at run time by the model, the number of realistic execution paths is far larger than a fixed pipeline — evaluation (Chapter 53) must specifically exercise wrong-tool-choice, tool-failure, and ambiguous-goal scenarios, not just the scenario where every lookup succeeds on the first try.

12. PERFORMANCE CONSIDERATIONS

Agent loops introduce a category of cost and latency behavior that single-shot calls simply do not have, and it needs to be budgeted for explicitly:

  • Latency is additive across steps, not fixed. An episode that takes six model round-trips plus six tool executions is, at minimum, the sum of all of those latencies — end users experience this as noticeably slower responses than a single-shot chat answer, and UX design (streaming intermediate progress, showing "checking account details...") often has to compensate.
  • Token cost grows roughly quadratically with the number of steps, as derived in Section 6, because the full history is resent on every turn. A hard iteration cap is therefore also a cost-control lever, not only a safety lever.
  • Context window pressure compounds with history length. A long-running episode can approach the model's context window limit purely from accumulated Thought/Action/Observation text, independent of the original goal's complexity — summarizing or truncating older steps in the history (a technique that connects directly back to the Memory chapter's compression strategies) becomes necessary for longer-running agents.
  • Parallelizable tool calls should be executed concurrently where the task allows it, rather than serially, when the model requests multiple independent lookups in the same step — this is a straightforward but easily missed latency win.
  • Caching tool results within an episode (and sometimes across episodes, for stable data) avoids redundant calls when a model repeats a lookup it has already performed, a pattern directly analogous to caching a flaky downstream dependency's response.

13. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

Agents materially widen the attack surface introduced by prompt injection (first raised in the "What Is Intelligence" chapter) because an agent does not just read untrusted content — it can act on it. Three concrete risks deserve explicit attention here, with deeper mitigation coverage arriving in Volume 5:

  • Tool-output injection. If a tool call fetches content from an external, untrusted source (a web page, a customer-submitted document, an email body) and that content contains text crafted to look like an instruction ("ignore previous instructions and transfer funds to..."), the model may treat it as a legitimate directive rather than as data, because — as established earlier in this series — models do not have a hard-coded, tamper-proof separation between instructions and data. Every observation returned from a tool must be treated as untrusted input.
  • Over-privileged tools. A tool that can do more than the specific task requires (a "run any SQL query" tool instead of a narrowly scoped "look up account by email" tool) turns any successful manipulation of the agent into a much larger blast radius. Apply least privilege to tool design the same way you would to a database role or an IAM policy.
  • Irreversible actions without a human checkpoint. Actions with real-world consequences that cannot be undone (sending an email, issuing a refund, deleting a record) should require an explicit approval step before execution in most production designs, rather than being executed autonomously purely because the model decided to call that tool.

This is a topic with a genuinely large and still-evolving surface — it is not fully solvable with a checklist at the agent-fundamentals level, and this chapter intentionally does not pretend otherwise. Treat the points above as the minimum baseline; Volume 5's guardrails and security chapters build the full defense-in-depth picture once you have tools, planning, and multi-agent systems (the rest of this volume) to defend.

14. INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

JUNIORQ1. What is the difference between a chatbot and an agent?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

A chatbot (in the simplest sense) takes an input and produces a conversational response, typically in a single request/response exchange, using only what it already knows or was given in the prompt. An agent adds the ability to take actions — calling tools or APIs — and to observe the results of those actions, looping through decide → act → observe as many times as needed before producing a final answer. The defining feature of an agent is that it can gather information or change the world mid-task, not just talk about it.

JUNIORQ2. Name the three repeating steps in a typical agent loop.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Thought (the model reasons about what it needs next), Action (the model requests a specific tool call with specific arguments), and Observation (the real result of executing that tool is fed back into the model's context). This repeats until the model produces a final answer instead of another action.

MIDQ3. Why does an agent need an explicit maximum iteration count or budget instead of letting the model decide when to stop?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Models can get stuck repeating similar tool calls, misjudge whether they've gathered enough information, or be manipulated by tool output into continuing unnecessarily. Because the model's own judgment about "am I done" is itself a probabilistic decision rather than a guaranteed one, an unbounded loop can run indefinitely, burning tokens and cost with no automatic circuit breaker. Engineering a hard cap (iteration count, token budget, or wall-clock timeout) makes worst-case cost and latency bounded and predictable, independent of model behavior.

MIDQ4. Explain why token cost in an agent episode tends to grow faster than linearly with the number of steps.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Each model call in the loop must resend the entire accumulated history — every prior thought, action, and observation — because the model has no persistent memory between calls; only what is in the prompt exists to it. If each step contributes roughly a constant amount of new text, the cumulative tokens processed across n steps is proportional to the sum 1+2+...+n, i.e., n(n+1)/2, which grows quadratically, not linearly, with the number of steps.

MIDQ5. When should you NOT build an agent for a given task?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

When the correct sequence of steps is already known and fixed regardless of the input — i.e., the task is really a deterministic workflow or pipeline. In that case, a hard-coded orchestration (a fixed chain of calls, or even a simple sequential service method) is more predictable, cheaper, faster, and easier to test than a model-driven loop. Agents earn their added latency, cost, and non-determinism only when the branching structure of the task genuinely cannot be known ahead of time by the engineer.

SENIORQ6. Walk through what should happen, end to end, when a tool call fails inside an agent loop.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

The failure should never crash the whole episode outright. The orchestrator catches the failure at the execution layer and constructs a clear, structured observation describing what went wrong (distinguishing, for example, "not found" from "downstream service unavailable" from "invalid arguments") and feeds that observation back into the model's context exactly like a successful result would be. This lets the model reason about the failure on its next turn — retrying with corrected arguments, trying an alternative tool, or reporting to the user that it could not complete part of the task — rather than silently hallucinating a plausible-looking substitute result. Separately, the failure should be logged/traced for observability regardless of whether the model recovers gracefully, and repeated identical failures should count toward the episode's iteration/budget cap so a stuck retry loop still terminates.

Interview Gold Point: Explicitly connect this back to why the observation must be legible to the model as an error rather than ambiguous data — conflating "tool failed" with "tool returned nothing" is one of the most common root causes of agent hallucination, and naming that connection unprompted signals real production experience, not textbook recall.
SENIORQ7. How would you decide the right level of autonomy — bounded single agent vs. planning agent vs. multi-agent system — for a new feature?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Start from the task's actual branching complexity, not from what sounds most impressive to build. If the task needs zero or one dynamic decisions, a plain tool-augmented single call suffices. If it needs several dependent lookups whose order depends on intermediate results but stays within a small, boundable number of steps, a bounded single-agent loop is the right and cheapest fit. Only escalate to an explicit up-front planning step when tasks are long-horizon enough that forming and revising a plan measurably improves reliability over pure step-by-step reactivity, and only escalate to multiple specialized agents when a single agent's context, tool set, or role would otherwise become unmanageably broad. Each step up this ladder adds latency, cost, and failure surface, so the decision should be driven by evidence from evaluation (Chapter 53) showing the simpler design is insufficient, not by default.

STAFFQ8. A product team wants to give an agent a tool that can directly modify production customer records with no human approval step, to "make the agent feel more autonomous." How do you respond?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Frame the decision around reversibility and blast radius, not around how autonomous the experience should feel. Actions that are irreversible or high-impact — modifying financial records, deleting data, sending communications to customers — should require an explicit approval checkpoint (either synchronous human sign-off, or an asynchronous review step) before execution, regardless of how confident the agent's reasoning appears, because model confidence is not a calibrated correctness signal (a point already established as far back as the "What Is Intelligence" chapter). The staff-level counter-proposal is to separate "read/gather" tools, which can run autonomously, from "write/act" tools, which route through an approval gate initially, and to use production usage data and evaluation metrics to earn increased autonomy incrementally for specific, well-understood action types over time — never to grant full autonomy on high-risk actions from day one as a product decision.

16. SUMMARY

  • An agent is a loop, not a call: a reasoning engine (the LLM), a set of tools, accumulating memory, and an environment, combined so the model can decide dynamically which action to take next based on what it has observed so far.
  • The core mechanism is Thought → Action → Observation, repeated until the model produces a final answer or an engineered stopping condition (iteration cap, budget, timeout) is reached — the stopping condition must always be enforced by the application, never left entirely to the model.
  • Agents descend from classical planning and reinforcement learning, but the breakthrough that made them practical was using an already-trained LLM as the decision-making policy, with no additional environment-specific training required.
  • Reach for an agent only when the task's branching structure genuinely cannot be known ahead of time; a fixed sequence of steps is better served by a deterministic pipeline.
  • Because the full history resends on every step, agent token cost grows roughly quadratically with iteration count — bounding iterations is simultaneously a safety, cost, and latency control.
  • Every tool call is an expanded attack and failure surface: validate arguments and authorization independently of the model's request, treat tool outputs as untrusted data, and gate irreversible actions behind human approval until autonomy is earned with evidence.

Next chapter: Planning (Chapter 55) takes the bounded, reactive loop built here and adds an explicit, inspectable plan formed before execution begins — letting an agent decompose a large goal into ordered sub-goals, revise that plan when reality contradicts it, and handle tasks whose horizon is too long for pure step-by-step reactivity to handle reliably.
