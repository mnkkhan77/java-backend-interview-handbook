CHAPTER 40 — PERFORMANCE OPTIMIZATION

Volume 2 — Spring AI · Chapter 15 of 15

Testing (Chapter 39) gave you confidence that your Spring AI application produces correct and consistent-enough results under a rubric you trust. This chapter asks the next question a production owner will ask the moment traffic arrives: is it fast enough, cheap enough, and stable enough under load? An LLM-backed endpoint that passes every evaluation but takes eleven seconds and costs four cents per call will still get paged at 2 a.m. and still get cut from the budget next quarter. Performance Optimization is where correctness meets economics, and it closes out Volume 2 because everything you have built — ChatClient, Advisors, memory, tools, structured output, testing — now has to survive contact with real concurrency, real latency budgets, and real bills.

1. MOTIVATION

Every chapter so far in this volume has optimized for one thing: getting a Spring AI feature to work correctly. This chapter optimizes for the thing that determines whether that feature survives in production: whether it works correctly fast enough, for a low enough cost, at the concurrency your users actually generate. These are not cosmetic concerns you bolt on afterward. In AI-backed systems specifically, performance and correctness are entangled in a way traditional CRUD services rarely experience — a poorly tuned retry policy can silently double your token spend; a naive memory strategy can grow your prompt until latency and hallucination both spike together; a synchronous blocking call to a model provider can quietly starve your entire Tomcat thread pool during a traffic burst that a normal REST dependency would have shrugged off.

There is also a blunt financial reality that does not exist in most backend work: every millisecond of thinking time and every token of context you send is a metered, billed resource. A database query that is twice as slow costs you nothing extra per call — it just costs latency. An LLM call that processes twice as many input tokens because you forgot to trim conversation history (covered in the Memory chapter) costs you real money, on every single request, forever, until someone notices the bill. Performance optimization for AI systems is therefore simultaneously a latency problem, a throughput problem, and a cost-engineering problem, and the techniques for each overlap more than they do in traditional systems.

Finally, this is the last chapter of Volume 2 for a reason: performance is the dimension along which every previous chapter's decisions get graded together. The Advisor chain you built for logging and memory, the retrieval you wired for grounding, the structured output parser you added for reliability — each one added correctness and each one added latency. This chapter teaches you to see that trade-off explicitly, measure it, and make it deliberately instead of by accident.

2. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

2.1 Performance Engineering Before LLMs

Backend performance engineering has a long, well-understood toolbox: index your database, cache expensive reads, batch small writes, use connection pooling instead of opening a socket per request, move blocking I/O off the request thread, and measure with percentiles (p50/p95/p99) instead of averages because averages hide the tail latency that actually causes user complaints. If you have ever tuned a HikariCP pool size, added a Redis cache in front of a slow query, or moved an email send onto a message queue instead of the request thread, you already own most of the vocabulary this chapter needs. None of that knowledge is wasted here — it is the majority of what still applies.

2.2 Performance Engineering for Classical Machine Learning Serving

Before LLMs, the "serve a model efficiently" problem had already been solved once, for classical ML and early deep learning: systems like TensorFlow Serving, TorchServe, and NVIDIA Triton pioneered dynamic request batching (grouping many small inference requests into a single larger GPU batch to amortize fixed overhead), model quantization (representing weights in lower precision — 16-bit or 8-bit instead of 32-bit — to trade a small amount of accuracy for large gains in memory and speed), and model distillation (training a smaller, faster model to imitate a larger one). These three ideas — batch aggressively, reduce precision, reduce model size — are the direct ancestors of the levers you now pull as an application engineer, except that as a Spring AI developer you usually do not implement them yourself; you choose a provider or a hosted model tier that has already made these trade-offs, and your job shifts to using that choice wisely.

2.3 The LLM-Specific Turn: Autoregression Changes Everything

Large language models introduced a performance characteristic that classical ML serving never had to deal with at the same scale: autoregressive, token-by-token generation. A classical model produces one output in one forward pass. An LLM must run one forward pass per output token, because each new token depends on every token generated before it (this mechanism is covered fully in the Transformers chapter). This single fact explains almost every LLM-specific optimization technique that emerged between 2020 and today: KV-caching (avoiding recomputation of earlier tokens' internal representations during decoding), speculative decoding (using a small, fast "draft" model to guess several tokens ahead and having the large model verify them in one pass instead of generating them one at a time), continuous batching (dynamically merging in-flight requests at the token level rather than the request level, because requests finish at wildly different times), and prompt/context caching (letting a provider reuse the already-processed representation of a prompt prefix across multiple calls, so you are not billed or delayed for re-reading the same system prompt or document on every request). Providers like OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google have all shipped prompt-caching features precisely because of this cost structure. As an application engineer, you rarely implement KV-caching or speculative decoding yourself, but you are the one who decides whether your request pattern is shaped to take advantage of them — a stable, reused prompt prefix benefits enormously from provider-side caching; a prompt that is reshuffled or re-ordered on every call gets none of that benefit.

Confusing these two layers is the single most common reason engineers new to AI performance work look in the wrong place first.

3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

Strip away the vocabulary and the problem this chapter solves is:

Given a Spring AI application whose dominant cost is an external, metered, high-latency, and only partially controllable model call, minimize end-to-end latency and monetary cost per request while maximizing sustainable throughput under real concurrency — without silently degrading output quality.

Every clause in that sentence rules out a naive solution. "External and only partially controllable" rules out classical solutions like just making your own code faster — for most requests, the model call itself dominates total latency, so optimizing your surrounding Java code first is optimizing the wrong 5%. "Metered and monetary" means the fastest possible solution (always call the biggest, most capable model, always send maximum context) is usually also the most expensive one, so latency and cost pull in the same direction only sometimes — often they trade off against each other and against quality. "Real concurrency" means a solution that looks fine in a single-user demo (Chapter 39's territory) can collapse the moment fifty users hit it simultaneously, because thread pools, connection limits, and provider rate limits all behave differently under load than in isolation. "Without silently degrading output quality" is the guardrail that stops this chapter from being a race to the cheapest, fastest, worst possible answer — trimming context too aggressively or routing every request to the cheapest model can "optimize" your way into an unusable product.

4. THEORY

4.1 The Three Metrics You Are Actually Trading Off

Before touching a single knob, fix three definitions in your head, because engineers new to this space routinely conflate them:

MetricDefinitionWho Feels It
LatencyTime from request sent to (final) response received, for one requestThe user waiting for an answer
ThroughputNumber of requests a system can sustain per unit time across many concurrent usersThe platform, under load
CostMoney spent per request (tokens billed) and per unit time (infrastructure)Finance, at the end of the month

These three are not independent. Sending less context lowers cost and often lowers latency, but if it removes information the model needed, it can lower effective throughput by increasing retries or follow-up questions. Using a bigger, smarter model can raise per-call cost and latency but lower total cost if it avoids a costly multi-step retry loop. There is no single "optimize performance" button — there is a design space, and your job is to pick a point in it deliberately for each feature, based on that feature's actual requirements (a real-time chat assistant has a very different latency budget than an overnight batch summarization job).

4.2 Time-to-First-Token vs. Time-to-Last-Token

LLM latency is not one number — it splits into two phases with very different characteristics, and this split is the single most important mental model in this chapter:

  • Prefill (time-to-first-token, TTFT): the time for the model to read and process your entire input prompt before it produces the very first output token. This phase scales roughly with input length and is largely parallelizable internally.
  • Decode (time-per-output-token, and by extension time-to-last-token, TTLT): the time to generate each subsequent token, one at a time, autoregressively. This phase scales with output length and, unlike prefill, cannot be meaningfully parallelized for a single request because each token depends on the one before it.

This split explains a fact that surprises engineers coming from traditional APIs: a request that returns a long answer is slow mostly because of decode time, not because your prompt was long. Doubling your input context might add a modest, roughly linear amount to TTFT; asking for twice as much output can dramatically increase total latency, because it doubles the number of sequential decode steps. This single insight — control output length, not just input length — is one of the highest-leverage, most underused optimizations available to you as an application engineer, and it costs nothing to apply: setting a sensible `maxTokens` and prompting for concision are nearly free wins.

4.3 Streaming as a Latency Illusion (That Is Also Real)

Streaming (returning tokens to the client as they are generated, instead of waiting for the full response) does not reduce total generation time — the model still has to produce every token. What it changes is perceived latency: the user sees the first words appear after TTFT instead of after TTLT, which for a long response can be the difference between a two-second wait and a fifteen-second wait before anything visible happens. This is a genuine, not merely cosmetic, user-experience win, and it is one of the cheapest performance improvements available in Spring AI, because the framework supports it natively (Section 9). But it is worth being honest that streaming optimizes perceived latency and UX responsiveness, not total compute cost or total wall-clock generation time — do not confuse "feels faster" with "is cheaper" when reporting numbers to stakeholders.

4.4 Caching Layers: Where Repetition Can Be Turned Into Free Answers

Because LLM calls are expensive relative to almost anything else in your stack, caching earns its keep faster here than almost anywhere else in a typical Spring application. There are at least three distinct caching opportunities, each catching a different kind of repetition:

  • Exact-response caching: identical input (same prompt, same parameters) → return the previously computed answer without calling the model at all. Effective for FAQ-style or deterministic tasks (with temperature at or near zero), nearly useless for open-ended creative or highly personalized conversation.
  • Semantic caching: use embedding similarity (Volume 3 territory, previewed here) to detect that a new question is close enough in meaning to a previously answered one, and reuse (or lightly adapt) that answer instead of recomputing. Higher hit rate than exact-match caching, but requires a similarity threshold tuned carefully to avoid returning a wrong-but-similar answer.
  • Provider-side prompt/context caching: as covered in Section 2, several providers will cache the internal representation of a stable prompt prefix (a long system prompt, a large document you reuse across many questions) and charge and process it far more cheaply on subsequent calls, provided the prefix is byte-identical and appears in the same position across calls.

Notice the shape of the trade here: the first two are things you build in Spring; the third is something you get for free from the provider if — and only if — you structure your prompts to keep the reusable, static part first and the variable, per-request part last. This single ordering habit (static content before dynamic content in the prompt) is a zero-cost architectural decision that directly increases provider-side cache hit rate.

5. INTERNAL WORKING

To optimize a system you must first know where the time actually goes. Walk a single chat request through a realistic Spring AI application end to end, and note where each millisecond is spent — this is the map every later optimization in this chapter attaches to.

5.1 Step-by-Step Request Lifecycle

  1. Client → Spring Boot controller. Network round trip, TLS handshake (amortized by keep-alive connections), request deserialization. Typically single-digit milliseconds if your infrastructure is healthy.
  2. Rate limiting / auth / request shaping. Cheap in isolation, but if implemented with blocking calls to an external auth service without caching, this can quietly add tens of milliseconds per request under load.
  3. Advisor chain execution (pre-call). Memory retrieval (loading prior conversation turns — a database or cache read), retrieval-augmented context fetch if RAG is wired in (a vector search — Volume 3), and any logging/guardrail advisors. Each Advisor in the chain (Chapter on Advisors) adds sequential latency unless explicitly parallelized.
  4. Prompt assembly. Concatenating system prompt, retrieved context, conversation history, and the new user message. Purely in-process CPU work — fast, but the size of what gets assembled here directly determines the next step's cost.
  5. Network call to the model provider. DNS/TLS/connection reuse, request serialization, then the provider's own queueing delay (which grows under the provider's own load, entirely outside your control) before your request even begins processing.
  6. Prefill phase (server-side, provider-controlled). The model reads your full assembled prompt. Roughly proportional to input token count; benefits from provider-side prompt caching if your prefix is stable.
  7. Decode phase (server-side, provider-controlled). The model generates output tokens one at a time. Roughly proportional to output token count; this is usually the single largest contributor to total latency for anything beyond a short answer.
  8. Streaming back / full response return. Either tokens arrive incrementally (perceived latency improvement, Section 4.3) or the full payload arrives once generation completes.
  9. Advisor chain execution (post-call). Response logging, memory persistence (writing the new turn back to storage), guardrail/output validation, structured-output parsing (Chapter on Structured Output) — each is sequential in-process work unless explicitly made asynchronous.
  10. Response serialization and return to client. Typically negligible unless the payload is unusually large.

The uncomfortable but critical realization from this walk-through: in a typical single-turn chat request, steps 6 and 7 (prefill and decode, entirely inside the provider) usually account for 70–95% of total latency. Everything you as a Spring engineer directly control (steps 1–5 and 8–10) is usually a small minority of the total time budget — which is exactly why Section 4's insight (control output length, shape prompts for caching, choose the right model) matters more than micro-optimizing your Java code. That said, under concurrency, steps 3, 4, and 9 stop being "small" the moment they involve blocking I/O on a limited thread pool — a slow synchronous memory-store read repeated across a thousand concurrent requests can starve your server long before the model provider becomes your bottleneck. Both truths hold simultaneously: the model call dominates latency for one request, and your own code can dominate throughput under load.

5.2 Edge Cases a Reader Should Be Able to Reason Through

  • What happens if the Advisor chain does a vector search AND a database memory read sequentially, each taking 80ms? You have added 160ms of pure latency before the model is even called — invisible in a single-request test, very visible in a p95 latency dashboard once you multiply it by chain depth and concurrency contention.
  • What happens if you cache full responses keyed only on the user's message text, ignoring conversation history? Two different users (or the same user in two different conversations) asking "what did I just ask you?" would get the same cached, wrong answer — caching keys must include everything that affects correctness, not just the most visible input.
  • What happens if you stream tokens to the client but your Advisor chain's guardrail/output-validation step needs to see the complete response first? You cannot validate a response you have not fully received — streaming and post-hoc output validation are in genuine tension, and reconciling them (validate incrementally, or validate and then reveal) is a real design decision, not a checkbox.

6. MATHEMATICS

This topic needs modest, practical mathematics — not because the ideas are shallow, but because the two formulas below are genuinely how capacity and cost get planned in real systems, and skipping them would leave you unable to size anything.

6.1 Little's Law — Sizing Concurrency

Little's Law, borrowed directly from queueing theory and already familiar to anyone who has sized a thread pool or a connection pool, states: 

        L = λ × W

  L = average number of requests "in flight" (in the system at once)
  λ = average arrival rate (requests per second)
  W = average time each request spends in the system (latency)

Apply this directly to an AI endpoint: if your chat endpoint has an average end-to-end latency of 3 seconds (W), and you expect 20 requests per second at peak (λ), you need your system — thread pool, HTTP client connection pool, and the provider's own rate limit for your account — to sustain 60 concurrent in-flight requests (L = 20 × 3). This single calculation is why "just add more replicas" without checking your provider's concurrent-request or tokens-per-minute rate limit is a common production outage: your Spring Boot instances can happily hold 60 concurrent requests in their thread pools while the provider silently starts returning HTTP 429 (rate limited) once you cross its ceiling.

6.2 Cost as a Direct Function of Tokens

Unlike most backend cost models, LLM cost per request is a simple, calculable formula you should be comfortable writing on a whiteboard: 

  cost_per_request = (input_tokens × price_per_input_token)
                    + (output_tokens × price_per_output_token)

  monthly_cost ≈ requests_per_month × avg(cost_per_request)

Input and output tokens are typically priced differently (output is usually several times more expensive than input, because decode is the expensive, sequential phase described in Section 4.2). This asymmetry is precisely why "ask for a shorter answer" is a stronger cost lever than "send a shorter prompt" — trimming 500 tokens of unnecessary output typically saves more money than trimming 500 tokens of input, even though both feel like equivalent-sized cuts on paper.

6.3 Cache Hit Rate and Effective Cost

  effective_cost_per_request = (1 − hit_rate) × full_cost
                              + hit_rate × cached_cost

  (cached_cost ≈ 0 for exact-match app-level caching;
   cached_cost ≈ 0.1×–0.5× full_cost for provider prompt caching,
   depending on provider pricing)

This formula is the honest justification for investing engineering time in caching at all: a semantic cache with a modest 30% hit rate on a high-traffic endpoint can cut effective cost by nearly a third with no change to the model or the user experience — often a larger single lever than switching to a cheaper model, and without the quality risk that model-switching carries.

9. JAVA / SPRING PERSPECTIVE

Spring AI and the modern Java platform give you concrete, idiomatic tools for nearly every lever described above.

9.1 Streaming with ChatClient

Spring AI's ChatClient exposes a reactive streaming API that returns a Flux<String> (or a Flux of richer chunk types), letting you forward tokens to the client as they are produced instead of blocking until generation completes: 

@RestController
class ChatController {

    private final ChatClient chatClient;

    ChatController(ChatClient.Builder builder) {
        this.chatClient = builder.build();
    }

    @GetMapping(value = "/chat/stream", produces = MediaType.TEXT_EVENT_STREAM_VALUE)
    Flux<String> streamChat(@RequestParam String message) {
        return chatClient.prompt()
                .user(message)
                .stream()
                .content();          // Flux<String> — one element per token/chunk
    }
}

This one change — swapping a blocking .call().content() for .stream().content() — is usually the single highest-leverage, lowest-effort perceived-latency win available in a Spring AI application, precisely because it converts a "wait for TTLT" experience into a "wait for TTFT" experience (Section 4.3) for free.

9.2 Bounding Output Length Explicitly

9.3 Virtual Threads for Advisor-Chain I/O

Java 21's virtual threads (enabled in Spring Boot via spring.threads.virtual.enabled=true) are a strong match for this workload precisely because a chat request's lifecycle (Section 5.1) is dominated by blocking waits — network calls to the model provider, cache reads, vector search calls — rather than CPU work. Virtual threads let you keep the simple, synchronous programming model for Advisors and memory stores while avoiding the platform-thread exhaustion that used to force teams into fully reactive rewrites just to survive concurrent load. This does not replace the reactive Flux-based streaming API in Section 9.1 — the two solve different problems (concurrency scalability vs. perceived per-request latency) and are commonly used together.

9.4 Resilience4j Around the Model Call

@CircuitBreaker(name = "chatModel", fallbackMethod = "fallbackResponse")
@TimeLimiter(name = "chatModel")
@Retry(name = "chatModel")
public CompletableFuture<String> askModel(String message) {
    return CompletableFuture.supplyAsync(() ->
        chatClient.prompt().user(message).call().content());
}

private CompletableFuture<String> fallbackResponse(String message, Throwable t) {
    return CompletableFuture.completedFuture(
        "The assistant is temporarily unavailable — please try again shortly.");
}

Configure the timeout deliberately using your measured p95/p99 latency (Section 12), not a guessed round number — a timeout set below your real p95 will trip the circuit breaker on entirely healthy traffic, and one set far above it will let a hung request tie up a thread far longer than necessary.

9.5 Application-Level Caching with Spring Cache

@Cacheable(value = "chatResponses", key = "#message", condition = "#temperature == 0.0")
public String deterministicAnswer(String message, double temperature) {
    return chatClient.prompt().user(message)
            .options(OpenAiChatOptions.builder().temperature(temperature).build())
            .call().content();
}

Note the guard condition: caching only makes sense for low/zero-temperature, deterministic-style requests — caching a high-temperature creative response as if it were the single correct answer defeats the purpose of asking for variety and will produce visibly stale, repetitive output.

10. BEST PRACTICES

  • Measure before optimizing — instrument token counts, TTFT, TTLT, and cost per endpoint from day one. Spring AI's response metadata exposes usage/token counts; wire it into your metrics pipeline (Micrometer) rather than discovering your cost structure from a monthly invoice.
  • Cap output length deliberately per use case. A classification task needs a handful of tokens; a summarization task needs a budget matched to the expected summary length. An unbounded maxTokens is an unbounded latency and cost tail.
  • Order prompts with static content first, dynamic content last to maximize provider-side prompt-caching eligibility (Section 4.4) — this is free money left on the table if ignored.
  • Route by task difficulty, not uniformly to your "best" model. Classification, extraction, and simple rewriting tasks are frequently well served by smaller, cheaper, faster models; reserve the most capable (and expensive) model for tasks that demonstrably need it, validated against your evaluation suite from Chapter 39.
  • Set timeouts from measured percentiles, not guesses, and pair every timeout with an explicit fallback (Section 9.4) so a slow provider degrades your feature instead of your whole request thread pool.
  • Stream by default for any user-facing, multi-sentence response. The engineering cost is low and the perceived-latency win is consistently large.
  • Load-test against provider rate limits, not just your own infrastructure. Use Little's Law (Section 6.1) to compute expected concurrency and confirm it against your account's tokens-per-minute and requests-per-minute ceilings before a real launch.

11. COMMON MISTAKES

  • Optimizing your Java code before checking where the time actually goes. As Section 5.1 showed, prefill and decode inside the provider usually dominate — shaving milliseconds off prompt-assembly code while ignoring an unbounded maxTokens setting is optimizing the wrong 5%.
  • Letting conversation memory grow unbounded. Every turn appended to history without summarization or windowing (Memory chapter) linearly increases input tokens, cost, and prefill latency on every subsequent turn of that conversation — a slow-motion performance regression that will not show up in a short test session.
  • Caching without including all relevant inputs in the cache key. Forgetting to key on conversation ID, user identity, or retrieved context produces stale or cross-contaminated answers — a correctness bug caused directly by a performance optimization.
  • Treating streaming as a substitute for actually reducing output length or model size. Streaming improves perceived latency, not total cost or total generation time (Section 4.3) — teams that stop optimizing once they add streaming often discover their cost problem was never addressed.
  • Sizing thread pools and connection pools without checking the provider's own concurrency limits. Scaling your Spring Boot replicas can create a wall of HTTP 429s the moment aggregate concurrency crosses the provider's ceiling — a failure mode invisible in single-instance testing.
  • Reordering or randomizing prompt content "for variety" without realizing it breaks prefix-based provider caching, silently paying full price and full prefill latency on every call that could have been discounted.
  • Reporting average latency instead of p95/p99. A system with a great average and a terrible tail still produces a stream of angry users — averages hide exactly the failures that matter most operationally.

12. PERFORMANCE CONSIDERATIONS

This entire chapter is about performance, so this section focuses on the concrete numbers and trade-off shapes you should carry into a real design review rather than repeating the theory above.

  • Latency budget allocation. For a typical customer-facing chat feature, a reasonable target split might be: <100ms for auth/routing/Advisor pre-work, <500ms TTFT, and a decode rate the user perceives as "typing speed" via streaming — total perceived responsiveness matters more than raw TTLT once streaming is in place.
  • Throughput ceilings are usually provider-imposed, not infrastructure-imposed. Before scaling Spring Boot replicas to handle more load, confirm your provider tier's requests-per-minute and tokens-per-minute limits — they are frequently the true ceiling, and Little's Law (Section 6.1) tells you exactly how much concurrency you can sustain within them.
  • Memory footprint of conversation history matters twice over: once as a token-cost/latency driver (Section 6.2) and once as literal JVM heap/cache memory if you hold many concurrent conversations in an in-memory store — externalize memory to Redis or a database once conversation count grows past a small in-process cache's comfort zone.
  • Cost concentration follows a long tail. In most real deployments, a small fraction of requests (very long conversations, large document uploads for summarization) account for a disproportionate share of total token spend — instrument and cap these outliers specifically rather than optimizing only the median case.
  • Caching returns diminish with personalization. Highly personalized, context-heavy conversations have inherently low cache hit rates; do not expect the same caching wins on a fully personalized assistant that you would see on a shared FAQ endpoint — measure hit rate per endpoint rather than assuming a single number applies everywhere.

13. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

Performance optimization has a real but genuinely narrower security surface than topics like prompt injection or data governance — it is worth being honest about that rather than manufacturing risk. Three concrete issues do belong here:

  • Cache poisoning and cross-user leakage. If an exact-match or semantic cache key is built carelessly (Section 11), one user's cached answer can leak into another user's session, or a maliciously crafted input can poison a shared cache entry that subsequent users then receive as if it were their own answer. Always scope cache keys to the correct trust boundary (per-user, per-tenant) unless the content is genuinely non-personalized and safe to share.
  • Denial-of-wallet via unbounded requests. Because cost scales directly with tokens (Section 6.2), an attacker who can submit arbitrarily long input (or repeatedly trigger long, expensive generations) can drive your bill up deliberately — a cost-based analog of a denial-of-service attack. Enforce input length limits, output token caps, and per-user rate limits as a security control, not merely a cost control.
  • Timing side channels from cache hits. A cache hit returning near-instantly versus a cache miss taking full model latency can, in principle, let an attacker infer whether a specific query (or one similar to it) has been asked before by someone else — relevant mainly in systems with shared caches across users handling sensitive queries; mitigate by scoping caches per-tenant and avoiding cross-user cache sharing for anything sensitive.

14. INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

JUNIORQ1. What is the difference between latency and throughput in the context of an LLM-backed API?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Latency is the time a single request takes from send to complete response. Throughput is how many requests the system can sustain per unit time across many concurrent users. A system can have low latency for one request but low throughput under load if it cannot handle many requests concurrently (e.g., a small thread pool or a provider rate limit), and the two must be measured and optimized separately.

JUNIORQ2. Why does streaming a response improve user experience even though it does not reduce total generation time?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

The model still has to generate every output token, so total compute time is unchanged. But without streaming, the user sees nothing until the entire response is done (time-to-last-token); with streaming, the user sees the first tokens after time-to-first-token, which is usually much shorter, so the request feels dramatically faster even though the underlying work took the same amount of time.

MIDQ3. Explain the difference between the prefill and decode phases of an LLM request, and why that difference matters for optimization.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Prefill is the phase where the model reads and processes the entire input prompt before producing any output; it scales roughly with input length and is largely parallelizable internally. Decode is the phase where the model generates output tokens one at a time, autoregressively, where each token depends on all previous tokens; it scales with output length and cannot be parallelized within a single request. It matters because decode is usually the dominant cost for typical requests, meaning capping and shortening the expected output (e.g., via maxTokens and concise prompting) is often a more effective latency and cost lever than shortening the input prompt.

MIDQ4. You add a cache in front of your chat endpoint but users start seeing answers meant for other users. What went wrong and how do you fix it?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

The cache key almost certainly omits an identity or context dimension that should distinguish the requests — for example, caching purely on message text without including user ID, tenant ID, or conversation ID. The fix is to include every input that affects the correct answer in the cache key (user/tenant scope, relevant conversation state, retrieved context), not just the most visible text input, and to default to per-user or per-tenant cache scoping unless the content is verified to be safe and identical to share across users.

SENIORQ5. How would you use Little's Law to decide how many concurrent in-flight requests your system needs to support, and what does that tell you about scaling strategy?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Little's Law states L = λ × W: the average number of requests in flight equals the arrival rate multiplied by the average time each request spends in the system. Given an expected peak arrival rate (from traffic projections) and a measured average end-to-end latency, you can compute the required concurrency the system — thread pools, HTTP connection pools, and the provider's own rate limits — must sustain. This matters for scaling strategy because it reveals that simply adding more Spring Boot replicas does not help once aggregate concurrency exceeds the model provider's own concurrent-request or tokens-per-minute ceiling; at that point the fix is provider-side (higher tier, multiple provider accounts, request queuing/shedding) rather than infrastructure-side.

Interview Gold Point: Mention explicitly that provider rate limits, not your own infrastructure, are frequently the true throughput ceiling in LLM-backed systems — this is a distinction many candidates miss, and naming it signals real production experience rather than textbook knowledge.
SENIORQ6. A stakeholder wants every request routed to your most capable (and most expensive) model "to guarantee quality." How do you respond from a performance and cost engineering standpoint?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

I would push back with data rather than intuition: build an evaluation set (from the Testing/Evaluation work) covering the actual task distribution, and measure whether a smaller, cheaper, faster model performs acceptably on the easier segment of that distribution — extraction, classification, simple rewriting tasks are frequently well served by smaller models. Propose a cost-aware routing strategy that sends only genuinely hard cases (as identified by the evaluation results, or by a lightweight classifier/heuristic) to the expensive model, and show the latency and cost delta this produces at expected volume. This turns "guarantee quality" into "guarantee quality where it is actually at risk," which is both cheaper and, because the expensive model has more headroom for the genuinely hard requests, sometimes even yields better aggregate outcomes.

SENIORQ7. Why can naive prompt construction defeat provider-side prompt caching, and how would you structure prompts to take advantage of it?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at senior level

Provider-side prompt caching works by recognizing a byte-identical, stably-positioned prefix across multiple calls and reusing its already-processed internal representation, avoiding reprocessing (and often reducing billed cost) for that portion of the prompt. If dynamic, per-request content (user message, timestamps, reordered instructions) is placed before or interleaved with the static content (system prompt, few-shot examples, large reference documents), the prefix is never identical across calls and the cache never hits. The fix is to structure prompts with the static, reusable content first and the variable, per-request content last, and to keep that ordering completely consistent across calls, so the provider can consistently recognize and reuse the cached prefix.

STAFFQ8. Design the performance and cost observability strategy you would put in place before launching a customer-facing Spring AI feature at scale. What would you measure, and what would trigger an alert?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

I would instrument, per endpoint and per model, at minimum: input and output token counts (for cost attribution), time-to-first-token and time-to-last-token as separate histograms (p50/p95/p99, never just averages), cache hit rate broken out by cache layer (exact-match, semantic, provider prompt cache where visible), provider error and rate-limit (429) rates, and cost-per-request rolled up to cost-per-day by feature. Alerting thresholds would be set from measured baselines, not guesses: alert on p95 TTFT/TTLT exceeding an agreed SLO, on a sustained rise in rate-limit errors (an early warning of an approaching provider ceiling per Little's Law), on cache hit rate dropping unexpectedly (often signals a prompt-construction regression breaking cache keys or provider prefix caching), and on daily cost per feature deviating meaningfully from its rolling baseline (catching runaway conversation growth or an unbounded output-length bug before it becomes a large invoice). The unifying design principle is that every lever discussed in this chapter — output length, caching, model routing, concurrency — is only manageable if it is first observable at the token and dollar level, per request.

JUNIORQ9. What is the difference between WebFlux and Spring MVC when calling a ChatClient, and does it matter for a single low-traffic endpoint?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Spring MVC handles each request on a dedicated (platform or virtual) thread and blocks that thread while waiting for the model provider to respond. WebFlux uses a small, fixed pool of event-loop threads and never blocks — a slow model call simply yields the thread back to handle other requests while it waits. For a single low-traffic endpoint the difference is invisible: one blocked thread among hundreds costs nothing. The difference only matters once concurrency rises high enough that thread count itself becomes the bottleneck, which is why this is a scaling decision, not a correctness one.

JUNIORQ10. What does maxTokens actually control, and why is leaving it unset risky?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

maxTokens caps how many output tokens the model is allowed to generate before it is forced to stop — it directly bounds the decode phase described in Section 4.2, which is usually the largest contributor to both latency and cost. Leaving it unset (or setting it unrealistically high) means a single unusual prompt — one that triggers a rambling, repetitive, or looping generation — can produce a response that is both very slow and very expensive, with no upper bound. Setting a sensible cap per use case is one of the cheapest guardrails available.

JUNIORQ11. Why is opening a new HTTP connection per model call wasteful, and what does Spring AI use to avoid it?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Opening a new TCP connection means paying the TCP handshake and TLS negotiation cost on every single request — often tens of milliseconds — before your prompt has even been sent. Spring AI's provider clients are built on Spring's RestClient/WebClient, which are backed by a pooled, reusable HTTP connection pool: connections are kept alive and reused across requests, so that handshake cost is paid once per connection rather than once per call. Misconfiguring or bypassing that shared client (for example, by constructing a new client per request) silently reintroduces this cost on every call.

JUNIORQ12. What is a blocking call, and why can synchronous ChatClient.call() calls under load exhaust a servlet thread pool?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

A blocking call ties up the calling thread for the entire duration of the operation — the thread cannot do any other work while it waits. ChatClient.call() blocks the request-handling thread until the full model response returns, which given typical multi-second LLM latency (Section 5.1) is a long time to hold a thread. On a traditional Tomcat platform-thread pool with a fixed size, enough concurrent slow requests will consume every available thread, leaving new requests queued or rejected even though the CPU itself is nearly idle — the server is thread-starved, not compute-starved.

JUNIORQ13. What is request batching in the context of calling an LLM provider, and can an application developer implement it themselves?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Batching means combining multiple independent inference requests so the underlying serving infrastructure processes them together, amortizing fixed overhead across them (Section 2.2). True token-level continuous batching happens inside the provider's serving stack and is not something a Spring AI developer implements directly. What an application developer can do is application-level batching — grouping several independent, non-urgent tasks (e.g., classifying 20 items) into a single prompt that asks the model to process all of them in one call, trading a little latency per item for far fewer total round trips and less repeated fixed prompt overhead.

JUNIORQ14. Why might choosing a smaller, faster model sometimes be the better engineering decision even when a larger model is available?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Bigger models are not universally better for every task — for many tasks (classification, extraction, short rewrites) a smaller model produces equivalent-quality output at a fraction of the latency and cost, because both prefill and decode are faster on a smaller model and pricing scales with model tier. Defaulting to the largest model for every request pays a latency and cost tax on the majority of requests that never needed that capability, while the tasks that actually benefit from a larger model are usually a minority best identified through evaluation (Chapter 39), not assumption.

Interview Gold Point: Naming "route by task difficulty, not uniformly to your best model" as a design principle, and tying it back to evaluation data rather than intuition, signals you think about model selection as an engineering decision rather than a purely qualitative one.
JUNIORQ15. What is prompt compression, and give one simple technique.

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Prompt compression is any technique that reduces the number of input tokens sent to the model while preserving the information the model actually needs to answer correctly. A simple technique is stripping redundant boilerplate — repeated instructions, verbose formatting, or unnecessary whitespace — from a system prompt or retrieved document before it is assembled into the final prompt. More aggressive techniques include summarizing long reference documents before including them, or using a smaller model to condense retrieved context prior to passing it to the main model.

JUNIORQ16. What does "context trimming" mean and why does it matter for both cost and latency?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Context trimming means deliberately limiting how much conversation history, retrieved documents, or other context gets included in a prompt, rather than including everything available by default. It matters for cost because input tokens are billed directly (Section 6.2), and for latency because prefill time scales roughly with input length (Section 4.2). Without trimming, a long-running conversation's prompt grows on every turn, meaning cost and latency both quietly increase turn over turn even though the user is asking equally simple questions — a slow-motion regression that a short demo session will never reveal.

JUNIORQ17. What is the practical benefit of Java virtual threads for a Spring AI application specifically?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

A typical Spring AI request spends most of its time blocked waiting on I/O — the model provider, a cache, a memory store — rather than doing CPU work (Section 5.1). Virtual threads let you write that logic in the normal synchronous, blocking style while the JVM transparently unmounts the virtual thread from its underlying platform thread during the wait, freeing that platform thread to serve other requests. This gives much of the concurrency scalability of a reactive rewrite without giving up the simpler synchronous programming model for Advisors, memory stores, and business logic.

JUNIORQ18. Why does temperature setting matter when deciding whether to cache a response?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Temperature controls how deterministic versus varied the model's output is. At or near zero, the same input reliably produces the same (or nearly the same) output, so caching that response and reusing it for identical future inputs is safe and effective. At higher temperatures the model is deliberately generating varied, creative output on each call; caching one instance of that output and serving it repeatedly defeats the purpose of asking for variety and will make responses feel stale and repetitive to users who expect fresh generations.

MIDQ19. Walk through how to implement an async, non-blocking model call using CompletableFuture when you are on a traditional Spring MVC (Tomcat) stack rather than WebFlux.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Wrap the blocking ChatClient call in CompletableFuture.supplyAsync(...), submitted to a dedicated, explicitly sized executor rather than the common ForkJoinPool, and return a CompletableFuture<String> (or use Spring MVC's async request support, e.g. DeferredResult) from the controller method. This releases the original Tomcat request thread back to the pool immediately after submission, and the response is written once the future completes on the executor thread. It gives you most of the throughput benefit of not holding a request thread for the full model call duration, without requiring a WebFlux rewrite of the whole application.

MIDQ20. How would you size a dedicated thread pool for blocking model calls, and why should it be separate from the default Tomcat request-handling pool?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Use Little's Law (Section 6.1) as the starting point: pool size ≈ expected concurrent in-flight model calls (arrival rate × average call latency), with headroom for latency variance. Keeping this pool separate from the pool that handles inbound HTTP requests matters because it lets you cap AI-call concurrency independently — protecting the provider from being hammered past its own rate limits — without also capping how many unrelated, fast HTTP requests (health checks, non-AI endpoints) your application can serve concurrently. Mixing the two pools means a slow model provider can starve capacity for completely unrelated traffic.

Interview Gold Point: Explicitly separating "AI-call concurrency" from "general request concurrency" as two independently tunable pools is the kind of distinction that separates candidates who have actually run this in production from those reciting Little's Law from a textbook.
MIDQ21. Describe how you would configure a shared WebClient (or RestClient) connection pool for calls to a model provider, and what specific pool parameters matter.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Configure a single, application-scoped ConnectionProvider (for Reactor Netty, underlying WebClient) with an explicit maximum connection count sized to your Little's Law concurrency target, a pending-acquire timeout so requests fail fast rather than hang indefinitely waiting for a free connection, and an idle connection eviction time so stale connections don't accumulate. Inject this single client/provider everywhere in the application rather than letting each component build its own — a per-component client silently multiplies your effective concurrency ceiling and makes the provider's own rate limit far easier to exceed without noticing.

MIDQ22. What is the difference between reactive backpressure and simply queuing requests, and why does it matter when streaming to slow clients?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Queuing accepts work faster than it can be processed and holds the excess in memory, which under sustained overload eventually exhausts memory or grows latency unboundedly. Backpressure is a signaling mechanism where the consumer (here, a slow client reading a streamed response) tells the producer how much it can currently accept, and the producer (Reactor's Flux) throttles emission to match — it does not buffer unboundedly by default. For token streaming, this matters because a slow client (poor network, backgrounded tab) should not cause the server to buffer an ever-growing number of unread tokens in memory across many concurrent streams.

MIDQ23. How would you implement conversation-history windowing in Spring AI to bound prompt growth, and what are the trade-offs of a token-count-based window versus a fixed-turn-count window?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Implement it as a memory Advisor (or a custom step before prompt assembly) that retrieves the full stored conversation but only includes the most recent N turns, or the most recent turns that fit under a token budget, when assembling the prompt — often combined with a running summary of older turns so context is not simply discarded. A fixed-turn-count window is simple and predictable but can wildly over- or under-shoot your actual token budget depending on how verbose each turn is. A token-count-based window directly targets what you actually care about (cost and prefill latency) but requires accurate token counting (Q28) and slightly more bookkeeping to implement correctly.

MIDQ24. How does embedding caching differ from response caching, and where in a Spring AI RAG pipeline would you add it?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Response caching (Section 4.4) stores the final generated answer keyed on the input. Embedding caching stores the vector representation of a piece of text, keyed on that text, so that if the same query or the same document chunk needs to be embedded again you avoid a redundant embedding-model call. It sits earlier in the pipeline — before retrieval, at the point where a user query or a document chunk is converted to a vector for similarity search — and is especially valuable because embedding calls are frequent (every query, every ingested chunk) even though each individual call is usually cheap and fast on its own.

MIDQ25. How would you implement a semantic cache in Spring using an embedding similarity search, and what threshold-tuning risk do you need to manage?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

On each incoming request, embed the query, search a vector store of previously answered queries (scoped per user/tenant per Section 13) for the nearest neighbor, and if its similarity score exceeds a chosen threshold, return the cached answer instead of calling the model; otherwise call the model and store the new query/answer/embedding triple. The core risk is threshold tuning: set it too low and semantically different questions get treated as equivalent, returning a confidently wrong cached answer with no indication anything went wrong; set it too high and the cache rarely hits, undermining the whole point. The threshold should be validated against a labeled set of near-duplicate and genuinely-different query pairs, not picked by feel.

MIDQ26. What Resilience4j components would you combine around a ChatClient call, and what does each protect against?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Typically all four together: a TimeLimiter bounds how long you'll wait for a single call, protecting against a hung or abnormally slow provider response; a CircuitBreaker tracks the failure/timeout rate and "opens" to fail fast without even attempting calls once the provider looks unhealthy, protecting your own threads and the provider from a pile-up of doomed retries during an outage; a Retry handles transient, recoverable failures (a single dropped connection) with backoff, protecting against noise-level flakiness; and a Bulkhead caps how many concurrent calls to that dependency are allowed at all, protecting the rest of your application from one slow dependency consuming unbounded resources. Each solves a different failure mode and they compose rather than substitute for one another.

Interview Gold Point: Explicitly stating that these four solve different failure modes — not that any one of them "handles resilience" — is what separates a candidate who has configured Resilience4j from one who has only read about it.
MIDQ27. How does parallelizing independent Advisor pre-call work (e.g., memory read and RAG retrieval) reduce latency, and how would you implement that in Spring?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

If memory retrieval and RAG lookup are independent of each other (neither needs the other's output), running them sequentially adds their latencies together (Section 5.2's example: 80ms + 80ms = 160ms), while running them concurrently costs only the slower of the two. In Spring, this is implemented with CompletableFuture.supplyAsync for each independent step, combined via CompletableFuture.allOf(...) (or the reactive equivalent, Mono.zip) before prompt assembly proceeds — turning latency-additive sequential I/O into latency-bound-by-the-slowest-branch concurrent I/O, at the cost of slightly more complex error handling (Q71).

MIDQ28. Why is counting tokens accurately (rather than estimating from character count) important, and what tool would you use in a Java application?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Character count is a rough, unreliable proxy for token count because tokenization is not a fixed ratio — it varies by language, by whether text is mostly common words or unusual identifiers/code, and by the specific tokenizer a model uses. Relying on a character-based estimate for cost guardrails, context-window checks, or budget enforcement (Q29, Q72) can silently under- or over-estimate real usage by a wide margin. In Java, libraries like jtokkit implement the actual BPE tokenizers used by major providers, letting you compute an accurate token count locally before a request is even sent.

MIDQ29. How would you build a pre-call cost guardrail that rejects or truncates a request before it reaches the model provider?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

After prompt assembly but before the model call, run the assembled prompt through an accurate tokenizer (Q28) and compare the resulting count (plus the requested maxTokens, to estimate worst-case output cost) against a configured budget — per request, and optionally a rolling per-user/per-tenant budget. If it exceeds the limit, either truncate the least-important part of the prompt (typically older conversation history first, per Q23's windowing) and proceed, or reject the request with a clear error, depending on the feature's tolerance for silent truncation. This turns an open-ended cost risk into an enforced, observable ceiling rather than something discovered on the monthly invoice.

MIDQ30. What extra latency does requesting structured output (e.g., via BeanOutputConverter / JSON schema) typically add, and where does it come from?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Two sources. First, the format instructions or JSON schema injected into the prompt add input tokens, which adds a small amount to prefill time. Second, and usually larger, is validation-and-retry overhead: if the model's output fails to parse against the expected schema, a well-built structured-output flow (Chapter on Structured Output) re-prompts the model to correct its output, which means an entire additional decode phase — potentially doubling total latency for that request. This is why p99 latency for structured-output endpoints is often disproportionately worse than p50 (Q78): the tail is dominated by the retry cases, not the median well-formed response.

MIDQ31. Explain why a tool-calling (function-calling) interaction with an LLM often costs more in latency than a plain chat completion, using the request lifecycle.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

A tool-calling interaction is not one prefill/decode round trip but potentially several: the model first generates a tool-call request (one decode pass), your application executes the tool and its own latency is added, the tool's result is appended to the conversation and sent back to the model as new input (a second prefill, now processing a longer context that includes the tool result), and the model then generates its final answer (a second decode pass). Each additional tool call in a chain multiplies this round trip. This compounding is why tool-heavy agentic flows (Chapter on Agent Fundamentals) typically have much higher and more variable latency than single-turn chat, and why minimizing unnecessary tool calls is itself a performance lever.

MIDQ32. How does adding a reranking step to a RAG pipeline affect end-to-end latency, and how would you decide whether it's worth it?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Reranking adds a distinct, sequential step between initial retrieval and prompt assembly — typically a call to a cross-encoder model that scores each candidate document against the query, which is slower per-document than the vector similarity search that produced the candidates. This adds tens to low-hundreds of milliseconds depending on candidate count and reranker size, entirely before the main model is even called. Whether it's worth it is an evaluation question, not a latency question in isolation: measure answer quality (via your evaluation suite, Chapter 39) with and without reranking, and only keep it if the quality gain is large enough to justify the added latency for that specific feature's latency budget.

Interview Gold Point: Framing "is it worth it" as an evaluation-driven trade-off rather than a blanket "reranking is good practice" answer shows you treat every added pipeline step as a cost that must earn its place, which is exactly the discipline this chapter is built around.
MIDQ33. How does chunk size choice in a RAG pipeline affect both retrieval latency and downstream prompt/token cost?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Smaller chunks mean more total chunks in the vector store, which can mean a larger index to search (a modest retrieval-latency effect) but, more importantly, each individually retrieved chunk contributes fewer tokens to the final prompt — so retrieving a fixed number of top-K chunks costs less in prefill tokens with small chunks than with large ones. Larger chunks retrieve richer context per chunk but consume more of your token budget per chunk retrieved, and once you multiply by top-K, the assembled context can balloon well past what's actually needed to answer the question, directly increasing both cost and prefill latency (Chapter on Chunking covers the retrieval-quality side of this trade-off).

MIDQ34. Why does connection keep-alive matter for calls to a model provider's REST API, and what happens if it's disabled?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Keep-alive lets a single TCP/TLS connection be reused across multiple sequential HTTP requests instead of tearing it down and re-establishing it each time. If disabled (or if a proxy/load balancer in the path strips it), every model call pays the full connection-establishment cost — TCP handshake plus TLS negotiation — on top of the actual request latency, which for a provider that may be many milliseconds of network round trip away can add a meaningful, entirely avoidable tax to every single call. Spring's pooled HTTP clients (Q11) keep connections alive by default; this is usually a misconfiguration to watch for rather than something to build.

MIDQ35. What benefit does HTTP/2 multiplexing offer for an application that makes many concurrent model calls, and does Spring AI's default HTTP client support it?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

HTTP/1.1 allows only one in-flight request per connection at a time (without pipelining, which is rarely used in practice), so high concurrency requires many separate connections. HTTP/2 multiplexes many concurrent request/response streams over a single connection, reducing the number of connections — and the associated handshake and resource overhead — needed to sustain high concurrency to the same host. Reactor Netty (which backs Spring's reactive WebClient) supports HTTP/2, and most major model providers' APIs support it as well, so enabling it where available is a low-effort way to reduce connection overhead under high concurrent load, though the actual model-side latency is unaffected.

MIDQ36. What is JVM warm-up / JIT compilation, and why might your first few requests after deployment be measurably slower even though the model call time is unchanged?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

The JVM initially interprets bytecode and only compiles frequently-executed ("hot") code paths into optimized native machine code after observing them run repeatedly (JIT — Just-In-Time compilation). Immediately after a fresh deployment, none of your request-handling, Advisor, or serialization code has been JIT-optimized yet, so the in-process portions of the request lifecycle (steps 1–5 and 8–10 in Section 5.1) run measurably slower for the first requests, even though the model call itself — which happens entirely outside the JVM — is unaffected. This "cold start" effect (distinct from provider-side cold starts, Q69) is usually small relative to total request latency but can matter for aggressive p99 SLOs immediately post-deploy.

MIDQ37. Why should logging and Advisor post-processing be made asynchronous rather than run inline before returning the response to the client?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Post-call work like persisting the new conversation turn to memory storage, writing detailed logs, or updating metrics does not need to complete before the client receives their answer — the user only cares about the model's response, not your bookkeeping. Running it inline (Section 5.1, step 9) adds its full latency to what the client experiences. Dispatching it to a background executor (or a message queue for durability) after the response has been sent lets the client see the answer as soon as it's ready, while the housekeeping work completes independently — provided you accept the durability trade-off that a crash between response and background persistence could lose that turn.

MIDQ38. What is an idempotency key, and why does it matter when you add automatic retries around a model call?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

An idempotency key is a unique identifier attached to a logical request so that if it is retried (by Resilience4j's Retry, or by a client-side retry after a timeout), the receiving system can recognize the retry as "the same request" rather than a brand-new one. For LLM calls this matters most on the application side of the boundary — if your own code interprets an ambiguous timeout as a failure and retries, but the original call actually succeeded and you already billed or persisted its result, an idempotency key on your own request-tracking layer prevents double-charging a user's budget or double-writing a conversation turn, even though the provider itself may not offer true request idempotency.

MIDQ39. What happens to an in-flight streaming model call if the client disconnects mid-stream, and how would you make sure you don't keep paying for tokens the client never receives?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

If nothing is done, the server may continue consuming the upstream Flux from the model provider — and the provider may continue generating and billing tokens — even after the downstream client has gone away, because the disconnect is not automatically propagated upstream. In Reactor, a subscriber's cancellation signal should propagate back through the reactive chain automatically if the pipeline is built correctly (no operators that eagerly buffer or that isolate the upstream from downstream cancellation), which cancels the subscription to the provider's stream. It's worth explicitly testing this behavior — a disconnected client silently leaving a generation running to completion server-side is a real, easy-to-miss cost leak in streaming implementations.

MIDQ40. How would you load-test a Spring AI endpoint in a way that produces a meaningful throughput ceiling number, rather than just a single-request latency number?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Ramp concurrent virtual users gradually (rather than a single fixed concurrency level) while recording p50/p95/p99 latency, error rate, and provider rate-limit (429) rate at each step. The throughput ceiling is the point where latency starts climbing sharply (queueing effects) or error/429 rate starts rising — not simply "requests per second at whatever concurrency you happened to test." Critically, this test must be run against the real (or a realistic sandbox of the) model provider, not a mocked fast stub, because the provider's own queueing and rate-limit behavior under load is usually the actual ceiling (Section 12), and a mock will never reveal it.

MIDQ41. What is a reasonable way to define an SLA/SLO for a streaming chat endpoint, given that "total latency" is a less meaningful number than for a typical REST API?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Define separate SLOs for the two phases users actually experience differently: a time-to-first-token target (e.g., p95 TTFT under 800ms) that governs perceived responsiveness, and a streaming-rate or time-to-last-token target that governs how quickly the full answer completes once it starts. A single blended "total latency" SLO obscures which phase is actually degrading — a regression in prefill (queue delay, prompt bloat) looks completely different operationally from a regression in decode (verbose responses, an overloaded provider), and conflating them into one number makes the alert far less actionable for on-call engineers trying to diagnose it.

MIDQ42. Should you cache a model error response? Explain the risk if you do it carelessly.

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Caching error responses can be useful briefly — a short-lived negative cache prevents a burst of identical requests from all retrying against a provider that is currently rate-limiting or erroring, reducing pointless load during an incident. Done carelessly, though, it risks caching a transient failure as if it were a permanent one: a request that would have succeeded on the very next attempt keeps returning a stale cached error to every user for the cache's full TTL. If you implement it at all, keep the TTL very short (seconds, not minutes), scope it narrowly to the specific failure type, and never apply it to input-validation-style errors that are actually the caller's fault and won't change on retry.

MIDQ43. What does it mean to "multiplex" across multiple API keys or provider accounts, and when would that be a legitimate performance technique rather than a hack?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

It means routing requests across more than one API key or account for the same provider — each with its own independent rate-limit ceiling — so that aggregate throughput is the sum of each account's limit rather than being capped by a single account. It is a legitimate technique when your genuine sustained traffic has outgrown a single account's contracted rate limit and the provider either doesn't offer a higher tier or the higher tier isn't yet justified, and when done transparently and within the provider's terms of service. It becomes a hack (and a fragile one) when used to evade fair-use limits rather than to serve genuine scaled traffic, or when the provider's terms explicitly prohibit it.

MIDQ44. Why can adding too many labels (e.g., per-user ID) to a Micrometer metric become a performance and operational problem of its own?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Each unique combination of label values creates a distinct time series in the underlying metrics backend. A label with high cardinality — like a raw user ID, applied to a metric that already has several other dimensions — can multiply the number of time series into the millions, which slows down or overwhelms the metrics storage/query system and can itself become a memory and performance liability inside the application (Micrometer meters accumulate in memory per unique tag combination). The fix is to keep high-cardinality identifiers out of metric tags entirely — use them in structured logs or traces instead, where per-event cardinality is expected and handled differently — and reserve metric tags for low-cardinality dimensions like feature name, model name, or tenant tier.

Interview Gold Point: Distinguishing where high-cardinality identifiers belong — logs and traces, not metric tags — shows you understand observability infrastructure trade-offs beyond just "add more instrumentation," which is a common blind spot even among engineers who instrument diligently.
MIDQ45. How would you structure an A/B test comparing two different models (or model versions) for a given feature, in terms of what you'd measure?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Split live traffic (or replay a representative sample) between the two models under otherwise identical conditions — same prompts, same Advisor chain, same caching behavior — and measure, per variant: the evaluation-suite quality score (Chapter 39) so you can detect a quality regression, alongside p50/p95 TTFT and TTLT, average and p95 token counts (input and output), and cost per request. The decision is rarely made on a single axis — a model that is 20% cheaper but produces meaningfully lower evaluation scores is not a win, and the same reasoning applies in reverse for a faster-but-lower-quality option; report all axes together rather than picking the winner on latency or cost alone.

MIDQ46. What is "progressive enhancement" in the context of model fallback — describe a pattern where a slow primary model degrades gracefully instead of failing.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Rather than a binary success/failure around a single model call, wrap the primary (higher-quality, typically slower) model call with a tight timeout, and on timeout — rather than surfacing an error to the user — fall back to a faster, cheaper secondary model to produce a "good enough" answer within the user's latency budget. This is the model-selection analog of Section 9.4's circuit-breaker fallback: the user gets a timely, if slightly lower-quality, response instead of a spinner or an error, and you can log the fallback event to track how often it triggers as a signal that your primary model's latency or the timeout budget needs attention.

MIDQ47. Why might debouncing or throttling client-side requests (e.g., in a chat UI with an autocomplete-style feature) be a legitimate performance optimization, even though it happens outside your Spring backend?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

If a UI feature triggers a model call on every keystroke or every minor state change, most of those calls are wasted — the user hasn't finished typing, and only the final state actually matters. Debouncing (waiting for a pause in input before firing the request) or throttling (capping request rate regardless of input frequency) on the client eliminates these wasted calls before they ever reach your backend, directly reducing token cost and provider-side load with zero server-side engineering. It's worth calling out in a performance discussion because engineers often only look for optimizations inside their own service boundary, missing that the cheapest request is the one never sent.

MIDQ48. You notice that after enabling client-side response caching in your frontend, users occasionally see answers that no longer reflect updated backend logic. What's the underlying caching problem, and how do you fix it?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

This is a cache-invalidation problem: the client cache has no way to know that the server-side prompt template, model version, or Advisor chain changed, so it keeps serving pre-change responses as if they were still valid. The fix is to version the cache key with something that changes whenever server-side behavior changes meaningfully — a prompt-template version, a model identifier, or a deploy build number — so a backend change automatically produces a cache-key miss on the client rather than relying on a TTL alone (which only bounds staleness, it doesn't eliminate it on a targeted change) or a manual cache-clearing step that is easy to forget.

SENIORQ49. Two features share the same Spring AI backend and the same model provider account. One has a strict 2-second latency SLA, the other is a background batch job with no user waiting. How would you architect concurrency and rate-limit budget allocation between them so the batch job cannot starve the latency-sensitive feature?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Treat the shared provider rate limit as a resource to be explicitly partitioned rather than left to first-come-first-served contention. Give each feature its own dedicated connection pool / bulkhead (Q53) with a hard concurrency cap, sized so that even at the batch job's maximum concurrency, enough of the provider's rate-limit headroom remains for the latency-sensitive feature's expected peak load — computed via Little's Law per feature, then summed against the account ceiling. Additionally, run the batch job at lower priority with its own token-bucket-style throttle and, ideally, prefer the provider's asynchronous batch API (Q58) for it entirely, removing it from the synchronous rate-limit pool altogether. Without this explicit partitioning, a burst in the batch job can silently eat the rate-limit budget the SLA-bound feature needs.

SENIORQ50. Your team wants to migrate a heavily-used synchronous Spring MVC AI endpoint to WebFlux purely for performance reasons. Walk through how you would decide whether that migration is actually justified versus simply adopting virtual threads.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Start from where the actual bottleneck lives: if the current problem is platform-thread exhaustion under blocking I/O, virtual threads (Q17, Section 9.3) solve that with a fraction of the engineering cost and risk of a full reactive rewrite — no change to the synchronous programming model, no need to re-learn error handling and testing patterns in Reactor, no risk of accidentally blocking an event-loop thread somewhere in a large codebase. WebFlux earns its added complexity mainly when you need reactive-specific capabilities virtual threads don't provide — fine-grained backpressure control for streaming to many slow clients, or non-blocking composition with other reactive infrastructure already in place. I would require a concrete, measured bottleneck (not a hunch) and a before/after load test target before approving a full-application rewrite of this scope, given that the model call itself, not the threading model, usually dominates single-request latency.

Interview Gold Point: Reframing "should we migrate to WebFlux" as "what specific capability does WebFlux provide that virtual threads don't" reveals you evaluate architecture migrations by the problem they solve, not by trend-following — a distinctly senior-level instinct.
SENIORQ51. Design a connection-pool sizing strategy for your HTTP client to a model provider, accounting for Little's Law, provider concurrency limits, and the number of application instances you run.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High at senior level

Compute the total desired concurrency via Little's Law (peak arrival rate × average latency) at the platform level, then divide by the number of horizontally scaled application instances to get the per-instance pool size — but cap the sum across all instances at (or comfortably below) the provider account's own concurrent-request ceiling, because provider limits are typically account-wide, not per-instance. This means per-instance pool size must be recalculated every time you scale instance count up or down; a static per-instance pool size sized for N instances silently becomes an over-commitment the moment you scale to N+k instances, which is exactly the failure mode described in Section 12 and Q86's postmortem scenario. Building this recalculation into your deployment/autoscaling process, rather than as a one-time manual tuning exercise, is the actual senior-level deliverable here.

SENIORQ52. How would you design cache invalidation for a semantic cache when the underlying system prompt or retrieved knowledge base changes?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Version every cache entry with an identifier that captures everything that affects correctness of the cached answer: a system-prompt version, and for RAG-backed answers, a content-version or last-modified marker for the knowledge base (or the specific documents that were retrieved to produce that answer). On lookup, only treat a semantic match as a valid hit if its stored version matches the current version; on a system-prompt or knowledge-base update, you can either bump a global version (effectively invalidating the entire cache at once, simple but blunt) or track document-level versions and invalidate only entries that depended on changed documents (more precise, more implementation complexity). The wrong approach — relying purely on a TTL and hoping it's short enough — trades correctness for a guess about how often your knowledge base changes, which is a bet that eventually loses.

SENIORQ53. Describe the bulkhead pattern and how you'd apply it to isolate a slow, low-priority AI feature from starving resources needed by a higher-priority one, within a single Spring Boot application.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

The bulkhead pattern, named after a ship's watertight compartments, isolates resource pools per dependency or feature so that failure or saturation in one compartment cannot flood and sink the others. In Spring, this means giving each AI-backed feature (or each downstream dependency, like a specific model tier) its own dedicated executor/thread pool and its own Resilience4j Bulkhead with an explicit max-concurrent-calls limit, rather than sharing one common pool across all AI calls in the application. If a low-priority summarization feature starts experiencing elevated latency from its model provider, its bulkhead saturates and starts rejecting or queuing new summarization requests — but the higher-priority chat feature, running in its own isolated pool, continues operating normally because it was never competing for the same threads.

SENIORQ54. How do you decide where to set a circuit breaker's failure-rate threshold and wait-duration-in-open-state for a model provider dependency, given that occasional provider slowness is normal and not always indicative of an outage?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Base both from measured baseline behavior rather than defaults: look at your historical failure/timeout rate during normal operation (which for a healthy provider dependency is rarely exactly zero) and set the failure-rate threshold meaningfully above that noise floor, so the breaker doesn't trip on ordinary variance but does trip promptly once failures spike well beyond it. The wait-duration-in-open-state should reflect how quickly provider incidents typically resolve versus how costly it is to keep failing fast during a false-positive trip — too short and you flap between open and half-open during a genuine sustained outage, hammering an already-struggling provider with the half-open probe traffic; too long and you keep failing fast for longer than necessary after the provider has actually recovered. This is a tuning exercise, best revisited after real incidents, not a one-time configuration.

SENIORQ55. Streaming a response to the client is in tension with an output-validation guardrail that needs to see the complete response. Design two different approaches to reconcile this, and state the trade-off of each.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Approach 1 — validate-then-reveal: buffer the full response server-side (not streaming to the client at all), run the guardrail check once complete, and only then send the (now-validated) response to the client — either as one payload or as a fast simulated stream. This guarantees no invalid content ever reaches the user, but sacrifices the entire perceived-latency benefit of streaming (Section 4.3), since the client waits for full TTLT regardless. Approach 2 — incremental validation: stream tokens to the client as they arrive while running lightweight, incremental checks (e.g., a fast keyword/PII scanner) on each chunk, and only fall back to buffering-and-blocking for checks that genuinely require the complete response (e.g., JSON schema validation). This preserves most of the streaming UX win but requires guardrails to be re-architected as incremental/streaming-aware, which not all guardrail types support. The right choice depends on how severe the risk of a bad response reaching the user actually is for that specific feature.

SENIORQ56. You've structured your prompts for provider-side prefix caching, but your observability shows the cache hit rate is much lower than expected. Walk through how you would debug this.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

First confirm the static prefix is genuinely byte-identical across calls — a subtle bug like a timestamp, a randomly-ordered list of few-shot examples, or a non-deterministic serialization of a Java object (e.g., unordered Map iteration when building the system prompt) can silently break byte-identity while looking identical to a casual glance. Second, confirm the static content is actually positioned first and the dynamic content strictly after it — even a small amount of dynamic content inserted before the static block breaks the prefix match entirely. Third, check whether the prefix is long enough to meet the provider's minimum cacheable-prefix length (providers often require a minimum token count before caching activates) and whether repeat calls are happening within the provider's cache TTL window (prefix caches typically expire after a few minutes of disuse). Reproduce with two consecutive identical calls and compare raw request bytes, not just your application's logical view of the prompt.

Interview Gold Point: Naming non-deterministic Java collection iteration order as a real-world way to accidentally break prefix identity is a specific, credible failure mode that shows hands-on debugging experience rather than textbook knowledge of caching.
SENIORQ57. Your application serves users across three continents, calling a single model provider region. How would you think about the latency implications of this, and what architectural options exist to mitigate it, while staying within the scope of application-level (not infrastructure/k8s-level) decisions?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium

Cross-continent network round trip adds fixed latency to every leg of the request (client-to-app and app-to-provider) that no amount of application tuning removes — it's speed-of-light-bound. At the application level, the levers available are: minimizing the number of sequential round trips per logical request (batching tool calls, parallelizing independent Advisor steps per Q27, avoiding unnecessary multi-turn tool-calling chains per Q31) so the fixed per-round-trip latency tax is paid as few times as possible; leaning harder on streaming (Section 4.3) so users perceive responsiveness sooner even though absolute network latency is unchanged; and, if the provider offers multiple regions, routing requests to whichever region is geographically closer to the calling user at the application/request-routing layer. True mitigation of the physical distance itself — deploying application instances regionally close to users and to provider regions — is an infrastructure/deployment decision outside this chapter's scope.

SENIORQ58. Several providers offer an asynchronous "batch API" at a reduced price for non-time-sensitive requests. Describe when you would route work to a batch API versus the normal synchronous endpoint, and what it costs you in latency.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Batch APIs trade turnaround time — typically results are returned within a window of hours rather than seconds — for a substantial per-token discount, because they let the provider schedule the work flexibly against its own spare capacity rather than serving it with the same urgency as live traffic. Route work here when there is no user actively waiting synchronously: overnight document summarization, bulk classification of a backlog, offline evaluation runs, or periodic report generation are strong candidates. It is a poor fit for anything in a user-facing request path, obviously, but also for anything with even a soft "within a few minutes" expectation. Architecturally this requires your application to be built around submit-then-poll (or webhook-callback) rather than request-response, which is a real integration cost, not just a configuration flag.

SENIORQ59. What is speculative decoding, and why, even though you cannot implement it yourself as a Spring AI application developer, might it still influence which model or provider you choose for a latency-critical feature?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Speculative decoding (introduced in Section 2.3) uses a small, fast "draft" model to guess several tokens ahead, which the larger target model then verifies in a single forward pass instead of generating each of those tokens sequentially — when the draft's guesses are correct, this can meaningfully cut decode-phase latency without changing output quality, because the larger model is still the one that determines the final tokens. You cannot turn this on yourself, but it's relevant to model/provider selection because some providers and some model configurations benefit from it more than others (it works best when the draft model's outputs are frequently correct, i.e., for lower-entropy generations), so two providers offering "the same model" can have measurably different real-world decode latency — a reason to benchmark actual TTLT per provider rather than assuming model name alone determines speed.

SENIORQ60. A very long, multi-document RAG context is causing noticeably higher latency and JVM memory pressure in your Spring application, even before the request reaches the model. Explain what's happening and how you'd address both the request-shape and JVM-level symptoms.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium

On the request-shape side: assembling a very large prompt from many retrieved documents means significant in-process string concatenation/formatting work (Section 5.1, step 4) before the model is even called, and a larger prompt directly increases prefill latency once it is sent. Address this by tightening retrieval (fewer, more relevant chunks via reranking, Q32, or smaller chunks, Q33) rather than sending everything retrieved. On the JVM side: many concurrent requests each holding large `String`/`StringBuilder` buffers for prompt assembly, plus the retrieved-document objects themselves, increases heap allocation rate and can increase GC pause frequency under load — worth confirming with GC logs/metrics before assuming it's the model call itself. If large contexts are a legitimate, frequent requirement for the feature (not just an unbounded retrieval bug), consider raising heap size and tuning for allocation-heavy, short-lived object patterns (a generational collector like G1 or ZGC handles this workload well), but treat that as a mitigation for a legitimate need, not a substitute for fixing an oversized retrieval in the first place.

SENIORQ61. Design the observability dashboard you would build for a staff+ engineering review of an AI feature's performance — what are the specific panels, and why did you choose them over other candidates?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Core panels: TTFT and TTLT as p50/p95/p99 line charts over time (never averages, per Section 11); input and output token count distributions, separated, because they have different cost weight and different root causes when they spike; cache hit rate broken out by layer (exact/semantic/provider-prefix); provider error and 429 rate; and cost-per-day rolled up by feature, with a rolling baseline band so a deviation is visually obvious rather than requiring mental math. I'd deliberately exclude a single blended "average latency" number as the headline metric — it actively hides the tail behavior that causes real user pain — and I'd avoid over-indexing on request-count-per-second alone, since for AI workloads token throughput is usually the more meaningful capacity signal than raw request count, given how widely token count varies per request.

SENIORQ62. How would you set a practical SLO for time-to-first-token versus time-to-last-token for a customer support chat assistant, and what would you do differently for a document-summarization batch feature on the same platform?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

For the chat assistant, TTFT is the user-facing responsiveness metric and deserves a tight SLO (e.g., p95 under 1 second) because the user is actively watching and waiting; TTLT matters less in absolute terms once streaming makes the wait feel productive, though an extreme TTLT tail (a runaway generation) still deserves a cap via maxTokens and a sane overall timeout. For the document-summarization batch feature, there is no live user watching a specific request, so neither TTFT nor per-request TTLT deserves a tight SLO at all — the meaningful SLO shifts to an aggregate one: total batch completion time for a submitted set of documents, and a cost-per-document ceiling. Applying a chat-style TTFT SLO to a batch feature would be over-engineering the wrong dimension; applying a batch-style aggregate SLO to a live chat feature would leave users staring at a spinner with no accountability.

Interview Gold Point: Explicitly stating that the same metric (TTFT) deserves a tight SLO in one feature and essentially no SLO in another demonstrates that you tie performance targets to actual user experience rather than applying one performance philosophy uniformly across an entire platform.
SENIORQ63. Under a traffic spike, your provider starts returning HTTP 429s intermittently. Design a load-shedding strategy at your Spring Boot application layer that protects both your own infrastructure and your relationship with the provider (avoiding a full account-level throttle).

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Add a client-side rate limiter (e.g., Resilience4j's RateLimiter, or a token-bucket implementation) configured slightly below the provider's known ceiling, so your application self-throttles before consistently hitting 429s rather than reactively backing off after the fact — reactive backoff-on-429 alone tends to produce oscillating, bursty traffic that makes the situation worse. When incoming demand exceeds that self-imposed ceiling, shed load deliberately and cheaply at the edge: reject or queue-with-a-cap lower-priority requests (per the bulkhead/priority partitioning in Q49 and Q53) before they ever reach the rate limiter, returning a fast, clear "temporarily unavailable, retry shortly" response rather than letting them queue silently and time out expensively later. This protects the provider relationship (steady, predictable request rate rather than bursty overshoot) and your own thread/connection pools (rejected requests release resources immediately instead of tying them up in a doomed retry loop).

SENIORQ64. Describe how you would implement a request queue at your API gateway or application layer to smooth bursty traffic before it reaches the model provider, and what latency cost this introduces.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium

Introduce a bounded queue (in-memory for a single instance, or a distributed queue like Redis-backed or a message broker for multi-instance coordination) between request acceptance and the actual model call, with a fixed worker concurrency draining it at a rate matched to your Little's Law-derived sustainable throughput. Bursts above that sustainable rate accumulate in the queue rather than immediately overwhelming the provider or your connection pool, smoothing the arrival pattern the provider actually sees. The direct cost is added queueing latency for any request arriving during a burst — which must be weighed against the feature's latency SLO; a queue depth cap plus fast-fail once the queue itself is full (rather than an unbounded queue) is essential so queueing latency has a hard ceiling instead of growing unboundedly during a sustained overload.

SENIORQ65. You support multiple tenants on a shared Spring AI platform. How would you attribute and report token cost per tenant, and what would you do if one tenant's usage pattern threatens to degrade latency for others?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Attribute cost by tagging every model call with a tenant identifier at the point of the call (not reconstructed after the fact from logs), capturing input/output token counts per call, and aggregating into a per-tenant, per-day cost rollup — the same token-and-dollar observability from Section 8's production architecture, but with tenant as an explicit dimension rather than an afterthought. If one tenant's usage pattern (very high volume, very long conversations, or unusually large documents) threatens to degrade shared latency for others, apply per-tenant concurrency and rate budgets (an extension of the bulkhead pattern, Q53, partitioned by tenant rather than by feature) so that tenant's load is capped independently of the shared pool, and consider a tiered pricing/SLA model (Q85) where higher usage is either priced accordingly or routed to dedicated capacity rather than silently degrading the shared pool for everyone.

SENIORQ66. How would you add a performance regression test to CI that fails the build if a code change increases average token usage or latency for a fixed set of representative prompts?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium

Maintain a small, fixed "golden set" of representative prompts (covering the feature's realistic input distribution) and run them against a low-temperature or mocked-but-realistic model configuration as part of CI, recording input tokens, output tokens, and measured latency for each. Compare against a stored baseline (checked into the repo or a metrics store) and fail the build if any metric regresses beyond a defined tolerance — e.g., average output tokens increasing more than 15%, which would usually indicate an accidental prompt change that removed a conciseness instruction, or a new Advisor step silently adding unbounded context. This is genuinely tricky to make reliable against a live, non-deterministic model provider, so many teams run it against a cheaper/faster model tier specifically for this test, or mock the model call and only assert on prompt-construction-side token counts, treating true end-to-end latency regression testing as a separate, less frequent load-test exercise (Q40) rather than a per-PR CI gate.

SENIORQ67. Compare, with concrete reasoning, the maximum sustainable throughput you'd expect from a synchronous blocking implementation, a CompletableFuture-based async implementation, and a full WebFlux reactive implementation of the same Spring AI endpoint, assuming the model call itself dominates latency in all three.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

With a small, fixed platform-thread pool, the synchronous blocking implementation's throughput ceiling is bounded tightly by pool size divided by average call latency (Little's Law), because every in-flight request permanently occupies a thread. The CompletableFuture-async implementation on the same platform-thread infrastructure releases the request thread quickly but still needs enough executor threads to hold the in-flight work somewhere — so it raises the ceiling versus naive blocking (by decoupling request-thread count from in-flight-call count) but the executor pool still needs sizing via the same Little's Law math. WebFlux (or virtual threads, Q17) removes the platform-thread-per-in-flight-call constraint almost entirely, so its throughput ceiling shifts to whatever the actual bottleneck becomes — nearly always the model provider's own rate limit, not your application's thread infrastructure. In practice, once you've adopted virtual threads or a properly-sized async executor, the meaningful throughput difference between that and a full WebFlux rewrite is usually small, because both have already moved the bottleneck off your application layer and onto the provider — which is the more important point to make than picking a "winner" between the three.

SENIORQ68. How would you allocate a fixed request-level timeout budget across a multi-step Advisor chain (memory read, RAG retrieval, guardrail check, model call) so that no single step can silently consume the whole budget?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Start from the feature's overall latency SLO and work backward, assigning each step an explicit sub-timeout that sums to comfortably less than the total budget (leaving headroom for network and serialization overhead not attributable to any single step). Steps that can run in parallel (memory read and RAG retrieval, per Q27) share the same slice of the budget rather than each getting a full independent allocation, since their combined cost is bound by the slower of the two, not their sum. The model call itself — usually the dominant, most variable-latency step — should get the largest and most carefully-tuned timeout, set from measured p95/p99 (Section 9.4), with everything else deliberately kept tight since they are typically fast, in-process, or well-bounded operations by comparison. Without this explicit per-step budgeting, a single misbehaving Advisor (a slow, unbounded database read) can silently consume the entire request timeout with nothing left for the model call itself to even attempt.

SENIORQ69. What is "cold start" in the context of a Spring AI application (as distinct from the model provider itself), and what application-level techniques reduce its impact on the first requests after deployment?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Application-side cold start covers everything that makes the first requests after a fresh instance start (or deployment) slower than steady state: JVM class loading and JIT warm-up (Q36), lazily-initialized Spring beans and connection pools that haven't yet established their first connections, and empty local caches (exact-match/semantic caches, Q24–Q25) that haven't yet accumulated any hits. Application-level mitigations include eager bean initialization for critical infrastructure (HTTP clients, connection pools) rather than lazy-on-first-use, a startup health check that only marks the instance ready for traffic after a synthetic warm-up request has run through the full pipeline once, and — where your deployment process supports it — routing a small trickle of real traffic to a new instance before shifting full load onto it. None of these eliminate the provider's own cold-start behavior (queueing, model loading on the provider side), which is outside your application's control.

SENIORQ70. Design a client-side (browser or mobile) caching strategy that complements your server-side caching, and explain what class of requests it helps with that server-side caching alone does not.

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Client-side caching (local storage of recent responses, keyed similarly to your server-side exact-match cache) helps with a class of repetition server-side caching cannot address at all: requests that never leave the client, such as a user re-viewing a previous answer by scrolling back, or a UI re-rendering the same content on a route change. It also eliminates the network round trip entirely for those cases, which server-side caching — however fast — cannot do, since server-side caching still pays client-to-server latency even on a cache hit. The two are complementary rather than redundant: client-side cache handles "the same user revisiting the same thing" cheaply and instantly, while server-side caching (exact-match and semantic) handles "any user asking an equivalent question," which a per-client cache structurally cannot capture.

SENIORQ71. How would you parallelize independent Advisor pre-call work using CompletableFuture.allOf (or a reactive equivalent), and what failure-handling subtlety do you need to get right so one slow or failing branch doesn't silently corrupt the combined result?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Launch each independent step (memory read, RAG retrieval) as its own CompletableFuture, combine with CompletableFuture.allOf(future1, future2), and then call .join() on each individual future once allOf completes to retrieve results with proper exception propagation — a common mistake is calling allOf(...).join() alone and assuming it surfaces individual failures clearly, when in practice you still need to inspect each underlying future to know which one failed and why. The subtlety to get right is deciding, per branch, whether a failure should fail the whole request or degrade gracefully: a failed RAG retrieval might reasonably fall back to answering without retrieved context (degraded but usable) while a failed memory read might need to fail the request outright if conversation continuity is essential to correctness — treating every branch failure identically (either always-fail or always-silently-ignore) is usually wrong for at least one of the branches involved.

SENIORQ72. Design a strategy for enforcing a per-user or per-tenant token budget in real time, before a request is sent to the model provider, without adding significant latency to every request.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Maintain a fast, in-memory-or-Redis running counter of tokens consumed per user/tenant within the current budget window (e.g., a sliding or fixed daily window), incremented asynchronously after each call completes rather than requiring a synchronous read-modify-write on the hot path for every request. Before sending a request, do a cheap, low-latency read of the current counter (a single Redis GET, sub-millisecond) against the budget ceiling; only if the user is near or over budget do you need to do anything more expensive, like a precise recomputation or a hard rejection. This keeps the common case (well within budget) essentially free while still providing a real-time enforcement point, accepting a small amount of eventual-consistency slack (a user could marginally exceed budget in the brief window between an async increment and the next check) as a reasonable trade-off against adding synchronous accounting overhead to every single request.

SENIORQ73. What are the practical latency and cost trade-offs of self-hosting a smaller open-weight model versus calling a hosted provider API, and under what traffic/cost profile does self-hosting typically start to make sense? (Keep this to the application-level decision, not the Kubernetes/infra deployment mechanics.)

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A hosted provider API gives you pay-per-token pricing with no fixed cost, professionally-optimized serving (batching, KV-caching, speculative decoding — Section 2.3), and zero operational burden, at the cost of per-token pricing that scales linearly with volume forever and latency that includes network round trip to the provider's infrastructure. Self-hosting a smaller open-weight model trades a large fixed cost (GPU capacity, whether owned or reserved) for a near-zero marginal cost per additional request once that capacity is provisioned, and can offer lower latency if colocated near your application — but you inherit responsibility for serving-stack performance (batching, quantization) that a hosted provider gives you for free. As a rough rule of thumb, self-hosting starts to become economically attractive once your sustained token volume is high and consistent enough that the fixed infrastructure cost, amortized per token, undercuts the hosted provider's per-token price — which in practice usually means very high, steady traffic, not spiky or low-volume workloads, since idle self-hosted GPU capacity is pure sunk cost.

SENIORQ74. How would you design an embedding-generation pipeline to batch and cache embeddings efficiently for a large, mostly-static document corpus, avoiding redundant embedding calls?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

At ingestion time, chunk the corpus once (Chapter on Chunking), then submit chunks to the embedding provider in batches rather than one call per chunk — most embedding APIs accept an array of inputs per request, and batching amortizes fixed per-request overhead across many chunks, sharply reducing total ingestion time and often cost. Store each chunk's content hash alongside its embedding, so re-ingestion of a mostly-unchanged corpus (a common re-index scenario) can skip re-embedding any chunk whose hash matches what's already stored — only genuinely new or changed content needs a fresh embedding call. For the query side, add an embedding cache (Q24) keyed on the normalized query text, since many real query distributions have significant repetition (common questions asked by many different users) that a query-embedding cache captures cheaply. The combination — batch at ingestion, hash-skip on re-ingestion, cache on the query path — addresses the three distinct sources of redundant embedding calls in a typical RAG system.

Interview Gold Point: Separating the three distinct redundancy sources — bulk ingestion, re-ingestion of unchanged content, and repeated queries — rather than proposing one generic "add a cache" answer demonstrates you've actually operated an embedding pipeline at meaningful scale.
SENIORQ75. Explain the "denial-of-wallet" risk in concrete terms and design at least three independent, layered controls that would prevent a single abusive client from running up an unbounded bill.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Denial-of-wallet (Section 13) is the cost-based analog of denial-of-service: because every token is billed, a client who can submit arbitrarily long input or repeatedly trigger expensive generations can deliberately or accidentally drive spend far beyond expected levels, with no service disruption to signal the problem until the invoice arrives. Layered controls, each independently sufficient to catch different attack shapes: (1) an input length cap enforced before prompt assembly, rejecting requests whose input token count exceeds a sane per-request maximum; (2) a hard maxTokens output cap on every call, bounding the most expensive phase per request regardless of input; (3) a per-user/per-tenant rate limit (requests per minute) independent of token budget, catching rapid-fire abuse even when each individual request is within token limits; and (4) the real-time token budget enforcement from Q72, catching sustained-but-moderate abuse that individually-reasonable rate limits would miss. No single control catches every abuse pattern — a client sending maximum-length inputs at a moderate rate defeats a rate limit alone but not a token budget, which is exactly why layering matters here.

SENIORQ76. Your team is debating whether to route "easy" requests to a small model automatically using a lightweight classifier versus always asking the user which mode they want. Discuss the performance and cost implications of each approach.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium

Automatic classifier-based routing adds a small, fast, cheap extra step before the main model call (Section 2's continuous-batching-style thinking applied at the application layer) but removes the routing decision from the user entirely, meaning every request benefits from cost-aware routing without any user friction — the trade-off is the classifier's own latency and error rate, since a misclassified "hard" request routed to the cheap model produces a real quality regression the user experiences with no warning. User-selected mode shifts the decision (and its consequences) explicitly to the user, avoiding classifier misrouting risk entirely, but only helps with cost/latency for the subset of users who both understand the trade-off and bother to select the cheaper mode — in practice, usually a minority, meaning the aggregate cost savings across the whole user base tend to be far smaller than with automatic routing. A well-evaluated automatic classifier (validated against your evaluation suite for misrouting rate, per Chapter 39) usually delivers materially more aggregate savings; user-selected mode is simpler to reason about and ship faster, and avoids the classifier becoming a new source of quality bugs.

SENIORQ77. Explain why average cost-per-request is a dangerous single metric to report to finance, and what additional metric(s) you'd insist on alongside it.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Average cost-per-request hides exactly the long-tail concentration described in Section 12: a small fraction of unusually long conversations or large-document requests can account for a disproportionate share of total spend, while the average looks perfectly reasonable because most requests are cheap and short. A stakeholder tracking only the average has no visibility into whether a small number of outlier requests are quietly driving most of the bill, and no early warning if that tail starts growing (a memory-windowing bug letting conversations grow unbounded, per Section 11, would barely move the average but could dramatically move the tail). I'd insist on reporting the p95/p99 cost-per-request alongside the average, plus total monthly cost broken down by feature and — where the outlier concentration is large — a specific "top N most expensive requests" or "cost by request-size decile" view, so a growing tail is visible well before it shows up as a surprising invoice.

SENIORQ78. How does adding an output-format guardrail (e.g., re-prompting the model if its JSON output fails schema validation) affect your p99 latency specifically, more than your p50?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Most requests produce valid, schema-conforming output on the first attempt, so the median (p50) case pays only the small, roughly constant overhead of the format instructions and the validation check itself (Q30) — a modest, predictable addition. But the subset of requests whose first attempt fails validation (typically a small percentage, but rarely zero) pay for an entire second prefill-plus-decode round trip on retry, which for a normal request can mean total latency roughly doubling. Because that failure rate is concentrated in a minority of requests rather than spread evenly, it shows up almost entirely in the upper percentiles — p99 latency for a structured-output endpoint is frequently dominated by the retry-path cases even though they represent a small fraction of total traffic, which is precisely why reporting only p50 (or an average) for such an endpoint would badly understate how bad the worst-case user experience actually is.

SENIORQ79. Design a graceful-degradation strategy for what your application should return to the user when the model provider is fully unavailable, distinguishing between a short outage and a sustained one.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

For a short outage (the circuit breaker's open state, Section 9.4, expected to resolve within its wait-duration window), the fallback should be a clearly-labeled, low-friction message — "the assistant is temporarily unavailable, please try again shortly" — rather than attempting an expensive full alternative, since the outage is likely to resolve before a heavier fallback would even be worth building. For a sustained outage — the circuit breaker repeatedly failing to close, or an explicit provider status-page confirmation — escalate the fallback: route to a secondary provider or a cached/degraded-quality response mode if one exists (Q46's progressive-enhancement pattern extended to a full-provider level), and surface the degraded state to users and stakeholders explicitly rather than silently continuing to serve the bare "unavailable" message indefinitely. The key design decision is making the fallback tier depend on outage duration and confirmed severity, not treating every provider failure identically regardless of how long it's been going on.

SENIORQ80. How would you validate, before a real production launch, that your computed Little's Law concurrency requirement actually fits inside your provider contract's rate limits, and what would you do if it doesn't?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Compute the required concurrency (L = λ × W) from realistic projected peak traffic and measured average latency, then translate that into both a concurrent-request figure and a tokens-per-minute figure (since providers frequently rate-limit on both dimensions independently, and either can be the binding constraint), and compare both against the specific numbers in your provider contract or account tier — not a generic published limit, which can differ from your actual negotiated tier. Validate with an actual load test (Q40) against the real provider account at the target concurrency, not just the arithmetic, since real-world token counts and latency variance rarely match average-case math exactly. If the requirement exceeds the contracted limits, the options are: negotiate a higher tier with the provider ahead of launch (the correct first move, given lead time), reduce required concurrency by cutting per-request latency or trimming unnecessary traffic (aggressive caching, Q24–Q25, directly reduces effective request volume against the provider), or architect explicit load-shedding (Q63) so the system degrades predictably at the ceiling instead of failing unpredictably — discovering this gap during a real incident, rather than before launch, is precisely the failure mode in Q86.

STAFFQ81. Design an end-to-end latency budget for a customer-facing AI assistant serving 5,000 concurrent users, from client request to final token, allocating a specific millisecond budget to each layer in the request lifecycle and justifying each allocation.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Working from Section 5.1's lifecycle with a target p95 TTFT of roughly 1.2 seconds: client-to-app network and auth/rate-limiting together get ~50ms (cheap, in-process or single-hop work with no excuse for more); parallelized Advisor pre-call work (memory read + RAG retrieval, run concurrently per Q27) gets a shared ~150ms budget, sized from measured p95 of the slower branch, not their sum; prompt assembly gets a negligible ~5ms (pure in-process CPU); the network hop and queueing delay to the provider gets ~100ms budget with the explicit caveat that queueing delay is the least controllable line item and needs the widest error bars; and the remainder, roughly 900ms, is allocated to provider-side prefill, which is where the bulk of the budget and the bulk of the uncertainty legitimately belongs, given Section 5.1's finding that prefill and decode dominate 70–95% of real total latency. Post-call Advisor work (persistence, logging) is deliberately excluded from this budget entirely by making it asynchronous (Q37), since it happens after the client has already received their response. At 5,000 concurrent users, this whole allocation is meaningless without the Little's Law-derived concurrency and connection-pool sizing (Q51) to actually sustain it under that load — the budget and the capacity plan are two halves of the same design, not separate exercises.

STAFFQ82. Design an organization-wide token/cost governance policy for a company running a dozen different Spring AI features across multiple teams, addressing budget attribution, alerting, and what happens when a team exceeds its allocation.

The unifying design principle, echoing Section 8, is that governance is only possible because the observability and attribution exist first — policy without accurate per-team measurement is unenforceable.

STAFFQ83. Design a multi-tier model-routing architecture (e.g., cheap/fast, mid, and premium models) with automatic fallback, and explain how you'd decide the routing rules and keep them honest against quality regression over time.

STAFFQ86. Write a brief postmortem-style analysis: your platform experienced a full-hour outage because aggregate concurrency across newly added replicas crossed the model provider's account-level rate limit, which had not been recalculated since the last capacity review. ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

STAFFQ90. Your company is considering adopting provider batch APIs at scale for a large class of non-interactive workloads to cut cost by roughly half. STAFFQ93. How would you design capacity planning across an entire portfolio of AI features sharing one provider contract, using Little's Law as a starting point but accounting for the fact that different features have very different latency and token profiles?

STAFFQ94. A new hire asks you why "just make the Java code faster" is usually the wrong first instinct when a Spring AI endpoint is slow, but insists it must matter some amount. Give a precise, defensible answer that acknowledges both truths.

Both things are true and they apply at different scales, which is exactly why the instinct misleads people. For a single request in isolation, Section 5.1 shows prefill and decode inside the provider typically consume 70–95% of total latency — your Java code genuinely is the smaller slice, so optimizing it first, before addressing output length, caching, or model choice, is optimizing the wrong 5% relative to the effort available. But "your own code" stops being small the moment it involves blocking I/O repeated across real concurrency: a slow synchronous memory-store read that costs 40ms per request is negligible for one request and can starve your entire thread pool at a thousand concurrent requests, well before the model provider itself becomes the bottleneck (Section 5.1's closing point). The precise answer, then, is that your own code's per-request latency contribution is usually small and a low-priority optimization target, while your own code's behavior under concurrency — thread pool sizing, blocking I/O patterns, connection pool configuration — can absolutely become the dominant constraint on throughput, and deserves first-class engineering attention even though it rarely shows up as the answer to "why is this one request slow."

STAFFQ95. Tie together caching, model routing, streaming, and concurrency control into a single coherent performance architecture narrative you would present to a VP of Engineering justifying six months of investment in this area — what's the throughline?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

The throughline is that all four are answers to the same underlying constraint stated in this chapter's problem statement (Section 3): the model call is external, metered, and only partially controllable, so every lever available to us either avoids calling it, shapes what we send it, changes perceived wait time, or controls how many calls compete for the same limited capacity at once. Caching (Section 4.4) is the "avoid the call entirely" lever, directly cutting both cost and latency for repeated work. Model routing (Q76, Q83) is the "call it more cheaply when the task allows" lever, matching model capability to task difficulty instead of paying premium cost uniformly. Streaming (Section 4.3) is the "make the unavoidable wait feel shorter" lever — it doesn't reduce cost, but it's often the cheapest perceived-latency win available. Concurrency control (Little's Law, connection pooling, bulkheads) is the "make sure the system survives real traffic instead of just a demo" lever, without which none of the other three matter once genuine load arrives. Presented together, the pitch to a VP is not "four separate optimizations" but one integrated claim: each lever protects a different one of the three metrics from Section 4.1 — cost, latency, and throughput — and skipping any one of them leaves a specific, predictable failure mode unaddressed, whether that's a runaway invoice, an unresponsive UI, or a production outage under load.

STAFFQ96. Looking back across this entire chapter, what is the single most common mistake you've seen senior engineers make when optimizing AI system performance, and what mental model would you give them to avoid it?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

The most common mistake is optimizing a dimension in isolation — chasing a latency number down, or a cost number down, or a throughput number up — without checking what that optimization did to the other two, or to quality. A team that aggressively trims context to cut cost and improve prefill latency can inadvertently increase effective cost and effective latency by triggering more follow-up questions and retries from a now-underinformed model (Section 4.1's warning, playing out in practice); a team that routes everything to the cheapest model to hit a cost target can quietly erode quality below what evaluation would tolerate if anyone were still checking. The mental model I'd give them is the one this whole chapter is structured around: performance optimization for AI systems is a four-dimensional trade-off — latency, throughput, cost, and quality — and a change that improves one without being measured against the other three isn't an optimization, it's a guess that happened to look good on the one number you were watching. The discipline that prevents this is simple to state and hard to maintain under deadline pressure: never ship a performance change without checking its effect on evaluation quality (Chapter 39) and at least one other dimension from this chapter, not just the one you set out to improve.

16. SUMMARY

  • Performance in AI systems is a three-way trade-off between latency, throughput, and monetary cost, entangled through the fact that the dominant, metered cost — the model call — is largely outside your direct control.
  • LLM latency splits into prefill (input-driven) and decode (output-driven, sequential, usually dominant); controlling output length is frequently a stronger lever than trimming input.
  • Streaming improves perceived latency by exposing time-to-first-token instead of time-to-last-token, but does not reduce total generation cost or compute time — the two must be optimized separately.
  • Caching at the application layer (exact-match, semantic) and shaping prompts to be provider-cache-friendly (static content first, dynamic content last) are among the highest-leverage, lowest-risk optimizations available to you.
  • Little's Law (L = λ × W) is the practical tool for sizing concurrency and thread/connection pools, and for recognizing when a provider's own rate limits — not your infrastructure — are the true throughput ceiling.
  • Every optimization in this chapter must be measured at the token and dollar level per endpoint; without that observability, "performance optimization" degrades into guesswork and can silently trade away quality for speed.
