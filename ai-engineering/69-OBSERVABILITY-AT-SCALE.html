CHAPTER 69 — OBSERVABILITY AT SCALE

Volume 5 — Enterprise AI Engineering · Chapter 6 of 12

Monitoring (Chapter 68) gave you dashboards and alerts that answer "is the system healthy right now?" — a handful of curated metrics like p99 latency, error rate, and token spend, wired to thresholds someone configured in advance. Observability at scale answers a much harder question that monitoring cannot: "why is this specific request, out of ten million today, behaving strangely, and I did not know in advance to look for it?" As your AI system grows from a single ChatClient call to a multi-hop pipeline of retrieval, tool calls, sub-agents, and re-ranking, the number of places a request can silently go wrong grows combinatorially — and the volume of telemetry needed to explain any single failure grows with it. This chapter is about building the instrumentation, propagation, and storage discipline to answer novel questions after the fact, without having predicted them, and without your telemetry bill exceeding your inference bill.

1. MOTIVATION

Every chapter so far in Volume 5 has assumed you can see inside your system when something goes wrong. Guardrails assume you can inspect what was blocked and why. Evaluation assumes you can replay a specific interaction. Cost tracking (the next chapter) assumes you can attribute every token to a request, a user, and a feature. None of that is possible for free — it is only possible if the system was built, from the instrumentation layer up, to be observable: to expose enough of its internal state, per request, that an engineer who was not watching in real time can still reconstruct exactly what happened.

Here is the concrete scenario that makes this urgent rather than academic. Your support-ticket triage agent, built on a ChatClient with a retrieval Advisor and two tool-calling functions, has been running fine for three weeks. On a Tuesday morning, a customer complains that the agent recommended a refund policy that does not exist. You have no idea, from the outside, whether the model hallucinated it outright, whether the vector store retrieved a stale or wrong document, whether a tool call returned bad data, or whether a prompt template regression silently changed the system instructions two deploys ago. With a single log line per request ("chat completed, 1200ms, 340 tokens") you cannot answer this. With full observability — a trace that shows every span the request touched, the exact retrieved chunks, the exact prompt sent to the model, and the exact completion returned — you can answer it in minutes instead of days.

The word "at scale" in this chapter's title is doing real work. Observability techniques that are trivial at ten requests per day — print the whole prompt and response to the console — become a serious engineering problem at ten thousand requests per second: you cannot store every full prompt/completion pair forever, you cannot attach unlimited unique labels to a metric without your metrics backend falling over, and you cannot trace every single request through a distributed system without the tracing overhead itself becoming a cost and latency problem. This chapter is about the specific engineering discipline — sampling, cardinality control, structured context propagation, and semantic conventions — that makes deep visibility survive contact with production volume.

2. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

2.1 From Logs to APM (1990s–2000s)

Early production debugging was grep against log files on individual machines — workable when a system was one process on one box. As applications became distributed across multiple servers, this stopped working: a single user request might touch five machines, and correlating log lines across them by hand was hopeless. Application Performance Monitoring (APM) tools (early commercial examples include Wily Introscope, later New Relic, AppDynamics, Dynatrace) emerged to auto-instrument code and centralize metrics and traces, giving engineers a single pane of glass instead of five terminals full of tail -f.

2.2 Distributed Tracing: Google's Dapper (2010)

The idea that unlocked modern observability came from Google's internal paper on Dapper, published in 2010. Its core contribution was conceptually simple but transformative: attach a single trace ID to a request the moment it enters the system, and propagate that ID (plus a parent-child span ID hierarchy) through every downstream service call, thread hop, and network boundary. Every service, instead of logging in isolation, reports its work as a span tagged with that shared trace ID. Reassembling the trace ID's spans afterward reconstructs the entire request's journey as a tree — exactly what you need to answer "which of the five services this request touched was slow, and why." Zipkin (from Twitter) and Jaeger (from Uber) were the open-source implementations that made this pattern broadly accessible outside Google.

2.3 The Three Pillars and Their Unification: OpenTelemetry (2019–present)

For most of the 2010s, logs, metrics, and traces were collected by separate tools with separate agents, separate data formats, and no shared identifiers — an engineer had to manually cross-reference a timestamp in a log file against a timestamp in a metrics dashboard against a trace ID in a tracing UI. OpenTelemetry (a 2019 merger of the OpenTracing and OpenCensus projects, now a CNCF standard) unified the instrumentation API and the wire format across all three pillars, and — critically for this chapter — standardized how context (trace ID, span ID, baggage) propagates across process and even language boundaries. This is the layer Spring Boot's Micrometer Tracing and Spring AI's own instrumentation sit on top of today.

2.4 The LLM-Specific Turn (2023–present)

Generic distributed tracing was built for services that call each other with well-defined, small, structured payloads (a user ID, an order total). LLM pipelines break that assumption: the "payload" worth inspecting is a multi-kilobyte prompt built from a system message, retrieved context chunks, conversation history, and tool schemas, and the thing you most need to debug — why did the model say this — is not visible from latency and status code alone. This produced a new category of tooling (LangSmith, Langfuse, Arize Phoenix, Helicone, and others) purpose-built to capture and visualize prompts, completions, retrieved documents, token usage, and evaluation scores as first-class trace data — and it produced the OpenTelemetry Semantic Conventions for Generative AI, a still-evolving standard for naming these LLM-specific attributes (gen_ai.system, gen_ai.request.model, gen_ai.usage.input_tokens, and similar) so that generic OTel-compatible backends can render them meaningfully without bespoke integration per model provider.

Why this history matters to you as an engineer: Spring AI's observability support is not a bespoke invention — it is a thin, idiomatic Java layer over Micrometer's Observation API, which itself bridges to OpenTelemetry. When you instrument a ChatClient call, you are plugging into the same trace-ID-propagation lineage that started with Dapper in 2010. Understanding that lineage tells you exactly what guarantees you get for free (cross-service correlation, vendor-neutral export) and what you still have to design yourself (what counts as a "span" inside an LLM call, how much of the prompt to actually persist).

3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

Give an engineer, after the fact and without having predicted the failure in advance, enough structured, correlated data to reconstruct exactly what a single AI request did end to end — while keeping the volume, cardinality, and cost of that data bounded as request volume scales into the millions per day.

Unpack the two halves of that sentence, because they pull in opposite directions and the entire engineering discipline of this chapter lives in the tension between them.

The first half — "reconstruct exactly what happened" — argues for capturing everything: the full prompt, the full completion, every retrieved chunk, every tool call's input and output, every intermediate reasoning step, at full fidelity, for every single request. This is the ideal for debugging. The second half — "keeping volume, cardinality, and cost bounded" — argues for the opposite: sample aggressively, truncate payloads, cap the number of unique label combinations, and discard detail as soon as it is no longer statistically necessary. A monitoring dashboard (Chapter 68) only ever needed the second half — a few dozen well-chosen numeric aggregates. Observability at scale needs both halves simultaneously, and reconciling them is the actual engineering problem: you cannot store full-fidelity data for every request forever, but you also cannot decide in advance which 0.1% of requests will turn out to matter for tomorrow's incident.

4. THEORY

4.1 The Three Pillars, and the Fourth One AI Adds

PillarWhat It CapturesBest Question It Answers
MetricsNumeric aggregates over time (counters, gauges, histograms)"Is p99 latency degrading over the last hour?"
LogsDiscrete, timestamped events, usually text or structured JSON"What exact error message did this request produce?"
TracesA causal tree of spans showing how a request moved through the system"Which specific step in this request's path was slow?"
Evaluations / Scores (AI-specific)Quality judgments attached to a specific trace — groundedness, relevance, toxicity, human/LLM-judge ratings"Was this specific answer actually good, not just fast?"

The fourth pillar is the one that makes AI observability genuinely different from classical service observability. A payment service's trace tells you it succeeded in 220ms; it does not need a fourth pillar because "succeeded" is unambiguous — the charge either went through or it did not. An LLM call can succeed in exactly the classical sense (200 OK, valid JSON, 220ms) while still being wrong — hallucinated, ungrounded, off-policy. Observability at scale for AI systems therefore has to carry evaluation scores as trace-attached data, not as a separate disconnected system, so that "this trace was slow" and "this trace was also wrong" can be correlated (later chapters on evaluation frameworks build directly on this attachment).

4.2 Cardinality: The Central Constraint

Cardinality is the number of unique combinations of label/tag values a metric can take on. A metric tagged only by status=success|failure has cardinality 2. A metric additionally tagged by user_id across a million users has cardinality in the millions — and most time-series metrics backends (Prometheus and its relatives included) store one independent time series per unique label combination. Tag a counter with a high-cardinality field like user_id, session_id, or a raw prompt hash, and you do not get "a metric with a useful dimension" — you get millions of near-empty time series, which is the single most common way engineering teams accidentally take down or bankrupt their metrics backend. This is why observability tooling draws a hard line between low-cardinality tags (safe on metrics: model name, provider, environment, error class) and high-cardinality attributes (safe on traces and logs, which are built to handle unbounded uniqueness per event, but not on metrics).

4.3 Sampling: You Cannot Keep Everything

Head-based sampling decides whether to trace a request at the moment it starts (e.g., "trace 1% of all requests, chosen randomly"), which is cheap but can miss the rare error you actually needed. Tail-based sampling defers the decision until the request finishes, keeping the full trace only if some condition is met after the fact — it errored, it exceeded a latency threshold, it scored badly on an evaluation, or it was flagged by a guardrail. Tail-based sampling is strictly more useful for debugging because it biases toward exactly the traces you would have wanted, but it costs more to implement because every span has to be buffered somewhere until the sampling decision is made, rather than being dropped or kept immediately.

4.4 Golden Signals, Extended for AI

The classical "four golden signals" from Google's SRE discipline — latency, traffic, errors, saturation — still apply, but AI pipelines need a parallel set that has no classical equivalent:

  • Time to first token (TTFT): for streaming responses, the gap between request start and the first visible token — a distinct, user-perceived latency signal separate from total completion time.
  • Tokens per second (throughput): how fast tokens stream once generation starts, which can bottleneck independently of TTFT.
  • Token counts (input/output): the primary cost driver, and a proxy for context bloat or runaway generation.
  • Retrieval hit rate / relevance: whether a RAG pipeline's retrieval step is actually surfacing useful chunks — a silent failure mode that produces no error, only a bad answer.
  • Groundedness / hallucination rate: the evaluation-pillar signal that most directly measures "was this answer trustworthy," usually sampled rather than computed on every request due to cost.

5. INTERNAL WORKING

Walk through exactly what happens, mechanically, when a single request flows through an instrumented Spring AI application, because the mechanism explains every design decision in the rest of the chapter.

  1. Request arrives, trace context is created or extracted. If the request came from an upstream service that already has a trace ID (propagated via an HTTP header, typically traceparent under the W3C Trace Context standard), that ID is reused so the new spans nest under the existing trace. If this is the entry point, a new trace ID is minted.
  2. A root span is opened for the HTTP request by the web framework's instrumentation (Spring MVC / WebFlux, auto-instrumented via Micrometer Tracing), tagged with low-cardinality attributes: HTTP method, route template (not the raw URL with IDs in it — that would blow up cardinality), and eventually the response status.
  3. The orchestration layer opens a child span as the ChatClient call begins. Spring AI's observation instrumentation wraps this call through Micrometer's ObservationRegistry, which is the same mechanism Spring Boot already uses to instrument HTTP clients and database calls — an Observation is started, enriched with context (model name, provider, whether streaming is used), and stopped when the call completes, automatically producing both a span (for tracing backends) and a set of timer/counter metrics (for metrics backends) from the same instrumentation point.
  4. Each Advisor in the chain gets its own nested span if it does meaningful work — a retrieval advisor's span shows how long the vector search took and, in a properly configured system, references (by ID, not necessarily full text, for cost reasons) which documents were retrieved. A memory advisor's span shows how much conversation history was loaded and how many tokens it added to the prompt.
  5. The actual call to the model provider is its own span, nested under the ChatClient span, carrying the GenAI-convention attributes: request model, response model (which can differ if a provider silently reroutes), input/output token counts, and finish reason. This is the span that carries cost-relevant data forward into the next chapter.
  6. If the model requests a tool call, the tool execution gets its own span, siblings to (or nested under, depending on instrumentation depth) the model call span, showing the tool's arguments, its latency, and its return value — this is frequently where the actual root cause of a bad final answer lives, because a tool returning stale or malformed data upstream of the model is invisible unless its span is captured explicitly.
  7. All spans, once closed, are batched and exported asynchronously by an OpenTelemetry exporter to a collector, which may apply tail-based sampling, redaction, and enrichment before forwarding to a tracing backend, a metrics backend, and (for GenAI-specific data) an LLM observability platform — frequently all fed from the same underlying span stream rather than three separate instrumentation efforts.
  8. The trace ID is also attached to every structured log line emitted during the request (via MDC — Mapped Diagnostic Context — populated automatically by the tracing instrumentation), which is what lets an engineer jump from "I see an elevated error rate in this metric" to "here are the exact trace IDs of the failing requests" to "here is the full log output and span tree for one of them," without three separate manual lookups.

The edge case worth reasoning through yourself: what happens across an asynchronous or reactive boundary — a Mono/Flux streaming response, or work handed off to a separate thread pool? Trace context is normally propagated via thread-local storage, which does not automatically survive a thread hop. Reactive instrumentation (Reactor's context propagation, combined with Micrometer's context propagation library) has to explicitly carry the trace context through the reactive pipeline; if this wiring is missing or misconfigured, you get orphaned spans — child spans that exist but do not correctly nest under their parent trace, which is one of the most common and most confusing observability bugs in reactive Spring applications.

6. MATHEMATICS

This topic needs arithmetic more than calculus, but the arithmetic is genuinely load-bearing — it is exactly what tells you whether an observability design will survive production volume or quietly bankrupt you.

6.1 Cardinality Explosion

If a metric is tagged along k independent dimensions with cardinalities c₁, c₂, …, c_k, the number of distinct time series it can produce is the product, not the sum: 

Series count = c₁ × c₂ × c₃ × … × c_k

Example: a metric tagged by
  model        (5 values)
  environment  (3 values)
  status       (2 values)
  → 5 × 3 × 2 = 30 series   (fine)

Same metric, someone adds:
  user_id      (2,000,000 distinct values)
  → 5 × 3 × 2 × 2,000,000 = 60,000,000 series   (catastrophic)

This is why "never put an unbounded field on a metric tag" is not a style preference — it is the difference between a metrics backend serving dashboards in milliseconds and one that falls over under its own index size.

6.2 Sampling and the Cost/Coverage Trade-off

At a sampling rate p (e.g., p = 0.01 for 1%), the expected number of full traces captured out of N total requests is simply N × p. The problem this exposes: for a rare failure mode occurring at true rate r in the traffic (say r = 0.001, one in a thousand requests), random head-based sampling at p = 0.01 captures that failure with probability only p per occurrence — you would expect to need roughly 1/(r × p) = 100,000 requests before capturing even one example of the rare failure as a full trace. Tail-based sampling breaks this dependency entirely by conditioning the keep-decision on the outcome itself (error, high latency, bad eval score) rather than on chance, which is precisely why it dominates head-based sampling for debugging rare, high-value failures despite its higher implementation cost.

6.3 Storage Cost Scaling

If a full trace (including prompt and completion text) averages s kilobytes and you serve N requests per day at retained sampling rate p, daily storage growth is N × p × s. With N = 10,000,000 requests/day, s = 8 KB per full trace, and p = 1%, that is 10,000,000 × 0.01 × 8 KB ≈ 800 MB/day just for sampled full-fidelity traces — manageable. Set p = 1.0 (trace everything at full fidelity) and the same math gives 80 GB/day, which compounds into a genuinely large, genuinely expensive retention problem within weeks. The lesson the arithmetic teaches directly: full-fidelity, unsampled tracing of every AI request is a decision with a real, computable dollar cost, and that cost should be an explicit engineering trade-off, not an accident of leaving default settings untouched.

The architectural point worth internalizing: the collector, not the application, is where sampling policy, redaction, and fan-out decisions live. This keeps the application code honest and simple — it always emits full-fidelity OTLP data on every request — while the operational cost and compliance controls are centralized in one place that can change without a redeploy of every service. This mirrors a pattern you already know from Spring: keeping cross-cutting concerns (logging format, security headers) out of business logic and into a shared filter or interceptor layer — here, the "interceptor layer" is the collector sitting between every service and every backend.

9. JAVA / SPRING PERSPECTIVE

Spring AI does not invent a parallel observability stack — it plugs directly into the same Micrometer ObservationRegistry that already instruments Spring Boot's RestTemplate/WebClient calls, JDBC queries, and Spring MVC request handling. This is a deliberate design choice: a ChatClient call and a downstream WebClient call show up as sibling spans in the same trace, using the same conventions, exportable to the same backend, without bespoke glue code.

ConceptRole in Spring AI Observability
ObservationRegistry (Micrometer)The shared registry that ChatClient and ChatModel implementations use to start/stop an Observation around each call, producing both a trace span and a set of metrics from a single instrumentation point.
ChatClient / ChatModelThe instrumented call sites — each invocation becomes an observation carrying model name, provider, token usage, and duration as attributes.
Advisor chainSince advisors are the AOP-style interception points around a chat call (as introduced in earlier Spring AI chapters), they are the natural place to add or inspect nested spans for retrieval, memory loading, and guardrail checks.
Micrometer Tracing (Brave / OpenTelemetry bridge)Supplies the actual trace ID / span ID propagation and export, independent of which tracing backend you choose — Spring AI's instrumentation does not need to know or care whether the backend is Zipkin, Jaeger, or a vendor SaaS.
Spring Boot ActuatorExposes the resulting metrics (request counts, latency histograms, token counters) over /actuator/metrics and /actuator/prometheus, the same mechanism used for every other Spring Boot metric.
MDC (Mapped Diagnostic Context)Automatically populated with the active trace/span ID by the tracing instrumentation, so every log line written with a standard SLF4J logger during request handling is trace-correlated without manual plumbing.

A realistic instrumentation pattern for a custom span around a retrieval step, using the Observation API directly rather than relying only on auto-instrumentation, looks like this: 

@Component
public class InstrumentedRetriever {

    private final VectorStore vectorStore;
    private final ObservationRegistry observationRegistry;

    public InstrumentedRetriever(VectorStore vectorStore,
                                  ObservationRegistry observationRegistry) {
        this.vectorStore = vectorStore;
        this.observationRegistry = observationRegistry;
    }

    public List<Document> retrieve(String query, int topK) {
        return Observation.createNotStarted("rag.retrieval", observationRegistry)
            .lowCardinalityKeyValue("top_k", String.valueOf(topK))
            .observe(() -> {
                List<Document> results = vectorStore.similaritySearch(
                    SearchRequest.query(query).withTopK(topK));

                // High-cardinality detail belongs on the span/log,
                // never as a metric tag.
                Observation.tryToSetName(
                    Objects.requireNonNull(observationRegistry.getCurrentObservation()),
                    "rag.retrieval");
                log.info("retrieval_complete query_len={} results={} top_score={}",
                    query.length(), results.size(),
                    results.isEmpty() ? 0.0 : results.get(0).getMetadata().get("score"));

                return results;
            });
    }
}

Two habits shown here matter more than the exact API surface: the numeric, bounded value (top_k) is attached as a low-cardinality key-value suitable for both traces and metrics, while unbounded detail (the raw query text, per-document scores) goes to the log line, which is trace-correlated via MDC but does not multiply metric cardinality. This split is the single most consequential Java-level decision in this entire chapter.

10. BEST PRACTICES

  • Propagate one trace ID from the edge to every downstream call, including tool calls and vector store queries. A trace that stops at the ChatClient boundary and treats retrieval or tool execution as an opaque black box defeats the purpose — instrument the layers below the model call, not just the call itself.
  • Enforce a hard cardinality budget on every metric tag in code review. Treat "does this tag have an unbounded set of possible values?" as a blocking question, the same way you would treat an unparameterized SQL query.
  • Default to tail-based sampling for anything that touches an LLM. Random sampling of AI traffic systematically under-captures the rare, expensive-to-diagnose failures (hallucinations, tool errors, guardrail triggers) that observability exists to catch in the first place.
  • Redact before you persist, not after. PII and secrets that make it into a trace store are a compliance liability the moment they land, regardless of how quickly you delete them afterward — redaction belongs in the collector pipeline, applied to every span, before export to any long-lived backend.
  • Attach evaluation scores to the same trace ID as the request they judge, whether the evaluation runs synchronously or as an asynchronous batch job hours later — this is what makes "which failures were also low-quality" a joinable query instead of a manual cross-reference exercise.
  • Set explicit retention tiers: full-fidelity traces (with prompt/completion text) for a short window (days), aggregated metrics for a long window (months to years), and sampled anonymized traces for medium-term trend analysis — one blanket retention policy is almost always wrong for at least one of these.

11. COMMON MISTAKES

  • Tagging metrics with user ID, session ID, or a raw prompt hash. This is the single most frequent cause of a metrics backend degrading or falling over in an LLM-serving system — the fix is always to move that data to a trace/log attribute instead of a metric tag, per Section 6.1's cardinality math.
  • Logging full prompts and completions at INFO level in production, unconditionally. This duplicates storage cost with the tracing backend, frequently captures PII or secrets pasted by users into a chat interface, and rarely gets the same retention/access controls as the purpose-built tracing pipeline.
  • Losing trace context across reactive or asynchronous boundaries. A Mono.subscribeOn hop, a @Async method, or a manually created thread pool that does not propagate the Reactor/Micrometer context produces orphaned spans that break the causal tree exactly where an incident investigation needs it most.
  • Treating "200 OK, low latency" as proof of a healthy request. Classical service health signals say nothing about groundedness or correctness — a fast, well-formed, hallucinated answer looks identical to a fast, well-formed, correct one on every classical golden signal.
  • No sampling strategy at all, in either direction. Teams either trace 100% of traffic (and get an unsustainable storage bill within weeks, as shown in Section 6.3) or sample nothing meaningfully (fixed 1% head-based sampling that systematically misses rare failures, as shown in Section 6.2) — both are the predictable result of never having made sampling an explicit design decision.
  • Building dashboards before building traces. Metrics answer "is something wrong"; only traces answer "what, exactly." Teams that invest entirely in the former discover, mid-incident, that they can see the fever but not diagnose the illness.

12. PERFORMANCE CONSIDERATIONS

Observability is not free, and at the request volumes this chapter targets, its own overhead needs to be actively managed rather than assumed away.

  • Instrumentation overhead per request — opening and closing spans, populating MDC, serializing attributes — is typically low (single-digit percent of request latency) when done via an established library like Micrometer, but home-grown, synchronous, un-batched exporters can add meaningful latency directly in the request path; always export asynchronously and in batches, never inline per span.
  • Payload size dominates cost more than span count. A trace with ten small spans is cheap; a trace with one span carrying an 8 KB prompt and a 4 KB completion is not. Truncate or reference-by-ID (store the full text in a separate, cheaper blob store keyed by trace ID, and keep only a pointer in the trace itself) whenever full text is not needed on every request.
  • Sampling rate is your primary cost lever, not the only one. Reducing retention window (Section 10) and truncating payload size compound multiplicatively with sampling rate — a 10x reduction in each, applied together, is a 1000x reduction in total storage, not merely additive.
  • High-cardinality metrics degrade query latency for everyone, not just for the team that introduced them — a shared Prometheus/Mimir instance with one team's cardinality explosion can slow down every other team's dashboards, which is why cardinality budgets need to be enforced centrally, not left to individual service teams' discretion.
  • Tail-based sampling has a memory cost proportional to in-flight request volume, because every span must be buffered until the keep/drop decision can be made at request completion — under-provisioning this buffer in the collector layer causes silent trace loss precisely during the traffic spikes when you need tracing the most.

13. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

Observability data is, by design, one of the richest single collections of sensitive information in an AI system — which makes it a genuine security surface, not a peripheral concern.

  • Prompts and completions frequently contain PII, credentials, or proprietary business data that a user typed into a chat interface or that a RAG pipeline retrieved from an internal document. A trace store that captures full text without redaction is, functionally, a second copy of your most sensitive data, often with weaker access controls than the primary data store it came from.
  • Prompt injection payloads get captured verbatim in traces — which is valuable for detecting and studying attacks (as covered in the Guardrails chapters), but means trace access itself needs to be treated as a privileged capability, since a trace viewer effectively has read access to attacker-crafted content alongside legitimate user data.
  • Access control on observability dashboards is frequently weaker than on the production data they expose. It is common for a broad engineering team to have dashboard access "for debugging" that amounts to unrestricted read access to user conversations — apply the same least-privilege principle to trace/log access that you would apply to a production database.
  • Redaction has to happen before persistence, in the collector pipeline, using pattern-based and/or model-assisted PII detection — redacting only at the presentation layer (masking in the UI while storing raw text underneath) does not reduce actual data exposure risk, only its visibility.
  • Honestly scoped: observability itself has a comparatively narrow security surface beyond data exposure — it does not typically introduce new injection or authentication attack vectors of its own. The real risk here is almost entirely about what sensitive data ends up captured and who can read it afterward, not about the tracing mechanism being attackable in a novel way.

14. INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

JUNIORQ1. What is the difference between monitoring and observability?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Monitoring is watching a predefined, curated set of metrics and alerts against known thresholds — it answers "is the system healthy right now" for questions you thought to ask in advance. Observability is the broader property of a system exposing enough structured, correlated data (logs, metrics, traces) that an engineer can answer a novel question after the fact, about a failure mode nobody predicted, without having to reproduce it.

JUNIORQ2. What are the three pillars of observability, and what unique question does each answer?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Metrics (numeric aggregates over time) answer "is something trending wrong?" Logs (discrete timestamped events) answer "what exact message or error did this request produce?" Traces (a causal tree of spans across a request's path) answer "which specific step, across which services, was slow or failed?" For AI systems a fourth pillar, evaluation/quality scores attached to a trace, answers "was the output actually good, independent of whether it was fast and well-formed."

MIDQ3. Explain cardinality and why it matters when instrumenting an LLM-serving application.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Cardinality is the number of unique label/tag combinations a metric can take on; most metrics backends store one independent time series per unique combination, and cardinality across independent tag dimensions multiplies rather than adds. Tagging a metric with an unbounded field like user ID or a raw prompt hash can turn a metric that should have a few dozen time series into millions, which degrades or crashes the metrics backend for every team sharing it. High-cardinality data (user ID, session ID, prompt text) belongs on traces and logs, which are designed for unbounded per-event uniqueness, never on metric tags.

MIDQ4. What is the difference between head-based and tail-based sampling, and when would you choose each?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Head-based sampling decides whether to keep a trace at request start (e.g., a fixed random 1%), which is cheap but samples rare failures proportionally rarely — it can take an enormous number of requests before a rare error is ever captured at full fidelity. Tail-based sampling defers the decision until the request completes and conditions it on outcome (error, high latency, low evaluation score), which reliably captures the highest-value traces at the cost of buffering every in-flight span until the decision is made. For AI systems, where the failures worth debugging (hallucinations, tool errors) are rare and high-value, tail-based sampling is usually the better trade-off despite its higher implementation and memory cost.

SENIORQ5. Design the tracing strategy for a multi-step RAG-plus-tool-calling pipeline built on Spring AI. What has to be true for a single incident to be diagnosable from the resulting traces?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

A single trace ID must be created (or propagated from upstream) at the request boundary and carried through every layer: the ChatClient call, each Advisor in the chain (memory, retrieval), the underlying ChatModel call(s), and any tool callback invocations — each as a nested span using Micrometer's ObservationRegistry, so both traces and metrics are produced from one instrumentation point. Retrieval spans must record retrieved-document identifiers and relevance scores (not necessarily full text, for cost/PII reasons). Model-call spans must record token counts, model/provider identity, and finish reason. Tool-call spans must record inputs/outputs since a bad upstream tool response is otherwise indistinguishable from a hallucination. Trace context must survive any reactive/async boundaries via Reactor context propagation. Finally, this must all be tail-sampled so that error, high-latency, and low-eval-score requests are reliably retained at full fidelity, with PII redaction applied in the collector before persistence — without all of these being true simultaneously, at least one class of incident (bad retrieval, bad tool data, or a rare model failure) will be undiagnosable after the fact.

Interview Gold Point: Explicitly call out that a raw "200 OK, N ms" trace tells you nothing about correctness, and that the evaluation/quality pillar must be attached to the same trace ID as the request it judges — even if the evaluation runs asynchronously, hours later, as a batch job. Interviewers weight this heavily because it distinguishes candidates who have only used generic distributed tracing from candidates who understand what is specifically different about observing an LLM pipeline.
SENIORQ6. A team's Prometheus instance is falling over under an LLM-serving application's metrics load. How do you diagnose and fix it?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Diagnose by inspecting which metrics have the highest number of distinct time series (most Prometheus-compatible backends expose this directly, e.g., via topk queries against the internal series-count metric) and cross-reference against recently added tag dimensions. The near-universal root cause is an unbounded tag (user ID, session ID, request path with embedded IDs instead of a route template, raw prompt hash) added to a counter or histogram. The fix is to remove that tag from the metric entirely and, if that dimension of data is genuinely needed for debugging, move it to a trace or log attribute instead, where per-event uniqueness is expected and handled efficiently. Prevent recurrence with a cardinality budget enforced in code review or via automated linting of instrumentation code, and by using route templates rather than raw paths in any HTTP-layer instrumentation.

SENIORQ7. Why is a full-fidelity trace of every single AI request usually the wrong default, even though it sounds like the "safest" choice?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at senior level

Storage and processing cost scale directly with request volume times payload size times retention window; at production scale (millions of requests/day, multi-kilobyte prompts and completions), unsampled full-fidelity tracing produces tens to hundreds of gigabytes of trace data daily and compounds into a large, ongoing cost with diminishing diagnostic value, since the overwhelming majority of successful, unremarkable requests never need to be inspected individually. It also increases the PII/compliance exposure surface, since every prompt a user ever typed is durably persisted somewhere. The better default is tail-based sampling that guarantees capture of error, high-latency, and low-quality-score requests at full fidelity, aggregated metrics for everything else, and short retention windows for the full-fidelity tier — this preserves debuggability for the cases that matter while keeping cost and compliance exposure proportional to actual value.

STAFFQ8. As a platform lead, how would you evolve an organization from "each team owns its own logging" to a shared, standardized observability platform for AI services, and what risks would you watch for?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Standardize on the OpenTelemetry instrumentation API and wire format organization-wide so that no team's telemetry is locked to a specific vendor, and adopt (or extend) the GenAI semantic conventions for LLM-specific attributes so that dashboards and tooling built for one team's AI service work unmodified for another's. Centralize sampling policy, PII redaction, and cardinality enforcement in a shared collector tier rather than leaving them to individual service teams' discretion, since inconsistent enforcement is how cardinality incidents and compliance gaps actually happen in practice. Provide a small number of golden, reusable instrumentation patterns (a shared Spring Boot starter wrapping the ObservationRegistry conventions for ChatClient/Advisor usage) rather than mandating every team hand-roll instrumentation, since inconsistent manual instrumentation is the single biggest source of low-quality, incomparable telemetry across teams. Watch for two risks specifically: teams treating the shared platform as a black box and never validating that their traces are actually complete (orphaned spans across async boundaries going unnoticed for months), and cost creep from teams defaulting to high sampling rates or unbounded retention because "storage is cheap" locally, when aggregated across the whole organization it is not.

16. SUMMARY

  • Observability at scale is the discipline of reconstructing exactly what a single request did, after the fact and without having predicted the question in advance — monitoring (Chapter 68) only answers questions you thought to ask up front.
  • AI pipelines add a fourth pillar beyond logs, metrics, and traces: evaluation/quality scores, because a request can succeed by every classical signal (fast, 200 OK, well-formed) while still being wrong.
  • Cardinality is the central mathematical constraint: independent tag dimensions multiply, and an unbounded field (user ID, session ID, raw prompt text) on a metric tag is the most common way teams accidentally break their own observability infrastructure.
  • Tail-based sampling, though costlier to implement than head-based sampling, reliably captures the rare, high-value failures that random sampling systematically misses — exactly the failures observability at scale exists to catch.
  • Spring AI's observability is not a separate system — it is built on Micrometer's ObservationRegistry, the same mechanism instrumenting Spring Boot's HTTP and database calls, which is why a ChatClient span and a downstream WebClient span appear naturally in the same trace.
  • Trace and log data are among the most sensitive data an AI system produces — redaction must happen in the collection pipeline before persistence, and dashboard access needs the same least-privilege discipline as production data access.
