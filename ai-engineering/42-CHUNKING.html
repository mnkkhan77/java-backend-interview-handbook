CHAPTER 42 — CHUNKING

Volume 3 — Embeddings & RAG · Chapter 2 of 13

The previous chapter gave you embedding models — the machinery that turns text into vectors so that semantic similarity becomes a computable distance. But an embedding model does not decide what text it is given; it faithfully compresses whatever chunk of text lands in front of it, well or badly. Feed it a whole 40-page PDF and you get one blurry vector representing everything and nothing. Feed it a single dangling sentence and you get a precise vector representing something out of context. Chunking is the decision layer that sits before embedding and determines whether your entire retrieval pipeline retrieves anything useful at all — get it wrong here and no amount of clever vector search downstream can save you.

1. MOTIVATION

It is tempting, once you understand embeddings, to think the hard part of Retrieval-Augmented Generation is behind you: turn text into vectors, store the vectors, search the vectors, done. In practice, the single decision that most determines whether a RAG system feels magical or feels broken is made earlier and is far less glamorous — it is the decision of where you cut the document. This is chunking: splitting a large body of text into smaller, retrievable, embeddable units.

Consider why this matters mechanically. An embedding model, as covered in the Embedding Models chapter, produces one fixed-size vector per unit of text it is given, and that vector is a lossy summary. If you embed an entire product manual as one unit, the resulting vector is a statistical average of hundreds of unrelated concepts — a warranty clause, an installation step, a troubleshooting table — smeared together into a single point in vector space that represents none of them well. Retrieval then fails not because the vector search algorithm is wrong, but because the thing you asked it to search for was never represented distinctly in the first place. Conversely, if you chunk too aggressively — one sentence per chunk — you get vectors that are precise but starved of context; a sentence like "Set this value to 3 in that case" is nearly meaningless in isolation, and its embedding will reflect that meaninglessness.

This is why experienced RAG engineers say, somewhat provocatively, that retrieval quality is chunking quality. You can swap in a better embedding model, add a reranker, tune your vector database's index parameters — and still get poor answers if the chunks being indexed do not correspond to coherent, self-contained units of meaning. Chunking is where information architecture, not machine learning, does most of the work. Getting it right is a prerequisite for everything the rest of this volume builds: vector databases (next chapter), retrieval strategies, and the advanced RAG patterns later in this volume all assume you are feeding them well-formed chunks. Getting it wrong quietly poisons every layer built on top.

2. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

Chunking as a named, deliberate engineering discipline is new — it did not exist as a concept before retrieval-augmented systems needed it — but the underlying problem it solves is an old one borrowed from information retrieval (IR) and natural language processing, and tracing that lineage explains why today's best practices look the way they do.

2.1 Classical Information Retrieval: The Document-Level Era

Traditional search engines, built on inverted indexes and term-frequency statistics like TF-IDF and BM25, generally indexed whole documents or, at finest granularity, whole paragraphs. This worked reasonably well because keyword-based retrieval degrades gracefully with document length: a long document containing the search term "gradient descent" once will still be found by a keyword query for "gradient descent," even if the rest of the document is unrelated. The unit of retrieval mattered far less because the matching mechanism was exact lexical overlap, not semantic similarity in a continuous vector space.

2.2 Passage Retrieval and Question Answering (2010s)

The open-domain question-answering research community — building systems that had to find a short passage inside a large corpus (Wikipedia, news archives) that actually answered a specific question — discovered the granularity problem well before modern RAG existed. Systems like DrQA and later dense passage retrieval work (DPR, 2020) showed that splitting corpora into fixed-size passages (often around 100 words) dramatically outperformed retrieving at the document level, because the "unit of relevance" for a question is usually a passage, not a whole article. This is the direct academic ancestor of today's RAG chunking practice: DPR's passage-splitting choices are functionally identical in spirit to the fixed-size chunking strategies still used as a baseline today.

2.3 The Embedding + LLM Era (2020–present)

When dense embeddings (covered in the previous chapter) and instruction-following LLMs matured enough to make RAG practical for general enterprise use, the passage-retrieval insight got inherited almost unchanged, but two new pressures were added. First, embedding models have a hard maximum input length (measured in tokens, as covered in the Tokens and Context Windows chapters) — text beyond that limit is silently truncated or rejected, so chunk size is no longer just a quality tuning knob, it is a hard constraint. Second, the retrieved chunk is not just displayed to a human the way a search result snippet is — it is concatenated directly into an LLM's prompt and the LLM is expected to reason over it, which means an incoherent or context-free chunk actively degrades the generated answer, not just the search ranking. This raised the stakes on chunking quality considerably, and is why the field moved quickly from naive fixed-size splitting toward structure-aware and semantic chunking strategies, which Sections 4 and 5 cover in depth.

EraRetrieval UnitMatching MechanismWhat Survives Today
Classical IR (TF-IDF/BM25)Whole documents/paragraphsExact lexical overlapHybrid search (keyword + vector), sparse retrieval as a fallback
Passage Retrieval / DPR eraFixed-size passages (~100 words)Dense vector similarityFixed-size chunking as the default baseline strategy
Modern RAGStructure-aware, semantically coherent chunksDense vector similarity + rerankingRecursive/semantic/hierarchical chunking, metadata-enriched chunks

Why this history matters to you as an engineer: every chunking strategy you will choose from in Section 4 is a descendant of a specific historical trade-off. Fixed-size chunking survives because it is simple and bounded; it is not the "old, bad" way and structure-aware chunking the "new, correct" way — they solve the granularity problem with different cost/quality trade-offs, and production systems frequently use several strategies for different document types within the same application.

3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

Strip away the tooling and libraries, and chunking reduces to one precise engineering problem:

Split a large body of text into units that are (a) small enough to embed accurately and fit within downstream token budgets, and (b) large enough, and coherent enough, to stand alone as a self-contained unit of meaning when retrieved in isolation.

Notice the tension baked directly into this statement — it is not a problem with one correct answer, it is a genuine optimization under conflicting constraints, which is why chunking has no single "correct" algorithm and instead a family of strategies each trading off differently. Three sub-problems fall out of the top-level statement:

  • The size problem. Embedding models have a maximum context length (often 512 tokens for older encoder models, up to 8,000+ tokens for newer embedding models), and the LLM generating the final answer has its own context window budget shared across every retrieved chunk plus the conversation and system prompt. Chunks must respect both ceilings.
  • The coherence problem. A chunk boundary that falls in the middle of a sentence, a table row, or a logical argument produces a chunk whose embedding represents a fragment, not a concept — and whose text, if retrieved, confuses the downstream LLM rather than informing it.
  • The context-loss problem. Even a perfectly coherent chunk can lose meaning when separated from its surrounding document — a paragraph that begins "Building on the previous point, this also applies to..." is uninterpretable without knowing what "the previous point" was, regardless of how cleanly it was split.

A good chunking strategy is, in essence, an answer to all three sub-problems simultaneously, tailored to a specific document type and retrieval use case — a strategy tuned for chunking legal contracts is not automatically the right strategy for chunking Slack message exports or source code, precisely because "coherent unit of meaning" means something structurally different in each.

4. THEORY

4.1 The Central Trade-off: Precision vs. Context

Every chunking decision sits somewhere on a single axis, and building intuition for this axis is more valuable than memorizing any specific algorithm. Picture two extremes:

ExtremeWhat HappensRetrieval Symptom
Chunk = entire documentOne vector per document, averaging every topic in itHigh recall of "roughly relevant" documents, terrible precision — the right document surfaces but the specific answer is buried inside a huge irrelevant blob passed to the LLM
Chunk = single sentence or clauseHighly specific vectors, but each stripped of surrounding contextHigh precision on keyword-like matches, but chunks are frequently unintelligible alone and miss questions that require combining two adjacent sentences

Every practical chunking strategy is an attempt to find a sweet spot on this spectrum — small enough that each chunk's embedding is a faithful, specific representation of one idea, but large enough that the idea is actually complete once you have it. This is directly analogous to a trade-off you already know from database design: an overly normalized schema requires expensive joins to reconstruct meaning (too small a chunk), while an overly denormalized table mixes unrelated concerns in one row and makes precise queries impossible (too large a chunk). Chunking is, structurally, a normalization decision applied to unstructured text.

4.2 Chunk Boundaries Should Follow Semantic Structure, Not Arbitrary Counts

The single biggest conceptual leap between naive and mature chunking is this: the best chunk boundary is wherever the document's own structure already indicates a topic shift — a paragraph break, a heading, a list item, a function definition — not wherever a fixed character or token count happens to land. A markdown document already tells you, via its heading hierarchy, exactly where one coherent idea ends and another begins; a Java source file already tells you, via method and class boundaries, where one logical unit ends. Naive fixed-size chunking throws this free structural information away and re-derives (badly, via arbitrary cut points) what the document format already gave you for free. This is why the dominant family of modern strategies, discussed in Section 5, is described as "recursive" or "structure-aware" — they exploit the document's own hierarchy before falling back to raw character counting.

4.3 Overlap as a Partial Fix for the Context-Loss Problem

Because even well-placed chunk boundaries lose some cross-boundary context (an argument spanning two adjacent paragraphs, a pronoun referring to something in the previous sentence), most practical strategies introduce overlap: each chunk repeats a small tail of the previous chunk's text. This does not eliminate the context-loss problem, it mitigates it statistically — if a concept spans a boundary, there is now a higher chance one of the two overlapping chunks captures it whole. Overlap is not free: it inflates the number of chunks stored, increases embedding and storage cost, and can cause the same passage to be retrieved twice at query time (usually deduplicated downstream). Treat overlap as a tunable dial, not a default you set once and forget — Section 10 gives concrete starting values.

5. INTERNAL WORKING

This section walks through how each major chunking strategy actually operates, mechanically, so you can reason about edge cases yourself rather than treating a chunking library as a black box.

5.1 Fixed-Size Chunking

The simplest strategy: pick a size (in characters or tokens), walk through the document, and cut every N units, optionally with an overlap of M units between consecutive chunks. 

Document (character stream):
"The invoice must be paid within 30 days of receipt. Late payments accrue
 interest at 1.5% per month. Disputes must be raised in writing within..."

Fixed-size, size=60 chars, overlap=15 chars:

Chunk 1: "The invoice must be paid within 30 days of receipt. Late "
Chunk 2: "s of receipt. Late payments accrue interest at 1.5% per m"
Chunk 3: "st at 1.5% per month. Disputes must be raised in writing.."
                └──── 15-char overlap tail carried into next chunk ────┘

Notice the failure mode visible even in this tiny example: chunk boundaries fall mid-word and mid-sentence purely because the cut point is a raw count, blind to the text's structure. Fixed-size chunking is cheap, deterministic, and trivially parallelizable, which is exactly why it remains a reasonable default for homogeneous, low-structure text (chat transcripts, log lines) — but it is the strategy most likely to produce the broken-sentence coherence problem described in Section 3.

5.2 Recursive Character/Structure Splitting

This is the workhorse strategy behind most production RAG systems (it is the default in frameworks like LangChain's RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter and conceptually mirrored in Spring AI's document transformers). The mechanism is a prioritized list of separators, tried in order from "most semantically meaningful" to "least": 

Separator priority list (typical default):
   1. "\n\n"   (paragraph break)   ← try first
   2. "\n"     (line break)
   3. ". "     (sentence boundary)
   4. " "      (word boundary)
   5. ""       (raw character)     ← last resort

Algorithm:
   function split(text, targetSize, separators):
       if length(text) <= targetSize:
           return [text]
       sep = first separator in separators that appears in text
       pieces = text.split(sep)
       merge adjacent pieces greedily until each merged group
           is as close to targetSize as possible without exceeding it
       for any resulting piece still > targetSize:
           recursively split() that piece using the NEXT separator in the list
       return final list of pieces

Walking through why this matters: the splitter first tries to cut only at paragraph breaks. If a paragraph is still too large after that, it descends to line breaks, then sentence boundaries, then word boundaries, only resorting to a raw character cut (which can break mid-word) if nothing more structured fits. This guarantees that whenever the document's natural structure permits a clean cut within the target size, the algorithm finds it — and only degrades to a less coherent cut when forced to. This is why the strategy is called "recursive": each recursive call operates on a smaller sub-problem with a less preferred separator.

5.3 Structure-Aware / Format-Specific Chunking

For documents with explicit machine-readable structure — Markdown headings, HTML tags, JSON objects, source code — you can skip generic separators entirely and split along the document's own schema. A Markdown splitter walks the heading tree (H1 → H2 → H3) and treats each section as a natural chunk boundary, optionally keeping the heading text attached to its body so a retrieved chunk still knows "I am the '## Refund Policy' section." A code-aware splitter (relevant if you are building a code-search or code-review RAG system) splits along function, method, or class boundaries using a language parser, rather than blind character counts, so that a chunk never bisects a method body.

5.4 Semantic Chunking

The most sophisticated common strategy uses the embedding model itself to find boundaries. The document is first split into small units (typically sentences), each sentence is embedded, and consecutive sentence-embeddings are compared for similarity. A large drop in similarity between sentence N and sentence N+1 is treated as evidence of a topic shift, and a chunk boundary is inserted there. 

Sentences:        S1    S2    S3    S4    S5    S6
Embeddings:       e1    e2    e3    e4    e5    e6

Cosine similarity between consecutive sentence embeddings:
   sim(e1,e2)=0.91   sim(e2,e3)=0.88   sim(e3,e4)=0.34   sim(e4,e5)=0.85   sim(e5,e6)=0.90
                                            ▲
                                   similarity drop ⇒ topic shift ⇒ cut here

Resulting chunks:   [ S1 S2 S3 ]        [ S4 S5 S6 ]
                    one coherent topic   a different coherent topic

This directly targets the coherence problem from Section 3 at the cost of an extra embedding pass over every sentence during ingestion, and a threshold-tuning step (how big a similarity drop counts as a "real" topic shift) that behaves differently across document types. It is powerful for long-form, loosely structured prose (books, transcripts, research papers) where paragraph breaks alone do not reliably signal topic boundaries, and less necessary for already well-structured documents where Section 5.3's approach is cheaper and just as effective.

5.5 Edge Cases Worth Reasoning Through

  • Tables. Splitting a table mid-row destroys it — a row's meaning depends on its header row, which may be far above it after chunking. Table-aware chunking either keeps whole tables as single chunks (re-attaching the header row to every chunk if a table must be split) or converts tables to a row-per-chunk format with the header injected into each row's text.
  • Very short documents. A two-sentence FAQ answer smaller than your target chunk size should not be padded or merged with an unrelated neighboring FAQ entry just to hit a size target — chunk size should be a ceiling, not a quota.
  • Code blocks embedded in prose. A code sample inside a technical document should generally not be split by prose-oriented separators (periods inside code are not sentence boundaries); format-aware chunkers detect fenced code blocks and treat them as atomic units.
  • Multi-lingual and non-whitespace-delimited text. Character-count-based sizing behaves inconsistently across languages (a token in Japanese or Chinese carries far more meaning per character than in English); token-count-based sizing, using the actual tokenizer of the embedding model, is more robust than raw character counting for multilingual corpora.

6. MATHEMATICS

Chunking is primarily an information-architecture problem, not a mathematical one, so this section stays intentionally light rather than manufacturing formalism the topic doesn't need. Two pieces of light math are genuinely useful in practice.

6.1 Overlap Ratio and Storage Cost

If chunk size is S tokens and overlap is O tokens, the effective "stride" between chunk start points is S − O. The number of chunks produced from a document of length L tokens is approximately: 

  num_chunks ≈ L / (S − O)

  Example: L = 10,000 tokens, S = 500, O = 50
  num_chunks ≈ 10,000 / 450 ≈ 22.2 → 23 chunks

  Compare to O = 0 (no overlap):
  num_chunks ≈ 10,000 / 500 = 20 chunks

The practical takeaway: overlap inflates the number of stored chunks (and therefore embedding cost and vector storage) by roughly S / (S − O). At a typical 10-15% overlap ratio this inflation is modest (11-18%); pushing overlap toward 50% roughly doubles storage and embedding cost for diminishing coherence gains — this is why Section 10's recommended overlap values stay in the 10-20% range rather than higher.

6.2 The Semantic-Chunking Similarity Threshold

For semantic chunking (Section 5.4), the boundary decision is simply a thresholded comparison of cosine similarity (formalized in the Vector Mathematics chapter) between consecutive sentence embeddings: 

  insert_boundary_after(sentence_i) = true  if  cos_sim(e_i, e_i+1) < threshold
                                     = false otherwise

There is no universally correct threshold — it depends on how topically homogeneous the source text naturally is (a legal contract's clauses are more uniformly "on-topic" than a rambling meeting transcript), so this value is empirically tuned per corpus, typically by sampling documents and inspecting where cuts land, rather than derived analytically.

This second diagram is the entire chapter in miniature: the same source text produces wildly different retrieval behavior purely based on where you decide to cut it, before a single line of embedding or vector-search code has run.

8. PRODUCTION ARCHITECTURE

In a real deployed RAG system, chunking is not a one-time script — it is a recurring stage in an ingestion pipeline that must handle new documents, updated documents, and deleted documents over the lifetime of the system. 

      ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
      │                    Document Sources                            │
      │  (S3/blob storage, Confluence, SharePoint, DB tables, CMS)      │
      └──────────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────┘
                                     ▼
      ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
      │                Ingestion Pipeline (batch or event-driven)       │
      │                                                                  │
      │   Parse ─► Chunk (this chapter) ─► Embed ─► Upsert to Vector DB  │
      │                    │                                             │
      │                    ▼                                             │
      │        Metadata store: chunk→source-doc mapping,                │
      │        chunk hash (for change detection & re-indexing)          │
      └──────────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────┘
                                     ▼
      ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
      │                       Vector Database (next chapter)            │
      └──────────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────┘
                                     ▼
      ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
      │        Retrieval Layer (query time) ──► Advisor / ChatClient    │
      │                (Spring AI, Volume 2 concepts revisited)         │
      └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Two production realities show up here that a one-off chunking script never has to face. First, re-chunking on document update: when a source document changes, you cannot cheaply diff "which chunks changed" unless you deliberately design for it — a common pattern is hashing each chunk's content and only re-embedding/re-upserting chunks whose hash changed, rather than deleting and re-embedding the entire document's chunk set on every edit. Second, chunk-to-source traceability: production systems store enough metadata per chunk (source document ID, page/section, ingestion timestamp) to let the application cite sources back to the user and to support deleting all chunks belonging to a document that was removed or a permission the user lost — retrieval without traceability is very hard to debug and, in regulated industries, is often a compliance requirement.

9. JAVA / SPRING PERSPECTIVE

Spring AI models this entire pipeline explicitly rather than leaving it as ad-hoc glue code, which matters because it means chunking has a first-class place in the framework's abstractions rather than being something you bolt on yourself.

The relevant building blocks in Spring AI's ETL (Extract, Transform, Load) abstraction for RAG ingestion are:

  • DocumentReader — reads raw source content (PDF, text, JSON, Markdown, and more via specific implementations) into Spring AI's Document objects, each carrying text content plus a metadata map.
  • DocumentTransformer — the interface under which chunking (referred to in Spring AI's documentation as "splitting") lives; implementations take a list of Document objects and return a new list of smaller, transformed Document objects. A token-aware text splitter implementation is provided out of the box, conceptually equivalent to the recursive splitting described in Section 5.2, operating on token counts rather than raw characters so that chunk sizes respect the embedding model's actual tokenizer.
  • DocumentWriter — takes the final chunked, embedded documents and writes them into a VectorStore implementation (covered in the next chapter).

A representative ingestion pipeline, written the way you would wire it in a Spring Boot batch job or startup CommandLineRunner, looks like this: 

@Component
public class DocumentIngestionPipeline {

    private final VectorStore vectorStore;

    public DocumentIngestionPipeline(VectorStore vectorStore) {
        this.vectorStore = vectorStore;
    }

    public void ingest(Resource pdfResource, String sourceId) {

        // 1. Extract: read the raw document into Spring AI Document objects
        DocumentReader reader = new PagePdfDocumentReader(pdfResource);
        List<Document> rawDocuments = reader.get();

        // 2. Transform: attach source metadata, then chunk
        rawDocuments.forEach(doc ->
            doc.getMetadata().put("sourceId", sourceId));

        TextSplitter splitter = new TokenTextSplitter(
                800,   // target chunk size, in tokens
                350,   // minimum chunk size to keep, in tokens
                5,     // minimum chunk length in characters to embed
                10000, // max number of chunks to generate
                true   // keep separators
        );
        List<Document> chunks = splitter.apply(rawDocuments);

        // 3. Load: embed (internally, via the configured EmbeddingModel)
        //    and upsert into the vector store
        vectorStore.add(chunks);
    }
}

Notice what this code deliberately does not do: it never calls an embedding model directly. The VectorStore.add(...) call internally invokes the configured EmbeddingModel (from the previous chapter) on each chunk before persisting it — chunking and embedding are cleanly separated stages, exactly mirroring the theoretical separation this chapter has drawn between "deciding what a unit of meaning is" and "representing that unit as a vector." This separation is also what lets you swap chunking strategy or embedding model independently, the same Strategy-pattern instinct a Spring engineer already applies to service implementations.

For structure-aware chunking of Markdown or HTML content — Section 5.3's approach — you would implement a custom DocumentTransformer that walks the heading hierarchy (using a Markdown/HTML parsing library) rather than relying purely on the generic token splitter, then compose it in the same pipeline before the token splitter runs as a final size-safety pass, since even a heading-delimited section can occasionally exceed a model's max chunk size and still needs a fallback split.

10. BEST PRACTICES

  • Default to structure-aware, recursive chunking, not fixed-size. Start from the document's own structure (headings, paragraphs, list items) and only fall back to raw character/token splitting for the rare oversized section — this alone eliminates most mid-sentence and mid-table breakage.
  • Size chunks in tokens of the target embedding model's tokenizer, not characters. Character counts are a poor proxy across languages and across models with different tokenizers (as covered in the Tokens chapter); a 500-character chunk can be wildly different token counts depending on content.
  • Use 10-20% overlap as a starting default, not zero and not 50%. This mitigates the context-loss problem from Section 3 at a modest, bounded storage/cost inflation, per the math in Section 6.1.
  • Attach structural metadata to every chunk — source document ID, section heading, page number — both for traceability (Section 8) and because some retrieval strategies (metadata filtering, covered in a later Volume 3 chapter) depend on it directly.
  • Prepend a short contextual header to each chunk when the source hierarchy matters (e.g., re-injecting "Document: Employee Handbook > Section: Parental Leave" at the top of every chunk from that section) so the chunk's embedding and text both retain topic identity even when retrieved with zero neighboring chunks.
  • Chunk differently per content type within the same system. A single global chunking configuration for PDFs, code files, and chat transcripts in the same knowledge base is a common source of quietly poor recall for whichever content type doesn't match the tuned defaults.
  • Evaluate chunking empirically, not by intuition alone — build a small labeled set of realistic queries with known correct source passages, and measure retrieval recall/precision (concepts formalized fully in this volume's evaluation chapters) before and after changing chunk size or strategy.

11. COMMON MISTAKES

  • Picking a chunk size once, globally, without revisiting it per document type. The right size for API documentation (short, structured, dense) is often wrong for narrative reports (longer, discursive) — a single "chunk_size=500" constant sprinkled through a codebase is a red flag.
  • Ignoring the embedding model's actual max input length. Chunks that exceed it get silently truncated by many embedding APIs, meaning the tail of a chunk is invisible to the embedding even though it is still stored and later shown, in full, to the LLM — a subtle bug that produces embeddings that don't match the text they're paired with.
  • Splitting tables and code blocks with prose-oriented separators. Sentence- or paragraph-based splitters applied blindly to tabular or code content produce chunks with no usable structure at all — this needs explicit, format-aware handling as covered in Section 5.5.
  • Treating overlap as free. Setting overlap to 50%+ "just to be safe" roughly doubles embedding and storage cost for a corpus while providing diminishing coherence benefit past the point where structure-aware boundaries already handle most cross-boundary context.
  • Never re-chunking after changing embedding models. A chunk size tuned around one embedding model's typical accuracy sweet spot does not automatically transfer to a different embedding model with a different max input length or different sensitivity to text length — swapping embedding models (a change the EmbeddingModel abstraction makes easy at the code level) should trigger a re-evaluation of chunking parameters, not just a re-embedding pass.
  • Losing the mapping between chunk and source document. Without metadata traceability, you cannot answer "why did the system say this?" or cleanly delete a document's data when it is removed or access to it is revoked — a serious problem in any system with compliance or access-control requirements.

12. PERFORMANCE CONSIDERATIONS

Chunking decisions ripple forward into every downstream performance and cost dimension of a RAG system:

  • Ingestion throughput. Semantic chunking (Section 5.4) requires embedding every sentence during the chunking decision itself, before the "real" chunk embeddings are even computed — this roughly doubles embedding API calls during ingestion compared to structure-aware or fixed-size chunking, which is a real cost and latency consideration for large corpora ingested on a tight schedule.
  • Storage and index size. More chunks (smaller chunk size, higher overlap) means more vectors stored, more index memory in the vector database, and higher per-query search cost — this is the direct engineering echo of the size/coherence trade-off from Section 4.1, now expressed in infrastructure cost rather than answer quality.
  • Query-time token budget. Every chunk retrieved at query time is concatenated into the LLM's prompt, competing for the same context window as the conversation history and system instructions (as covered in the Context Windows and Context Engineering chapters). Larger chunks mean fewer chunks fit per query, which can force you to retrieve fewer, larger passages instead of many precise, small ones — a direct trade-off between chunk size and how many distinct pieces of evidence the LLM can see per answer.
  • Re-indexing cost on document churn. In systems where source documents update frequently, chunk-level content hashing (Section 8) is what keeps re-indexing cost proportional to the amount of content that actually changed, rather than proportional to total corpus size on every update.

13. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

Chunking's direct security surface is narrower than topics like prompt handling or tool calling, but it is not zero, and being honest about where it does and does not matter is more useful than manufacturing risk. Two real considerations exist. First, access-control granularity: if a source document contains sections with different sensitivity levels (a report with a public summary and a confidential appendix), chunk-level metadata is your only lever for enforcing that distinction at retrieval time — if chunking discards which section a chunk came from, you lose the ability to filter retrieval by permission level, and a user could retrieve confidential content indirectly through a semantically similar query even if they'd never be shown the original document. Second, indirect prompt injection surface, inherited from the broader problem introduced in the Intelligence and Hallucinations chapters: since retrieved chunks are inserted directly into an LLM's prompt as trusted-looking context, a chunk boundary that happens to isolate an instruction-like sentence from its surrounding disclaiming context ("this is an example of what NOT to do: ignore all previous instructions...") can make that instruction-like text appear more prominent and less obviously quoted than it would in the full source document — a reason to treat all retrieved chunk content as untrusted input to the LLM regardless of how carefully it was chunked, a defense that belongs in the guardrails layer, not in the chunking logic itself.

14. INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

JUNIORQ1. What is chunking, and why can't you just embed an entire document as a single vector?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Chunking is splitting a large document into smaller units before embedding and storing them for retrieval. Embedding an entire document as one vector produces a single point that statistically averages every topic in the document, so the resulting vector represents no single idea distinctly — retrieval against it becomes imprecise because a query about one narrow topic in the document won't stand out against the "blur" of everything else averaged into the same vector.

JUNIORQ2. What is chunk overlap, and why would you use it?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Chunk overlap means consecutive chunks repeat a small tail/head of text between them, rather than cutting cleanly at the boundary. It mitigates context loss at chunk boundaries — an idea or reference that spans two chunks has a better chance of appearing whole in at least one of the overlapping chunks. It comes at the cost of more total chunks stored and embedded, so it is typically kept to roughly 10-20% of chunk size rather than set arbitrarily high.

MIDQ3. Compare fixed-size chunking to recursive/structure-aware chunking. When would you choose each?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Fixed-size chunking cuts text at raw character or token counts, blind to sentence or paragraph structure — it is simple, deterministic, and cheap, but frequently produces chunks that break mid-sentence or mid-table, hurting embedding quality and downstream LLM comprehension. Recursive/structure-aware chunking tries progressively more granular natural separators (paragraph, then line, then sentence, then word) and only falls back to a raw cut when forced, producing chunks that respect the document's own structure. Fixed-size is reasonable for low-structure, homogeneous text (raw logs, chat exports); structure-aware is the better default for prose, documentation, and any content with meaningful paragraph/heading structure.

MIDQ4. How does semantic chunking decide where to place a chunk boundary?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

The document is split into small units (typically sentences), each is embedded individually, and cosine similarity is computed between the embeddings of consecutive sentences. A significant drop in similarity between two adjacent sentences is treated as evidence of a topic shift, and a chunk boundary is inserted at that point. This directly targets topical coherence rather than relying on formatting cues, at the cost of an extra embedding pass per sentence during ingestion and an empirically-tuned similarity threshold.

SENIORQ5. A RAG system is retrieving the right documents but generating wrong or incomplete answers. Walk through how you would diagnose whether chunking is the root cause, and what you would change.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Start by inspecting the actual retrieved chunks for representative failing queries, not just the source documents — check whether chunk boundaries are severing the answer from its supporting context (a boundary landing between a question and its answer, or between a table header and its rows), whether chunks are too large and diluting the specific answer among unrelated surrounding text, or whether chunks lack the contextual anchor (heading, document title) needed to be interpretable alone. Cross-check chunk size against the embedding model's actual tokenizer and max input length to rule out silent truncation. If failures cluster around structural content (tables, code, nested lists), the fix is usually format-aware chunking rather than tuning size/overlap on the generic splitter. Validate any change against a labeled evaluation set of query-to-correct-passage pairs rather than a handful of manually inspected examples, since chunking changes can improve some queries while regressing others.

Interview Gold Point: Explicitly separate "retrieval found the right document" from "retrieval found the right passage within that document" — most candidates conflate the two, but they are different failure modes with different fixes (better embedding/search tuning for the former, better chunking for the latter), and naming that distinction signals real production RAG debugging experience.
SENIORQ6. How would you design a chunking pipeline that stays correct as source documents are updated over time, without re-embedding the entire corpus on every change?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Store a content hash per chunk alongside its source document ID and section/position metadata. On document update, re-run chunking on the new version, compute hashes for the new chunk set, and diff against the stored hashes for that document's existing chunks: unchanged chunks (same hash) are left untouched, changed or new chunks are re-embedded and upserted, and chunks present in the old set but absent from the new one are deleted from the vector store. This keeps embedding cost and index churn proportional to the amount of content that actually changed rather than to total document size, and the metadata layer (document ID, section, timestamp) is also what makes deleting all chunks for a removed or access-revoked document a simple, targeted operation instead of a full corpus scan.

STAFFQ7. A stakeholder proposes a single, global chunking configuration (one chunk size, one overlap, one strategy) to keep the ingestion pipeline "simple" across a knowledge base spanning PDFs, source code, structured tickets, and chat transcripts. How do you respond?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Simplicity in configuration is not free here — it trades away retrieval quality unevenly across content types, because "a coherent unit of meaning" is structurally different per format: a chunk size tuned for narrative PDF prose will bisect ticket fields or source-code functions, and a splitter tuned to respect code function boundaries wastes structure-detection effort on plain chat logs. The staff-level response is to keep a single ingestion pipeline abstraction (one interface, e.g. Spring AI's DocumentTransformer) but allow the concrete chunking strategy and its parameters to vary per content-type, selected by document source or MIME type at ingestion time — preserving operational simplicity (one pipeline, one deployment) while allowing the actual splitting logic to match each format's real structure. This should be backed by a small per-content-type evaluation set so the parameter choice per type is measured, not guessed, and revisited whenever the embedding model or corpus composition changes materially.

STAFFQ8. How should chunking strategy change, if at all, when your organization swaps to a different embedding model with a larger context window?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

A larger embedding context window removes the hard truncation constraint that previously forced small chunks, but it does not remove the coherence trade-off from Section 4.1 — an embedding model that can accept 8,000 tokens will still produce a diluted, averaged vector if you hand it eight unrelated paragraphs, exactly as a smaller model would with less input. The correct response is to re-run empirical evaluation (Section 10) at several candidate chunk sizes with the new model rather than assuming "bigger window means bigger chunks are now free," because the quality ceiling of a chunk is set by how many distinct ideas it contains, not purely by whether it fits. In practice, a larger context window is more valuable for reducing overlap-driven truncation edge cases and allowing slightly larger, still-coherent structural units (e.g., whole subsections instead of paragraphs) than for blindly maximizing chunk size.

16. SUMMARY

  • Chunking is the decision layer between raw documents and embeddings, and it determines retrieval quality more than any downstream vector search or reranking tuning can compensate for.
  • The central trade-off is precision versus context: chunks too large produce diluted, averaged embeddings; chunks too small lose the surrounding context needed to be meaningful on their own.
  • The best chunk boundaries follow the document's own structure (paragraphs, headings, function definitions) whenever available, falling back to raw character/token counts only when structure runs out — this is the "recursive" strategy that underlies most production systems.
  • Overlap between adjacent chunks mitigates, but does not eliminate, context loss at boundaries, at a measurable and boundable storage/cost inflation.
  • Chunking is not a one-time script but a recurring ingestion pipeline stage that must handle document updates efficiently (via content hashing) and preserve chunk-to-source traceability for citation, deletion, and access control.
  • Spring AI models this explicitly through its DocumentReader / DocumentTransformer (including token-aware text splitters) / DocumentWriter pipeline, keeping chunking cleanly separated from embedding and storage.
