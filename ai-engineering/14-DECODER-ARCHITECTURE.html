CHAPTER 14 — DECODER ARCHITECTURE

Volume 1 — AI Foundations · Chapter 11 of 22

The previous chapter gave every token a sense of "where" it sits in a sequence — positional encoding solved the problem that raw self-attention treats a sentence as an unordered bag of vectors. But knowing your position is useless without a rule for what you are allowed to look at. Every model you will call through Spring AI — GPT-family, Claude, Llama — generates text one token at a time, left to right, and is architecturally forbidden from peeking at tokens it has not produced yet. That forbidding rule, and the stack of layers built around it, is the decoder architecture, and it is the single most consequential design decision behind every chat completion API you will ever call.

1. MOTIVATION

Every time your Java code calls a chat model and streams a response back token by token, you are watching a decoder execute. Understanding why that architecture looks the way it does is not academic trivia for this book — it directly explains three things you will deal with constantly as an AI engineer: why generation is sequential and therefore latency scales with output length, why a model can never "go back and edit" a token it already committed to, and why the ChatClient.stream() API in Spring AI (covered fully in Volume 2) exists and returns a reactive stream of partial tokens rather than one big blob.

Up to this point in the series you have learned how a Transformer represents meaning (embeddings), how it lets tokens exchange information (self-attention), and how it knows the order of tokens (positional encoding, Chapter 13). None of those pieces, on their own, tell you how a model actually writes — how it goes from "understanding" an input to producing a coherent, growing sequence of new tokens, one at a time, each conditioned on everything before it. That is the job of the decoder stack, and it is the architecture family that every large language model you will integrate through Spring AI belongs to.

There is also a practical, career-relevant reason to master this chapter: architecture interviews for AI engineering roles routinely probe the distinction between encoder, decoder, and encoder-decoder models, because getting it wrong signals that a candidate has only used LLMs as an API consumer and never understood what is happening underneath the HTTP call. This chapter gives you the vocabulary and the mental model to speak about that distinction with the same confidence you already have about, say, the difference between a stateless and a stateful service.

2. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

2.1 The Original Transformer Was Two Halves, Not One

The 2017 paper that introduced the Transformer architecture was written to solve machine translation — turning a sentence in one language into a sentence in another. Translation has a natural two-part shape: first you need to fully understand the source sentence (read the whole thing, in both directions, before committing to anything), and then you need to generate the target sentence one word at a time, since a translation is produced left to right and each word you write constrains what comes next. The original architecture mirrored this shape exactly with two stacked components: an encoder, which processes the entire input sequence at once with full, bidirectional self-attention (every token can look at every other token, left and right), and a decoder, which generates the output sequence one token at a time, attending both to its own previously generated tokens and to the encoder's finished representation of the input. This is called an encoder-decoder (or sequence-to-sequence) architecture, and it is still the right shape for tasks with a clean "read fully, then write" structure — translation, summarization with a fixed source document, and speech-to-text.

2.2 The Decoder-Only Turn

The generative AI you interact with daily — GPT-family models, Claude, Llama, Mistral — does not use that two-part shape at all. Starting with GPT (2018) and cemented by GPT-2 and GPT-3, researchers found something important: if your real goal is open-ended text generation rather than a fixed translation-style transformation, you can drop the encoder half entirely and keep only a stack of decoder blocks. Feed the model a prompt as if it were "already-generated" text, and let it continue generating from there using the exact same left-to-right, causally-masked self-attention mechanism a decoder uses to generate translated output. This is called a decoder-only architecture, and it turned out to scale better, train more simply (a single, uniform stack of identical blocks instead of two different components with a bridge between them), and generalize to virtually any text task — question answering, coding, summarization, chat — by simply reframing the task as "continue this text," a technique you will see formalized later in this volume as prompting.

2.3 Why "Decoder-Only" Won for General-Purpose LLMs

The encoder-decoder split makes an implicit assumption: that there is a clean moment where "reading" ends and "writing" begins. Real conversational AI does not have that clean split — a chat model is simultaneously reading your latest message, remembering everything said before, and writing its response, and the boundary between "context" and "generation" is fluid (the model's own previous turns become context for its next turn). A decoder-only stack handles this uniformly: everything, whether it originated from the user or from the model itself, is just a sequence of tokens flowing through the same causally-masked layers. This uniformity is precisely why a single architecture family could scale from GPT-2 to the frontier chat models you call through Spring AI today, without needing a fundamentally different design for "understanding" versus "generating."

Why this history matters to you as an engineer: when a colleague says "we're using a Transformer," that sentence alone tells you almost nothing about how it behaves. Whether it is encoder-only, encoder-decoder, or decoder-only determines whether it can see the future of its own input (encoder: yes; decoder: never), whether it produces a fixed-size vector or a growing sequence, and whether the right Spring AI abstraction is an embedding model call or a ChatModel call. Every chat-completion API you will ever integrate is decoder-only under the hood — this chapter is the architecture behind that entire product category.

3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

Strip away the history, and the decoder architecture exists to solve one precise engineering problem:

Build a model that can generate a sequence of tokens one at a time, where each new token is conditioned on everything generated so far (and the original prompt), while training that model efficiently on massive datasets in parallel — without ever letting the model "cheat" by seeing tokens it has not generated yet.

Unpack the two halves of that sentence, because both are load-bearing. The first half — "conditioned on everything generated so far" — is what makes output coherent: token 500 of a response needs to be aware of tokens 1 through 499, not just the last few words, or a model's writing would drift into incoherence within a sentence. Self-attention (from the Attention Mechanism and Multi-Head Attention chapters) is exactly the mechanism that lets every position look back at every prior position — the decoder architecture's specific contribution is restricting that lookback to be only backward, never forward.

The second half — "efficiently, in parallel, without cheating" — is a genuinely subtle systems problem. During training, you have the full target sequence available (you know what the "correct" next word is at every position, because it is sitting right there in your training corpus). It would be wasteful to train the way you generate — one token at a time, waiting for each prediction before computing the next. The decoder architecture's central engineering trick, causal masking, lets you feed an entire sequence through the network in a single parallel pass during training while mathematically guaranteeing that the network's prediction for position i could not have used any information from position i+1 or beyond. Get this wrong — leak even a single future token during training — and the model will appear to perform brilliantly during training while being useless at real generation time, because at generation time the future genuinely does not exist yet.

4. THEORY

4.1 The Core Idea: Causality as an Architectural Constraint, Not a Convention

The most important mental model in this chapter is this: causality in a decoder is not a soft guideline the model tries to follow — it is a hard, mathematical constraint baked into the attention computation itself, the same way a Java compiler enforces type safety rather than merely recommending it. Think of a decoder block as a very disciplined committee meeting where each participant (token position) is allowed to ask "what did everyone who spoke before me say?" but is physically prevented from hearing anyone who has not spoken yet, no matter how much they might want to. This is enforced identically at every layer, every head, every training step — there is no code path anywhere in a standard decoder where a future token's information can leak backward.

4.2 Autoregression: Generation as Repeated Conditional Prediction

The decoder implements what statisticians call an autoregressive model: it predicts the probability of the next token given all previous tokens, and it generates a full sequence by repeatedly sampling one token from that distribution, appending it to the sequence, and repeating. Formally, the decoder learns to approximate: 

P(token_1, token_2, ..., token_n)
    = P(token_1)
    × P(token_2 | token_1)
    × P(token_3 | token_1, token_2)
    × ...
    × P(token_n | token_1, ..., token_n-1)

This is exactly the "predict the next word" framing from the What Is Intelligence chapter, now given architectural teeth. Every one of those conditional probabilities on the right is computed by the same decoder stack, just with a longer prefix each time. This is also why chat models feel like they are "thinking as they type" — they genuinely are recomputing a probability distribution fresh at every single token, conditioned on everything so far, including their own words from a moment ago.

4.3 Teacher Forcing vs. Autoregressive Inference — Two Different Modes of the Same Network

A subtlety that trips up many engineers: the decoder runs in two structurally different modes depending on whether it is training or generating, even though it is the identical set of weights.

AspectTraining (Teacher Forcing)Inference (Autoregressive Generation)
Input at position iThe true token from the training data, even if the model's own prediction at i-1 was wrongThe model's own previously generated token — errors can compound
ParallelismEntire sequence processed in one forward pass (causal mask enforces no cheating)Strictly sequential — token n+1 cannot be computed until token n exists
Cost per sequenceOne forward + one backward pass regardless of sequence lengthOne forward pass per generated token — cost scales with output length
Failure modeModel never sees its own mistakes propagateExposure bias — a single bad token can nudge everything after it off-distribution

This training/inference asymmetry — called exposure bias in the literature — is a real, known limitation: the model is trained on a diet of always-correct prefixes but must generate from its own, sometimes-imperfect prefixes at inference time. It is one root contributor (alongside the "plausibility versus truth" mechanism from earlier chapters) to why small early errors in a long generation can snowball into larger ones later in the same response.

4.4 The Anatomy of a Single Decoder Block

A decoder-only model is a tall stack of identical blocks (GPT-3-class models use dozens of them). Each block performs the same sequence of operations, and understanding one block means understanding the entire stack:

  1. Masked multi-head self-attention — every position attends to itself and all earlier positions (never later ones), using the multi-head mechanism from the Multi-Head Attention chapter.
  2. Residual connection + layer normalization — the attention output is added back to the block's input (a "skip connection") and normalized, which is what makes it possible to stack dozens of these blocks without training becoming unstable.
  3. Position-wise feed-forward network — a small two-layer neural network applied identically and independently to every position, giving the model additional capacity to transform each token's representation.
  4. Another residual connection + layer normalization — same stabilizing pattern as after attention.

Stack this block N times, and the final block's output at each position is projected through a linear layer into vocabulary-sized logits, then turned into a probability distribution over the entire vocabulary via softmax — the next-token distribution described in Section 4.2.

5. INTERNAL WORKING — STEP BY STEP

5.1 From Prompt to First Generated Token

Trace an actual request end to end, the way it happens when your Spring AI application calls a chat model:

  1. Tokenization (formalized next chapter) turns your prompt text into a sequence of token IDs.
  2. Embedding + positional encoding (Chapters covered earlier) turn each token ID into a vector that also encodes its position in the sequence.
  3. This sequence of vectors is fed through the entire decoder stack in one parallel pass — this phase is called prefill. Because the causal mask already guarantees no position can see the future, the whole prompt can be processed simultaneously, which is why prefill is comparatively fast even for long prompts.
  4. The final block's output at the last position of the prompt is projected to a probability distribution over the vocabulary. A token is sampled from this distribution (using temperature, top-p, or other sampling strategies covered in the Sampling Strategies chapter later in this volume).
  5. That newly sampled token is appended to the sequence, and the entire sequence — original prompt plus the one new token — is fed through the decoder stack again to produce the next token. This phase, repeated once per output token, is called decode.
  6. Steps 4–5 repeat until a stop condition is reached (a special end-of-sequence token, a max-token limit, or an application-defined stop sequence).

Notice the architectural asymmetry this reveals: prefill is one parallel pass over potentially thousands of prompt tokens; decode is many sequential passes, one per output token, each technically reprocessing the whole growing sequence. Real inference engines do not naively recompute everything from scratch at every decode step — they exploit a critical optimization called the KV cache, covered next, which is one of the most important production-performance facts in this entire book.

5.2 Why the Causal Mask Alone Isn't Enough — The KV Cache

Inside masked self-attention, each token position computes three vectors: a Query, a Key, and a Value (from the Attention Mechanism chapter). Here is the key operational insight: once a token's Key and Value vectors are computed at a given layer, they never change — a token at position 5 will have the exact same Key and Value at layer 12 no matter how many more tokens get appended after it, because causal masking guarantees position 5 was never allowed to see anything past itself in the first place. This means that during decode, instead of recomputing Key and Value vectors for the entire growing sequence at every single step, an inference engine can cache the Keys and Values computed for all previous positions and simply compute the Query, Key, and Value for the one brand-new token, then attend that new Query against the full cached history. This is the KV cache, and without it, generating a 1,000-token response would require roughly 1,000× more redundant computation than it actually does in a production system.

5.3 Edge Cases Worth Reasoning Through Yourself

  • What happens if the model needs to "reconsider" an earlier token? It architecturally cannot — once a token is emitted and appended to the sequence, it is fixed context for everything after it. This is precisely why techniques like chain-of-thought prompting (Volume 1, later chapter) and self-correction patterns work by generating additional tokens that reference and revise earlier reasoning, rather than by editing anything in place.
  • What happens at the very first position, where there is no "previous" token? The causal mask allows position 1 to attend only to itself — this is well-defined and simply means the first token's representation is built purely from its own embedding plus whatever the prompt's system/instruction tokens preceding it contain.
  • Does a longer prompt make every subsequent generated token more expensive? Yes — every decode step attends over the full cached history, so attention cost per generated token grows with total context length, which is a direct driver of the cost and latency behavior discussed in Section 12.

6. MATHEMATICS — THE INTUITION BEFORE THE FORMULAS

You already have the attention formula from earlier chapters: 

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( (Q · K^T) / sqrt(d_k) ) · V

The decoder's entire contribution to this formula is a single additive term applied before the softmax: a mask matrix that forces the attention weight of any "future" position to become exactly zero after softmax. Concretely, before the softmax is applied, every entry of the raw score matrix that corresponds to "position i attending to position j where j > i" has negative infinity added to it: 

MaskedAttention(Q, K, V) = softmax( (Q · K^T) / sqrt(d_k)  +  M ) · V

  where M[i][j] = 0        if j <= i   (allowed: look at self or earlier)
                = -infinity if j > i    (forbidden: cannot look ahead)

Why negative infinity specifically? Because softmax turns scores into probabilities via an exponential, and exp(-infinity) = 0. Adding negative infinity to a forbidden position's raw score guarantees its post-softmax attention weight is precisely zero — not "very small," precisely zero — no matter what values the Query and Key vectors happen to take. This is why causality is a hard architectural guarantee rather than something the model has to learn to approximate: it is enforced by arithmetic, identically at every layer, every head, every training example, forever.

The only other formula worth internalizing at intuition-level is layer normalization, which appears twice per decoder block. Its job is simple to state even without the full formula: after each sub-layer (attention, then feed-forward), rescale that layer's output vector so its values have a stable mean and variance before passing it to the next sub-layer. Without this, stacking dozens of blocks would cause internal values to drift to extreme magnitudes over many layers, making training numerically unstable — the same practical concern that motivates normalizing inputs in classical machine learning pipelines, just applied repeatedly, deep inside the network.

8. PRODUCTION ARCHITECTURE

When you call a hosted chat completion API from a Spring Boot service, the decoder architecture is running inside a specialized inference server, not inside your JVM. Understanding that server's shape explains API behaviors you will otherwise find mysterious — streaming, latency-per-token pricing, and context-length limits. 

┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                     Your Spring Boot Application                    │
│      ChatClient.prompt(...).stream() ──► Flux          │
└───────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────────┘

│                    Model Provider Inference Server                  │

│   │  in parallel   │        │  reuses KV cache from prior steps    │   │
│   │  (fast, batched)│        │  (sequential, latency-bound)         │   │

└───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

This two-phase shape is why production inference systems batch requests differently at each phase, why "time to first token" (dominated by prefill) and "tokens per second" (dominated by decode) are reported as separate latency metrics by every serious model provider, and why techniques like continuous batching (interleaving decode steps from many concurrent users on the same GPU) and speculative decoding (using a small, fast draft model to guess several tokens ahead, which the full decoder then verifies in parallel) exist purely to work around the fundamentally sequential nature of the decode phase described in Section 5.

9. JAVA / SPRING PERSPECTIVE

You will never implement a decoder block yourself in a Spring Boot application — that work happens inside the model provider's inference infrastructure. What you will do constantly is design your application around the architectural realities this chapter just explained, and Spring AI's API surface reflects those realities directly.

A concrete illustration: when you write reactive streaming code against a chat model in Spring AI, you are directly consuming the decode loop from Section 5 as it happens on the server: 

ChatClient chatClient = ChatClient.create(chatModel);

Flux<String> tokenStream = chatClient
        .prompt()
        .user("Summarize the causal masking mechanism in two sentences.")
        .stream()
        .content();

tokenStream.subscribe(
    token -> System.out.print(token),   // each emission ≈ one decode step
    error -> log.error("stream failed", error),
    () -> System.out.println("\n[generation complete]")
);

Each element flowing through that Flux corresponds, roughly, to one iteration of the decode loop described in Section 5.1 — the reactive, incremental nature of the API is not a stylistic choice, it is a direct reflection of the fact that the underlying model has no way to produce the full answer except by generating it one causally-constrained step at a time.

10. BEST PRACTICES

  • Stream by default for user-facing, open-ended generations. Because decode is inherently sequential, waiting for the entire response before showing anything to a user needlessly adds the full generation latency to perceived response time; streaming lets time-to-first-token dominate the perceived experience instead.
  • Budget context length like a scarce, cost-bearing resource, not a free convenience. Every additional token in the prompt is attended over at every subsequent decode step — trimming irrelevant history or retrieved context (Volume 3) has a real, compounding latency and cost effect, not just a cosmetic one.
  • Design multi-turn conversation state assuming it will be replayed as flat token sequence each turn. There is no persistent "memory" inside the decoder between API calls — every previous turn you want the model to be aware of must be explicitly included in the next request's input sequence, which is exactly why Spring AI's chat memory abstractions exist.
  • Prefer asking the model to "think step by step" via additional generated tokens over expecting it to silently revise earlier output. Because tokens cannot be un-emitted, techniques that let the model externalize intermediate reasoning as visible tokens (covered fully in the Prompting and Chain-of-Thought chapters) work with the architecture instead of against it.

11. COMMON MISTAKES

  • Assuming a chat model can "see ahead" or plan its entire response before writing the first word. A pure decoder-only model commits to each token based only on what precedes it; there is no hidden buffer holding a fully-formed answer that is merely being typed out slowly. This is why prompting a model to outline before answering measurably improves quality — the outline itself becomes visible context for the tokens that follow.
  • Confusing encoder-style embedding models with decoder-style chat models when choosing a Spring AI component. An embedding model (encoder-only, bidirectional) is the wrong tool for open-ended generation, and a chat model (decoder-only, causal) is the wrong tool for producing a single fixed-size similarity vector — mixing these up is a common early architectural mistake covered again in Volume 3.
  • Treating latency as roughly constant regardless of requested output length. Because decode is one sequential pass per output token, asking for a 2,000-token answer is not "free" the way returning a larger JSON payload from a cached database read would be — it is roughly proportional additional wall-clock time.
  • Believing more conversation history always helps. Every prior turn included in context is attended over by every subsequent decode step; beyond a point, additional history adds cost and can dilute attention on the actually-relevant recent turns, not just add "more information for free."
  • Expecting the model to correct an earlier factual error in the same response by "changing its mind" retroactively. It can only append new tokens that reference or contradict the earlier ones — it cannot edit them, which is why a model sometimes visibly contradicts itself within one long generation rather than silently self-correcting.

12. PERFORMANCE CONSIDERATIONS

The decoder's architecture directly determines the shape of latency, throughput, and memory characteristics you will design around in production:

  • Latency is dominated by output length, not input length, in the common case. Prefill processes the entire prompt in one parallel pass; decode requires one full pass per generated token. A short prompt asking for a long answer is typically slower than a long prompt asking for a short answer.
  • The KV cache trades memory for speed. Caching Keys and Values for every previous position avoids massive redundant recomputation, but that cache grows linearly with context length and must be held in GPU memory for the duration of a request — this is a primary reason very long context windows are memory-expensive to serve, independent of model size.
  • Throughput at scale depends on batching decode steps across many concurrent requests (continuous batching), because a single request's decode phase under-utilizes a GPU that is capable of far more parallel work than one sequential token stream demands.
  • Cost is typically billed per token for both input and output precisely because both phases have real, distinct compute costs — matching model size and requested output length to actual task difficulty (echoing the Best Practices from earlier AI Foundations chapters) is a direct cost lever, not a micro-optimization.

13. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

The decoder architecture itself has a narrower direct security surface than, say, the prompt-injection concerns covered under Volume 5 — but two considerations are genuinely specific to how decoders operate and are worth knowing rather than skipping over. First, because every prior message in a conversation (including the model's own earlier turns) is simply appended into the same token sequence the decoder attends over, any untrusted content injected earlier in a long-running conversation remains fully "live" context for every subsequent generation — a manipulation planted early in a session does not decay or get forgotten the way a human's attention might drift; it persists with full attention weight until it falls outside the context window. Second, at the infrastructure level, shared KV cache implementations used to accelerate multi-tenant inference servers must be carefully isolated per request; naive cache-sharing designs intended purely for the performance benefit described in Section 12 could, if implemented carelessly, risk leaking cached representations of one tenant's prompt content into another's request — a reason serious inference platforms treat KV cache isolation as a hard security boundary, not merely a performance implementation detail.

14. INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

JUNIORQ1. What is the difference between an encoder and a decoder in a Transformer?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

An encoder processes an entire input sequence at once with full, bidirectional self-attention — every token can attend to every other token, before and after it. A decoder generates a sequence one token at a time and uses causal (masked) self-attention, where each position can only attend to itself and earlier positions, never later ones, because at generation time future tokens genuinely do not exist yet.

JUNIORQ2. Why can't a decoder-only model see tokens that come after the one it is currently predicting, even during training when the full sentence is available?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

If it could see future tokens during training, it would learn to "cheat" by copying the answer instead of learning to predict it, and would then fail completely at real inference time when future tokens genuinely do not exist. The causal mask enforces the same restriction during training and inference so the model learns a task it can actually perform when generating for real.

MIDQ3. Explain, mechanically, how the causal mask is implemented inside the attention computation.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Before the softmax step in scaled dot-product attention, a mask matrix is added to the raw attention scores. Any position pair where the key position comes after the query position gets negative infinity added to its score. Since exp(-infinity) = 0, softmax assigns exactly zero attention weight to any future position, guaranteeing causality as a hard arithmetic fact rather than a learned tendency.

MIDQ4. What is a KV cache, and why does it matter for production inference latency?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Because causal masking guarantees a token's Key and Value vectors never change once computed (no future token can ever influence them), an inference engine can cache the Keys and Values for all previously processed positions and, at each new decode step, compute only the Query/Key/Value for the newest token, attending it against the cached history. Without this, generating each new token would require recomputing attention over the entire sequence from scratch, making long generations far slower and more compute-intensive than they need to be.

Interview Gold Point: Mention explicitly that the KV cache grows linearly with context length and must be held in GPU memory for the life of a request — tying it to why very long context windows are expensive to serve shows you understand the production cost implications, not just the mechanism.
MIDQ5. What is exposure bias, and why is it a consequence of how decoders are trained versus how they generate?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

During training (teacher forcing), the model always sees the true, correct prefix from the training data at every position, even if its own prediction at the previous step would have been wrong. During inference, the model must condition on its own previously generated tokens, which may contain errors. This mismatch between "always-correct training prefixes" and "possibly-imperfect inference prefixes" is exposure bias, and it is one contributor to why small early mistakes in a long generation can compound into larger drift later in the same output.

SENIORQ6. Why does prefill scale differently from decode in terms of latency, and how does this affect how you'd design a latency budget for an AI feature?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Prefill processes the entire prompt in one parallel forward pass because the causal mask already guarantees no position needs information from a not-yet-computed future step within the same pass — so it is comparatively fast even for long prompts. Decode is inherently sequential: token N+1 cannot be computed until token N exists and has been fed back through the whole stack, so its latency scales roughly linearly with the number of tokens generated. Practically, this means a feature's latency budget should separate "time to first token" (prompt-length sensitive, one-time cost) from "tokens per second" during streaming (output-length sensitive, ongoing cost), and that requesting shorter, more targeted outputs is often a more effective latency lever than trimming the prompt.

SENIORQ7. A teammate proposes sharing a single KV cache across requests from different tenants to save GPU memory. What's your response?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Naively sharing a KV cache across tenants risks leaking cached representations of one tenant's prompt content into another tenant's generation, since the cache directly encodes attention-relevant information derived from the original input tokens. The correct approach is per-request or per-session cache isolation, with sharing (if pursued at all, e.g., for a common long system prompt via prefix caching) restricted to content that is genuinely identical and non-sensitive across tenants, with the isolation boundary treated as a security requirement, not just a performance optimization to be traded off casually.

STAFFQ8. Why did the industry converge on decoder-only architectures for general-purpose LLMs instead of continuing to use the original encoder-decoder design, and what does that imply architecturally for a platform team choosing between model types?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

The encoder-decoder split assumes a clean boundary between "reading" and "writing," which fits fixed-transformation tasks like translation but does not fit open-ended, multi-turn interaction where the model's own prior output becomes context for what it writes next. A decoder-only stack treats all tokens — user input and the model's own previous generations — uniformly as one growing causal sequence, which is what allows a single architecture to scale across chat, coding, summarization, and reasoning-style tasks via prompting alone, without task-specific architectural changes. For a platform team, this implies that general-purpose conversational and agentic systems (Volume 4) should default to decoder-only chat models behind an abstraction like Spring AI's ChatModel, while encoder-only or encoder-decoder models remain the right, narrower choice for specific sub-tasks like embedding generation for retrieval (Volume 3) or fixed-format translation/summarization pipelines — the two families are not competitors so much as tools suited to structurally different problems.

16. SUMMARY

  • A decoder is a stack of identical blocks (masked multi-head self-attention, residual connections, layer normalization, feed-forward layers) that generates a sequence one token at a time, each token conditioned on everything before it.
  • The original Transformer paired an encoder (full bidirectional attention) with a decoder (causal attention) for translation; nearly every modern general-purpose LLM you call through Spring AI is decoder-only, because uniform, causal token processing generalizes to open-ended chat, coding, and reasoning without task-specific architecture changes.
  • Causality is enforced by a mask that adds negative infinity to "future" attention scores before softmax — a hard arithmetic guarantee, not a learned behavior, identical at every layer and every training step.
  • Training uses teacher forcing (always-correct prefixes, fully parallel); inference is strictly autoregressive and sequential (the model's own possibly-imperfect tokens feed back in), and this mismatch — exposure bias — is a real source of compounding errors in long generations.
  • The KV cache is the production-critical optimization that avoids recomputing attention over the entire sequence at every decode step, and it directly explains why context length drives both memory usage and cost in real inference systems.
  • Spring AI's streaming ChatClient API, its chat memory/trimming advisors, and the very existence of "time to first token" versus "tokens per second" as separate latency metrics are all direct, practical reflections of the decoder's prefill/decode architecture — this is not abstract theory, it is the mechanism behind APIs you will use in every chapter from here forward.
