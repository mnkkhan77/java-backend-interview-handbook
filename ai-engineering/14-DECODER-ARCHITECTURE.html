CHAPTER 14 — DECODER ARCHITECTURE

Volume 1 — AI Foundations · Chapter 11 of 22

The previous chapter gave every token a sense of "where" it sits in a sequence — positional encoding solved the problem that raw self-attention treats a sentence as an unordered bag of vectors. But knowing your position is useless without a rule for what you are allowed to look at. Every model you will call through Spring AI — GPT-family, Claude, Llama — generates text one token at a time, left to right, and is architecturally forbidden from peeking at tokens it has not produced yet. That forbidding rule, and the stack of layers built around it, is the decoder architecture, and it is the single most consequential design decision behind every chat completion API you will ever call.

1. MOTIVATION

Every time your Java code calls a chat model and streams a response back token by token, you are watching a decoder execute. Understanding why that architecture looks the way it does is not academic trivia for this book — it directly explains three things you will deal with constantly as an AI engineer: why generation is sequential and therefore latency scales with output length, why a model can never "go back and edit" a token it already committed to, and why the ChatClient.stream() API in Spring AI (covered fully in Volume 2) exists and returns a reactive stream of partial tokens rather than one big blob.

Up to this point in the series you have learned how a Transformer represents meaning (embeddings), how it lets tokens exchange information (self-attention), and how it knows the order of tokens (positional encoding, Chapter 13). None of those pieces, on their own, tell you how a model actually writes — how it goes from "understanding" an input to producing a coherent, growing sequence of new tokens, one at a time, each conditioned on everything before it. That is the job of the decoder stack, and it is the architecture family that every large language model you will integrate through Spring AI belongs to.

There is also a practical, career-relevant reason to master this chapter: architecture interviews for AI engineering roles routinely probe the distinction between encoder, decoder, and encoder-decoder models, because getting it wrong signals that a candidate has only used LLMs as an API consumer and never understood what is happening underneath the HTTP call. This chapter gives you the vocabulary and the mental model to speak about that distinction with the same confidence you already have about, say, the difference between a stateless and a stateful service.

2. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

2.1 The Original Transformer Was Two Halves, Not One

The 2017 paper that introduced the Transformer architecture was written to solve machine translation — turning a sentence in one language into a sentence in another. Translation has a natural two-part shape: first you need to fully understand the source sentence (read the whole thing, in both directions, before committing to anything), and then you need to generate the target sentence one word at a time, since a translation is produced left to right and each word you write constrains what comes next. The original architecture mirrored this shape exactly with two stacked components: an encoder, which processes the entire input sequence at once with full, bidirectional self-attention (every token can look at every other token, left and right), and a decoder, which generates the output sequence one token at a time, attending both to its own previously generated tokens and to the encoder's finished representation of the input. This is called an encoder-decoder (or sequence-to-sequence) architecture, and it is still the right shape for tasks with a clean "read fully, then write" structure — translation, summarization with a fixed source document, and speech-to-text.

2.2 The Decoder-Only Turn

The generative AI you interact with daily — GPT-family models, Claude, Llama, Mistral — does not use that two-part shape at all. Starting with GPT (2018) and cemented by GPT-2 and GPT-3, researchers found something important: if your real goal is open-ended text generation rather than a fixed translation-style transformation, you can drop the encoder half entirely and keep only a stack of decoder blocks. Feed the model a prompt as if it were "already-generated" text, and let it continue generating from there using the exact same left-to-right, causally-masked self-attention mechanism a decoder uses to generate translated output. This is called a decoder-only architecture, and it turned out to scale better, train more simply (a single, uniform stack of identical blocks instead of two different components with a bridge between them), and generalize to virtually any text task — question answering, coding, summarization, chat — by simply reframing the task as "continue this text," a technique you will see formalized later in this volume as prompting.

2.3 Why "Decoder-Only" Won for General-Purpose LLMs

The encoder-decoder split makes an implicit assumption: that there is a clean moment where "reading" ends and "writing" begins. Real conversational AI does not have that clean split — a chat model is simultaneously reading your latest message, remembering everything said before, and writing its response, and the boundary between "context" and "generation" is fluid (the model's own previous turns become context for its next turn). A decoder-only stack handles this uniformly: everything, whether it originated from the user or from the model itself, is just a sequence of tokens flowing through the same causally-masked layers. This uniformity is precisely why a single architecture family could scale from GPT-2 to the frontier chat models you call through Spring AI today, without needing a fundamentally different design for "understanding" versus "generating."

Whether it is encoder-only, encoder-decoder, or decoder-only determines whether it can see the future of its own input (encoder: yes; decoder: never), whether it produces a fixed-size vector or a growing sequence, and whether the right Spring AI abstraction is an embedding model call or a ChatModel call. Every chat-completion API you will ever integrate is decoder-only under the hood — this chapter is the architecture behind that entire product category.

3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

Strip away the history, and the decoder architecture exists to solve one precise engineering problem:

Build a model that can generate a sequence of tokens one at a time, where each new token is conditioned on everything generated so far (and the original prompt), while training that model efficiently on massive datasets in parallel — without ever letting the model "cheat" by seeing tokens it has not generated yet.

Unpack the two halves of that sentence, because both are load-bearing. The first half — "conditioned on everything generated so far" — is what makes output coherent: token 500 of a response needs to be aware of tokens 1 through 499, not just the last few words, or a model's writing would drift into incoherence within a sentence. Self-attention (from the Attention Mechanism and Multi-Head Attention chapters) is exactly the mechanism that lets every position look back at every prior position — the decoder architecture's specific contribution is restricting that lookback to be only backward, never forward.

The second half — "efficiently, in parallel, without cheating" — is a genuinely subtle systems problem. During training, you have the full target sequence available (you know what the "correct" next word is at every position, because it is sitting right there in your training corpus). It would be wasteful to train the way you generate — one token at a time, waiting for each prediction before computing the next. The decoder architecture's central engineering trick, causal masking, lets you feed an entire sequence through the network in a single parallel pass during training while mathematically guaranteeing that the network's prediction for position i could not have used any information from position i+1 or beyond. Get this wrong — leak even a single future token during training — and the model will appear to perform brilliantly during training while being useless at real generation time, because at generation time the future genuinely does not exist yet.

4. THEORY

4.1 The Core Idea: Causality as an Architectural Constraint, Not a Convention

The most important mental model in this chapter is this: causality in a decoder is not a soft guideline the model tries to follow — it is a hard, mathematical constraint baked into the attention computation itself, the same way a Java compiler enforces type safety rather than merely recommending it. Think of a decoder block as a very disciplined committee meeting where each participant (token position) is allowed to ask "what did everyone who spoke before me say?" but is physically prevented from hearing anyone who has not spoken yet, no matter how much they might want to. This is enforced identically at every layer, every head, every training step — there is no code path anywhere in a standard decoder where a future token's information can leak backward.

4.2 Autoregression: Generation as Repeated Conditional Prediction

The decoder implements what statisticians call an autoregressive model: it predicts the probability of the next token given all previous tokens, and it generates a full sequence by repeatedly sampling one token from that distribution, appending it to the sequence, and repeating. Formally, the decoder learns to approximate: 

P(token_1, token_2, ..., token_n)
    = P(token_1)
    × P(token_2 | token_1)
    × P(token_3 | token_1, token_2)
    × ...
    × P(token_n | token_1, ..., token_n-1)

This is exactly the "predict the next word" framing from the What Is Intelligence chapter, now given architectural teeth. Every one of those conditional probabilities on the right is computed by the same decoder stack, just with a longer prefix each time. This is also why chat models feel like they are "thinking as they type" — they genuinely are recomputing a probability distribution fresh at every single token, conditioned on everything so far, including their own words from a moment ago.

4.3 Teacher Forcing vs. Autoregressive Inference — Two Different Modes of the Same Network

A subtlety that trips up many engineers: the decoder runs in two structurally different modes depending on whether it is training or generating, even though it is the identical set of weights.

AspectTraining (Teacher Forcing)Inference (Autoregressive Generation)
Input at position iThe true token from the training data, even if the model's own prediction at i-1 was wrongThe model's own previously generated token — errors can compound
ParallelismEntire sequence processed in one forward pass (causal mask enforces no cheating)Strictly sequential — token n+1 cannot be computed until token n exists
Cost per sequenceOne forward + one backward pass regardless of sequence lengthOne forward pass per generated token — cost scales with output length
Failure modeModel never sees its own mistakes propagateExposure bias — a single bad token can nudge everything after it off-distribution

This training/inference asymmetry — called exposure bias in the literature — is a real, known limitation: the model is trained on a diet of always-correct prefixes but must generate from its own, sometimes-imperfect prefixes at inference time. It is one root contributor (alongside the "plausibility versus truth" mechanism from earlier chapters) to why small early errors in a long generation can snowball into larger ones later in the same response.

4.4 The Anatomy of a Single Decoder Block

A decoder-only model is a tall stack of identical blocks (GPT-3-class models use dozens of them). Each block performs the same sequence of operations, and understanding one block means understanding the entire stack:

  1. Masked multi-head self-attention — every position attends to itself and all earlier positions (never later ones), using the multi-head mechanism from the Multi-Head Attention chapter.
  2. Residual connection + layer normalization — the attention output is added back to the block's input (a "skip connection") and normalized, which is what makes it possible to stack dozens of these blocks without training becoming unstable.
  3. Position-wise feed-forward network — a small two-layer neural network applied identically and independently to every position, giving the model additional capacity to transform each token's representation.
  4. Another residual connection + layer normalization — same stabilizing pattern as after attention.

Stack this block N times, and the final block's output at each position is projected through a linear layer into vocabulary-sized logits, then turned into a probability distribution over the entire vocabulary via softmax — the next-token distribution described in Section 4.2.

5. INTERNAL WORKING — STEP BY STEP

5.1 From Prompt to First Generated Token

Trace an actual request end to end, the way it happens when your Spring AI application calls a chat model:

  1. Tokenization (formalized next chapter) turns your prompt text into a sequence of token IDs.
  2. Embedding + positional encoding (Chapters covered earlier) turn each token ID into a vector that also encodes its position in the sequence.
  3. This sequence of vectors is fed through the entire decoder stack in one parallel pass — this phase is called prefill. Because the causal mask already guarantees no position can see the future, the whole prompt can be processed simultaneously, which is why prefill is comparatively fast even for long prompts.
  4. The final block's output at the last position of the prompt is projected to a probability distribution over the vocabulary. A token is sampled from this distribution (using temperature, top-p, or other sampling strategies covered in the Sampling Strategies chapter later in this volume).
  5. That newly sampled token is appended to the sequence, and the entire sequence — original prompt plus the one new token — is fed through the decoder stack again to produce the next token. This phase, repeated once per output token, is called decode.
  6. Steps 4–5 repeat until a stop condition is reached (a special end-of-sequence token, a max-token limit, or an application-defined stop sequence).

Notice the architectural asymmetry this reveals: prefill is one parallel pass over potentially thousands of prompt tokens; decode is many sequential passes, one per output token, each technically reprocessing the whole growing sequence. Real inference engines do not naively recompute everything from scratch at every decode step — they exploit a critical optimization called the KV cache, covered next, which is one of the most important production-performance facts in this entire book.

5.2 Why the Causal Mask Alone Isn't Enough — The KV Cache

Inside masked self-attention, each token position computes three vectors: a Query, a Key, and a Value (from the Attention Mechanism chapter). Here is the key operational insight: once a token's Key and Value vectors are computed at a given layer, they never change — a token at position 5 will have the exact same Key and Value at layer 12 no matter how many more tokens get appended after it, because causal masking guarantees position 5 was never allowed to see anything past itself in the first place. This means that during decode, instead of recomputing Key and Value vectors for the entire growing sequence at every single step, an inference engine can cache the Keys and Values computed for all previous positions and simply compute the Query, Key, and Value for the one brand-new token, then attend that new Query against the full cached history. This is the KV cache, and without it, generating a 1,000-token response would require roughly 1,000× more redundant computation than it actually does in a production system.

5.3 Edge Cases Worth Reasoning Through Yourself

  • What happens if the model needs to "reconsider" an earlier token? It architecturally cannot — once a token is emitted and appended to the sequence, it is fixed context for everything after it. This is precisely why techniques like chain-of-thought prompting (Volume 1, later chapter) and self-correction patterns work by generating additional tokens that reference and revise earlier reasoning, rather than by editing anything in place.
  • What happens at the very first position, where there is no "previous" token? The causal mask allows position 1 to attend only to itself — this is well-defined and simply means the first token's representation is built purely from its own embedding plus whatever the prompt's system/instruction tokens preceding it contain.
  • Does a longer prompt make every subsequent generated token more expensive? Yes — every decode step attends over the full cached history, so attention cost per generated token grows with total context length, which is a direct driver of the cost and latency behavior discussed in Section 12.

6. MATHEMATICS — THE INTUITION BEFORE THE FORMULAS

You already have the attention formula from earlier chapters: 

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( (Q · K^T) / sqrt(d_k) ) · V

The decoder's entire contribution to this formula is a single additive term applied before the softmax: a mask matrix that forces the attention weight of any "future" position to become exactly zero after softmax. Concretely, before the softmax is applied, every entry of the raw score matrix that corresponds to "position i attending to position j where j > i" has negative infinity added to it: 

MaskedAttention(Q, K, V) = softmax( (Q · K^T) / sqrt(d_k)  +  M ) · V

  where M[i][j] = 0        if j <= i   (allowed: look at self or earlier)
                = -infinity if j > i    (forbidden: cannot look ahead)

Why negative infinity specifically? Because softmax turns scores into probabilities via an exponential, and exp(-infinity) = 0. Adding negative infinity to a forbidden position's raw score guarantees its post-softmax attention weight is precisely zero — not "very small," precisely zero — no matter what values the Query and Key vectors happen to take. This is why causality is a hard architectural guarantee rather than something the model has to learn to approximate: it is enforced by arithmetic, identically at every layer, every head, every training example, forever.

The only other formula worth internalizing at intuition-level is layer normalization, which appears twice per decoder block. Its job is simple to state even without the full formula: after each sub-layer (attention, then feed-forward), rescale that layer's output vector so its values have a stable mean and variance before passing it to the next sub-layer. Without this, stacking dozens of blocks would cause internal values to drift to extreme magnitudes over many layers, making training numerically unstable — the same practical concern that motivates normalizing inputs in classical machine learning pipelines, just applied repeatedly, deep inside the network.

8. PRODUCTION ARCHITECTURE

When you call a hosted chat completion API from a Spring Boot service, the decoder architecture is running inside a specialized inference server, not inside your JVM. Understanding that server's shape explains API behaviors you will otherwise find mysterious — streaming, latency-per-token pricing, and context-length limits.

This two-phase shape is why production inference systems batch requests differently at each phase, why "time to first token" (dominated by prefill) and "tokens per second" (dominated by decode) are reported as separate latency metrics by every serious model provider, and why techniques like continuous batching (interleaving decode steps from many concurrent users on the same GPU) and speculative decoding (using a small, fast draft model to guess several tokens ahead, which the full decoder then verifies in parallel) exist purely to work around the fundamentally sequential nature of the decode phase described in Section 5.

You will never implement a decoder block yourself in a Spring Boot application — that work happens inside the model provider's inference infrastructure. A concrete illustration: when you write reactive streaming code against a chat model in Spring AI, you are directly consuming the decode loop from Section 5 as it happens on the server: 

ChatClient chatClient = ChatClient.create(chatModel);

Flux<String> tokenStream = chatClient
        .prompt()
        .user("Summarize the causal masking mechanism in two sentences.")
        .stream()
        .content();

tokenStream.subscribe(
    token -> System.out.print(token),   // each emission ≈ one decode step
    error -> log.error("stream failed", error),
    () -> System.out.println("\n[generation complete]")
);

Each element flowing through that Flux corresponds, roughly, to one iteration of the decode loop described in Section 5.1 — the reactive, incremental nature of the API is not a stylistic choice, it is a direct reflection of the fact that the underlying model has no way to produce the full answer except by generating it one causally-constrained step at a time.

10. BEST PRACTICES

  • Stream by default for user-facing, open-ended generations. Because decode is inherently sequential, waiting for the entire response before showing anything to a user needlessly adds the full generation latency to perceived response time; streaming lets time-to-first-token dominate the perceived experience instead.
  • Budget context length like a scarce, cost-bearing resource, not a free convenience. Every additional token in the prompt is attended over at every subsequent decode step — trimming irrelevant history or retrieved context (Volume 3) has a real, compounding latency and cost effect, not just a cosmetic one.
  • Design multi-turn conversation state assuming it will be replayed as flat token sequence each turn. There is no persistent "memory" inside the decoder between API calls — every previous turn you want the model to be aware of must be explicitly included in the next request's input sequence, which is exactly why Spring AI's chat memory abstractions exist.
  • Prefer asking the model to "think step by step" via additional generated tokens over expecting it to silently revise earlier output. Because tokens cannot be un-emitted, techniques that let the model externalize intermediate reasoning as visible tokens (covered fully in the Prompting and Chain-of-Thought chapters) work with the architecture instead of against it.

11. COMMON MISTAKES

  • Assuming a chat model can "see ahead" or plan its entire response before writing the first word. A pure decoder-only model commits to each token based only on what precedes it; there is no hidden buffer holding a fully-formed answer that is merely being typed out slowly. This is why prompting a model to outline before answering measurably improves quality — the outline itself becomes visible context for the tokens that follow.
  • Confusing encoder-style embedding models with decoder-style chat models when choosing a Spring AI component. An embedding model (encoder-only, bidirectional) is the wrong tool for open-ended generation, and a chat model (decoder-only, causal) is the wrong tool for producing a single fixed-size similarity vector — mixing these up is a common early architectural mistake covered again in Volume 3.
  • Treating latency as roughly constant regardless of requested output length. Because decode is one sequential pass per output token, asking for a 2,000-token answer is not "free" the way returning a larger JSON payload from a cached database read would be — it is roughly proportional additional wall-clock time.
  • Believing more conversation history always helps. Every prior turn included in context is attended over by every subsequent decode step; beyond a point, additional history adds cost and can dilute attention on the actually-relevant recent turns, not just add "more information for free."
  • Expecting the model to correct an earlier factual error in the same response by "changing its mind" retroactively. It can only append new tokens that reference or contradict the earlier ones — it cannot edit them, which is why a model sometimes visibly contradicts itself within one long generation rather than silently self-correcting.

12. PERFORMANCE CONSIDERATIONS

The decoder's architecture directly determines the shape of latency, throughput, and memory characteristics you will design around in production:

  • Latency is dominated by output length, not input length, in the common case. Prefill processes the entire prompt in one parallel pass; decode requires one full pass per generated token. A short prompt asking for a long answer is typically slower than a long prompt asking for a short answer.
  • The KV cache trades memory for speed. Caching Keys and Values for every previous position avoids massive redundant recomputation, but that cache grows linearly with context length and must be held in GPU memory for the duration of a request — this is a primary reason very long context windows are memory-expensive to serve, independent of model size.
  • Throughput at scale depends on batching decode steps across many concurrent requests (continuous batching), because a single request's decode phase under-utilizes a GPU that is capable of far more parallel work than one sequential token stream demands.
  • Cost is typically billed per token for both input and output precisely because both phases have real, distinct compute costs — matching model size and requested output length to actual task difficulty (echoing the Best Practices from earlier AI Foundations chapters) is a direct cost lever, not a micro-optimization.

13. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

The decoder architecture itself has a narrower direct security surface than, say, the prompt-injection concerns covered under Volume 5 — but two considerations are genuinely specific to how decoders operate and are worth knowing rather than skipping over. First, because every prior message in a conversation (including the model's own earlier turns) is simply appended into the same token sequence the decoder attends over, any untrusted content injected earlier in a long-running conversation remains fully "live" context for every subsequent generation — a manipulation planted early in a session does not decay or get forgotten the way a human's attention might drift; it persists with full attention weight until it falls outside the context window. Second, at the infrastructure level, shared KV cache implementations used to accelerate multi-tenant inference servers must be carefully isolated per request; naive cache-sharing designs intended purely for the performance benefit described in Section 12 could, if implemented carelessly, risk leaking cached representations of one tenant's prompt content into another's request — a reason serious inference platforms treat KV cache isolation as a hard security boundary, not merely a performance implementation detail.

14. INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

JUNIORQ1. What is the difference between an encoder and a decoder in a Transformer?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

An encoder processes an entire input sequence at once with full, bidirectional self-attention — every token can attend to every other token, before and after it. A decoder generates a sequence one token at a time and uses causal (masked) self-attention, where each position can only attend to itself and earlier positions, never later ones, because at generation time future tokens genuinely do not exist yet.

JUNIORQ2. Why can't a decoder-only model see tokens that come after the one it is currently predicting, even during training when the full sentence is available?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

If it could see future tokens during training, it would learn to "cheat" by copying the answer instead of learning to predict it, and would then fail completely at real inference time when future tokens genuinely do not exist. The causal mask enforces the same restriction during training and inference so the model learns a task it can actually perform when generating for real.

MIDQ3. Explain, mechanically, how the causal mask is implemented inside the attention computation.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Before the softmax step in scaled dot-product attention, a mask matrix is added to the raw attention scores. Any position pair where the key position comes after the query position gets negative infinity added to its score. Since exp(-infinity) = 0, softmax assigns exactly zero attention weight to any future position, guaranteeing causality as a hard arithmetic fact rather than a learned tendency.

MIDQ4. What is a KV cache, and why does it matter for production inference latency?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Because causal masking guarantees a token's Key and Value vectors never change once computed (no future token can ever influence them), an inference engine can cache the Keys and Values for all previously processed positions and, at each new decode step, compute only the Query/Key/Value for the newest token, attending it against the cached history. Without this, generating each new token would require recomputing attention over the entire sequence from scratch, making long generations far slower and more compute-intensive than they need to be.

Interview Gold Point: Mention explicitly that the KV cache grows linearly with context length and must be held in GPU memory for the life of a request — tying it to why very long context windows are expensive to serve shows you understand the production cost implications, not just the mechanism.
MIDQ5. What is exposure bias, and why is it a consequence of how decoders are trained versus how they generate?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

During training (teacher forcing), the model always sees the true, correct prefix from the training data at every position, even if its own prediction at the previous step would have been wrong. During inference, the model must condition on its own previously generated tokens, which may contain errors. This mismatch between "always-correct training prefixes" and "possibly-imperfect inference prefixes" is exposure bias, and it is one contributor to why small early mistakes in a long generation can compound into larger drift later in the same output.

SENIORQ6. Why does prefill scale differently from decode in terms of latency, and how does this affect how you'd design a latency budget for an AI feature?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Prefill processes the entire prompt in one parallel forward pass because the causal mask already guarantees no position needs information from a not-yet-computed future step within the same pass — so it is comparatively fast even for long prompts. Decode is inherently sequential: token N+1 cannot be computed until token N exists and has been fed back through the whole stack, so its latency scales roughly linearly with the number of tokens generated. Practically, this means a feature's latency budget should separate "time to first token" (prompt-length sensitive, one-time cost) from "tokens per second" during streaming (output-length sensitive, ongoing cost), and that requesting shorter, more targeted outputs is often a more effective latency lever than trimming the prompt.

SENIORQ7. A teammate proposes sharing a single KV cache across requests from different tenants to save GPU memory. What's your response?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Naively sharing a KV cache across tenants risks leaking cached representations of one tenant's prompt content into another tenant's generation, since the cache directly encodes attention-relevant information derived from the original input tokens. The correct approach is per-request or per-session cache isolation, with sharing (if pursued at all, e.g., for a common long system prompt via prefix caching) restricted to content that is genuinely identical and non-sensitive across tenants, with the isolation boundary treated as a security requirement, not just a performance optimization to be traded off casually.

STAFFQ8. Why did the industry converge on decoder-only architectures for general-purpose LLMs instead of continuing to use the original encoder-decoder design, and what does that imply architecturally for a platform team choosing between model types?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

The encoder-decoder split assumes a clean boundary between "reading" and "writing," which fits fixed-transformation tasks like translation but does not fit open-ended, multi-turn interaction where the model's own prior output becomes context for what it writes next. A decoder-only stack treats all tokens — user input and the model's own previous generations — uniformly as one growing causal sequence, which is what allows a single architecture to scale across chat, coding, summarization, and reasoning-style tasks via prompting alone, without task-specific architectural changes. For a platform team, this implies that general-purpose conversational and agentic systems (Volume 4) should default to decoder-only chat models behind an abstraction like Spring AI's ChatModel, while encoder-only or encoder-decoder models remain the right, narrower choice for specific sub-tasks like embedding generation for retrieval (Volume 3) or fixed-format translation/summarization pipelines — the two families are not competitors so much as tools suited to structurally different problems.

JUNIORQ9. What shape does the causal mask matrix have, and how would you generate it in code for a sequence of length n?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

The causal mask is an n×n lower-triangular matrix: for row i (the query position) and column j (the key position), the entry is "allowed" (0) when j ≤ i and "forbidden" (-infinity) when j > i. In code it is typically built once and reused:

mask = full((n, n), -infinity)
for i in range(n):
    for j in range(i + 1):
        mask[i][j] = 0
# equivalently: mask = triu(full((n,n), -inf), diagonal=1)

This matrix is added element-wise to the raw QK^T attention scores before softmax, so every "future" entry collapses to zero probability regardless of what the Query/Key dot products actually computed.

MIDQ10. Is the causal mask the same across every attention head and every layer, or does it differ per head/layer?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

The causal mask itself is identical across every head and every layer — it is a structural property of position, not something learned or varied. What differs per head and layer are the Query, Key, and Value projection weights, which determine how much attention weight is distributed among the allowed (non-masked) positions. The mask only ever decides which positions are eligible to receive any weight at all; the weights themselves are learned and layer/head-specific.

MIDQ11. How does causal masking interact with padding masks when you batch sequences of different lengths together?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

When sequences of different lengths are batched, shorter sequences are padded to match the longest one, and a separate padding mask marks which positions are real tokens versus filler. Both masks are combined additively before softmax: a position is only attendable if it satisfies both "not in the future" (causal mask) and "not a padding token" (padding mask). If either mask marks a position as forbidden, negative infinity is added and that position receives zero attention weight — the two masks compose rather than compete.

SENIORQ12. Why is the causal mask applied additively via negative infinity before softmax rather than by simply zeroing out attention weights after softmax?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Zeroing weights after softmax would leave the softmax denominator computed over all positions, including forbidden future ones — the remaining "allowed" weights would no longer sum to 1, and the forbidden positions would have already influenced the normalization even though their final weight is set to zero. Adding negative infinity before softmax ensures forbidden positions contribute nothing to the exponential sum in the first place, so the surviving probability mass is correctly renormalized only over legitimately visible positions. It is the difference between "excluding a candidate from the vote" versus "counting their vote and then discarding it after."

JUNIORQ13. What made GPT-1 and GPT-2 "decoder-only," as opposed to following the original Transformer's two-part encoder-decoder shape?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

GPT dropped the encoder half entirely and kept only a stack of causally-masked decoder blocks. Instead of encoding a source sequence and generating a separate target sequence, GPT simply treats the prompt as the beginning of the sequence it is continuing — there is no cross-attention into a separate encoder representation, only self-attention over the single growing token stream (prompt plus generated tokens so far).

JUNIORQ14. If a decoder-only model has no encoder, how does it "read" and understand the prompt at all?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

It reads the prompt the same way it reads its own previously generated tokens — as part of the one causal sequence fed through masked self-attention. During prefill, the entire prompt is processed in parallel through every decoder block, and each prompt token's representation is built by attending over the earlier prompt tokens. There is no separate "understanding" phase; comprehension and generation both happen through the identical mechanism, just applied to different portions of the same token sequence.

MIDQ15. Compare BERT's (encoder-only) training objective to GPT's (decoder-only) training objective, and explain why each objective fits its architecture.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

BERT is trained with masked language modeling: random tokens in the input are hidden, and the model must predict them using context from both directions — which requires full bidirectional attention, i.e. an encoder. GPT is trained with next-token prediction: given a prefix, predict the single next token, using only leftward context — which is exactly what causal self-attention provides. Each objective is not just compatible with its architecture, it is only well-defined given that architecture: you cannot do meaningful bidirectional mask-filling with a causal-only model, and next-token prediction with full bidirectional attention would trivially "see the answer" sitting in the input.

MIDQ16. Why do encoder-decoder models like T5 still exist today, and when would you choose one over a decoder-only chat model?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Encoder-decoder models remain a good fit when a task has a genuinely fixed, well-defined "source" that should be fully understood with bidirectional attention before any generation begins — canonical examples are machine translation and structured summarization of a fixed input document. The encoder can build a complete, non-causal representation of the source once, and the decoder repeatedly attends into that fixed representation while generating, which can be more parameter-efficient for narrowly-scoped transformation tasks. A decoder-only chat model is the better default for open-ended, multi-turn, or agentic use cases where "source" and "generation" are not cleanly separable.

SENIORQ17. What specific scaling and engineering advantages does a decoder-only stack have over an encoder-decoder design at very large parameter counts?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

A decoder-only model is architecturally uniform — one block type repeated N times — which simplifies distributed training infrastructure (pipeline/tensor parallelism partitions identical blocks rather than two structurally different components with a cross-attention bridge between them). It also means every parameter is exercised on every token, whereas an encoder-decoder split allocates capacity between two components whose optimal ratio isn't obvious and can waste parameters if the split doesn't match the task mix. Finally, uniform self-attention-only blocks make it straightforward to reuse the exact same training data format (raw text continuation) across every task via prompting, avoiding the need for task-specific encoder-decoder data curation at massive scale.

JUNIORQ18. What is "prefill" in the context of decoder generation?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Prefill is the phase where the entire input prompt is processed through the decoder stack in a single parallel forward pass, producing the Key/Value vectors for every prompt position (populating the KV cache) and the next-token probability distribution for the very first generated token. Because the causal mask already prevents any position from seeing the future within that one pass, all prompt tokens can be processed simultaneously rather than one at a time.

MIDQ19. Walk through, step by step, what happens computationally when a decoder-only model receives a 500-token prompt and is asked to generate 100 tokens.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

First, the 500 prompt tokens are embedded and fed through every decoder block in one parallel pass (prefill); this populates the KV cache with Keys/Values for all 500 positions and produces logits for position 500, from which the first new token (position 501) is sampled. Then decode begins: token 501 is embedded, fed through the stack, and at each layer its Query attends over the cached Keys/Values for positions 1–500 plus its own newly computed Key/Value, producing logits used to sample token 502. This repeats — each new token attends over an ever-growing cache — for 100 iterations, so 100 sequential forward passes occur after the single prefill pass, each one only computing fresh Q/K/V for exactly one new position.

MIDQ20. What is the "decode phase," and why does each decode step require a full pass through every layer just to produce one new token?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

The decode phase is the repeated loop that runs after prefill: at each iteration, exactly one new token is embedded and pushed through the entire decoder stack — every block, every layer — because each layer builds progressively richer representations on top of the previous layer's output, and the LM head at the very end needs the final layer's representation to produce next-token logits. There's no shortcut that skips layers, because each layer's transformation is a genuine function of the one below it; you can't jump straight to layer 40's output without computing layers 1 through 39 for that new token first.

SENIORQ21. How does the sequence length passed into the model differ between the prefill call and each subsequent decode call, and how does the KV cache change that?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Without a KV cache, a naive implementation would re-feed the entire growing sequence (prompt + all tokens generated so far) at every decode step, so sequence length passed in would grow with each iteration: 501, 502, 503... tokens. With the KV cache, only the single newest token is fed in at each decode step — the Keys and Values for every earlier position are already cached from prior passes and simply reused, so the "new work" per decode step is O(1) tokens of fresh computation combined with an attention lookup over an O(n)-sized cache, rather than O(n) tokens of fresh computation at every step.

SENIORQ22. If you disabled the KV cache entirely, how would per-token decode cost scale with generated sequence length, and why?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Without caching, generating the k-th token would require recomputing Keys and Values for all k-1 preceding positions from scratch at every layer, then attending — so the cost of a single decode step grows linearly with current sequence length, and the total cost to generate n tokens grows quadratically (roughly 1 + 2 + 3 + ... + n ∝ n²) instead of linearly. For a 1,000-token generation this is the difference between roughly 1,000 units of redundant work and 500,000 — which is why every serious inference engine implements KV caching as a baseline, non-optional optimization rather than a nice-to-have.

JUNIORQ23. What does the "language modeling head" do at the end of a decoder stack?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

The LM head takes the final decoder block's output vector at a position and projects it, via a single linear layer, from the model's hidden dimension into a vector of vocabulary-size logits — one raw score per possible next token. A softmax is then applied to turn those logits into a genuine probability distribution over the entire vocabulary, from which a token is sampled (or the top one taken, under greedy decoding).

MIDQ24. What is weight tying between the input embedding matrix and the output LM head, and why is it commonly used?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Weight tying reuses the same matrix for both the input token embedding lookup (token ID → vector) and the output LM head projection (vector → vocabulary logits), just transposed. This roughly halves the parameter count consumed by embeddings, which can be a substantial fraction of total parameters for large vocabularies, and empirically tends to improve training stability and quality because it forces the model to use one consistent geometric space for "what a token means" whether it's being read in or predicted out.

MIDQ25. Why is the final linear projection in the LM head sized [hidden_dim × vocab_size], and what does that imply about compute cost as vocabulary size grows?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

The projection must map a hidden_dim-sized vector into one logit per vocabulary entry, so the weight matrix necessarily has hidden_dim × vocab_size parameters, and computing the logits is a matrix-vector multiply of that size at every single position. As vocabulary size grows (modern tokenizers often exceed 100K–250K entries), this final projection becomes a non-trivial fraction of total compute per forward pass — at decode time, where only one new position is processed per step, the LM head's cost relative to the rest of that step's work is proportionally larger than during prefill, which processes many positions at once and amortizes better.

SENIORQ26. How does the softmax over the vocabulary interact with the sampling strategy chosen at inference time — is the full softmax always computed?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium at senior level

Conceptually every decoding strategy operates on the same underlying full logit vector, but in practice a full, numerically stable softmax over the entire vocabulary is usually computed once per step (it's a cheap elementwise-plus-normalize operation compared to the matrix multiplies around it), and then the chosen decoding strategy — greedy argmax, top-k truncation, top-p truncation, or temperature rescaling — filters or reweights that distribution before sampling. Some highly latency-sensitive serving stacks do fuse or approximate parts of this (e.g., only computing softmax over a pre-filtered top-k logit subset) as a micro-optimization, but functionally the model always produces a distribution over the full vocabulary; the decoding strategy decides what to do with it.

STAFFQ27. Design implication: how would you reduce LM-head compute cost for a very large vocabulary (e.g., 250K tokens) at serving time without changing model quality?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

A few production levers exist without retraining: fuse the final linear projection and softmax into a single optimized kernel to avoid materializing the full logit vector in memory twice; during decode (where only one position needs logits per step), skip computing logits for intermediate positions entirely and only project the final position's hidden state; and if the application only needs top-k or top-p sampling, some serving stacks compute an approximate top-candidates set via a cheaper mechanism before falling back to exact logits only for the surviving candidates. None of these change the model's weights or outputs — they only avoid redundant computation, which is the same "don't do unnecessary work" principle behind the KV cache, just applied to the output projection instead of the attention mechanism.

JUNIORQ28. What is greedy decoding, and what's a simple example where it produces suboptimal output?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Greedy decoding always picks the single highest-probability token at every step, with no exploration of alternatives. It can produce suboptimal output because the locally best token at step 1 doesn't always lead to the globally best sentence — for example, given the prefix "The weather today is", greedy might pick "nice" (highest single-step probability) even though "absolutely wonderful" (a lower-probability first word, "absolutely," followed by a higher combined-probability continuation) would have formed a more fluent, higher total-likelihood sentence. Greedy decoding has no mechanism to look ahead or reconsider — it's myopic by construction.

JUNIORQ29. What does "temperature" do to a model's output distribution before sampling?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Temperature rescales the logits before softmax by dividing them by a temperature value T: logits / T. A temperature below 1.0 sharpens the distribution (makes the model more confident/deterministic, pushing probability mass toward the already-highest-scoring tokens), while a temperature above 1.0 flattens it (makes the model more random, giving lower-probability tokens a more competitive chance of being sampled). Temperature = 1.0 leaves the distribution unchanged from the model's raw output.

JUNIORQ30. What is top-k sampling in plain terms?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Top-k sampling restricts sampling to only the k highest-probability tokens at each step, discarding everything else and renormalizing the probabilities among that fixed-size shortlist before sampling. It prevents the model from ever picking a wildly implausible token from the distribution's long tail, while still allowing some randomness among the genuinely competitive candidates.

MIDQ31. Explain top-p (nucleus) sampling and how it differs from top-k, with a worked numeric example.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Top-p sampling includes the smallest set of highest-probability tokens whose cumulative probability reaches a threshold p, then samples only from that set — the size of the candidate set varies dynamically per step, unlike top-k's fixed size. Example: suppose four candidate tokens have probabilities 0.50, 0.30, 0.15, 0.05. With top-p = 0.8, you'd include "0.50" and "0.30" (cumulative 0.80) and stop there — only two candidates survive. With top-k = 3 on the same distribution, you'd keep three candidates (0.50, 0.30, 0.15) regardless of how much cumulative probability that represents. Top-p adapts to how "peaked" or "flat" the distribution is at each individual step; top-k always keeps the same count even when the model is extremely confident (wasting slots on near-zero-probability tokens) or extremely uncertain (unfairly excluding several plausible tokens).

MIDQ32. Explain beam search and why it is rarely used for open-ended chat generation despite being common in translation.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Beam search maintains the top-B partial sequences (beams) at every step instead of just one, expanding each beam by all plausible next tokens, scoring the resulting sequences, and keeping only the best B again — approximating a search for the overall highest-probability full sequence rather than committing greedily one token at a time. In translation this works well because there is usually one "correct" target sentence and beam search reliably finds a high-likelihood one. For open-ended chat, beam search tends to produce bland, repetitive, overly "safe" text — because the highest-likelihood sequence in an open-ended distribution is often generic and repetitive (the model hedges toward common phrasing), whereas sampling-based methods (top-p, temperature) better preserve the diversity and creativity real conversation needs.

MIDQ33. Worked example: given logits [2.0, 1.0, 0.5, 0.1, -1.0] for 5 candidate tokens, show qualitatively how temperature 0.1 versus temperature 1.5 changes the resulting distribution.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

At temperature 0.1, dividing each logit by 0.1 multiplies the gaps between them by 10× (e.g., 2.0 becomes 20, 1.0 becomes 10), so after softmax the top token's probability approaches ~1.0 and the rest collapse toward 0 — the distribution becomes nearly deterministic, functionally close to greedy decoding. At temperature 1.5, dividing by 1.5 shrinks the gaps (2.0 becomes ~1.33, 1.0 becomes ~0.67), so after softmax the probabilities are much closer together — the lowest-scoring token that was nearly unreachable at low temperature now has a meaningfully higher chance of being sampled. This is why "low temperature = deterministic/focused" and "high temperature = random/creative" is the correct mental shorthand, and why temperature 0 is typically implemented as a special case that just does greedy argmax (dividing by 0 is undefined).

MIDQ34. What's the difference between "sampling" decoding and "search" decoding (beam search), and what failure mode does each avoid or introduce?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Sampling decoding (greedy, top-k, top-p, temperature) draws from the model's probability distribution at each step independently, optionally reshaped — it avoids the "bland, repetitive" failure mode that pure likelihood-maximization tends toward, at the cost of introducing genuine randomness (the same prompt can produce different outputs across runs). Search decoding (beam search) explicitly tries to find a high-total-likelihood full sequence by exploring multiple candidate continuations in parallel — it avoids randomness and produces reproducible output, but tends toward generic phrasing and can loop or degenerate on open-ended tasks precisely because "most likely overall" and "most interesting/useful" are not the same target.

SENIORQ35. Why does greedy decoding combined with a repetitive prompt often produce degenerate repeated loops of text, and how does that connect to the autoregressive feedback loop?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Once a phrase repeats once in the context, the model's learned statistics make "repeat the pattern again" a very high-probability continuation — language genuinely does contain repeated structures, so the model isn't malfunctioning, it's following its training distribution. Under greedy decoding there is no mechanism to break out of a locally-maximal choice: each repetition raises the conditional probability of repeating again, since the model now has direct evidence in its own context that "this is a text that repeats this phrase," and that evidence is fed straight back in via autoregression at the very next step. Because the decoder cannot un-emit a token, once a short loop starts, greedy decoding tends to reinforce and continue it rather than escape it — this is precisely why sampling randomness, repetition penalties, and no-repeat n-gram constraints exist as production mitigations.

SENIORQ36. How would you combine temperature, top-p, and top-k together in one decoding configuration, and what does each parameter contribute when combined?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

A common production pipeline applies them in sequence: first temperature rescales the raw logits to sharpen or flatten the overall distribution shape; then top-k (if set) truncates to a fixed-size candidate shortlist as a hard safety ceiling against extremely long-tail garbage tokens; then top-p further truncates that shortlist to the smallest subset reaching the cumulative probability threshold, adapting dynamically to how confident the model is at this particular step; finally, the surviving candidates are renormalized and sampled from. Each parameter contributes a different kind of control — temperature shapes "how spread out," top-k sets a hard cap on candidate count, and top-p adapts the candidate count to the model's actual per-step confidence — and using all three together is standard in most commercial chat completion APIs, though many teams find top-p alone (with a moderate temperature) sufficient for most use cases.

SENIORQ37. In what scenario would you deliberately choose beam search over sampling in a production decoder-only deployment?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium at senior level

Beam search is the right choice when there is a genuinely well-defined "best" output and reproducibility/determinism matters more than diversity — for example, generating a short, constrained caption or label from a fixed input, or a translation-like transformation where the goal is closely matching a single correct target rather than exploring creative alternatives. It is a poor choice for anything resembling open-ended chat, brainstorming, or long-form generation, where its tendency toward generic, repetitive high-likelihood sequences actively hurts perceived quality — which is why virtually every consumer chat product defaults to sampling-based decoding instead.

MIDQ38. What is repetition penalty, and mechanically how does it modify logits?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Repetition penalty reduces the logit score of any token that has already appeared in the generated sequence so far, making it less likely to be picked again. A common implementation divides (for positive logits) or multiplies (for negative logits) the logit of an already-seen token by a penalty factor greater than 1 before softmax: logit = logit / penalty if logit > 0 else logit * penalty. This is a blunt, uniform mechanism — it penalizes a token equally whether it appeared once or ten times, and regardless of whether repeating it here would actually be natural (e.g., function words like "the" legitimately repeat often).

MIDQ39. Explain the difference between frequency penalty and presence penalty as used in OpenAI-style APIs.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Presence penalty applies a fixed logit reduction to any token that has appeared at least once already, regardless of how many times — it discourages introducing repeated topics/tokens at all, but doesn't further punish heavy repetition. Frequency penalty scales the logit reduction proportionally to how many times a token has already appeared, so a token used five times gets penalized far more heavily than one used once — it specifically discourages a token from being overused, even if using it once or twice would be fine. In practice they're often combined: presence penalty nudges the model toward new vocabulary, frequency penalty prevents any single token from dominating.

MIDQ40. What is logit bias, and give a concrete production use case for manually biasing specific token logits?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Logit bias lets a caller add a fixed offset to specific token IDs' logits before softmax, directly increasing or decreasing the chance those exact tokens are sampled — a large negative bias can make a token essentially impossible to generate, while a large positive bias can force it to dominate. A concrete use case: banning profanity or a competitor's brand name from a customer-facing generation by applying a strongly negative bias to those tokens' IDs, or nudging a classification-style completion to strongly prefer specific label tokens (e.g., biasing the tokens for "POSITIVE" and "NEGATIVE" upward when the completion is meant to be a single-word sentiment label).

MIDQ41. What is constrained/grammar-guided decoding, and how does it mechanically restrict which tokens can be sampled at each step?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Constrained decoding enforces that the generated output conforms to a formal grammar (e.g., valid JSON matching a schema, or a regular expression) by computing, at every decode step, the set of tokens that would keep the sequence-so-far a valid prefix of some string in the grammar, and masking out every other token's logit to negative infinity before sampling — exactly the same additive-mask mechanism used for causal masking, just driven by grammar state instead of position. Concretely, an automaton tracking the grammar's current state is advanced after each token is emitted, and at the next step only the transitions valid from that state are left unmasked; the model is still doing ordinary next-token sampling, it is simply never offered an invalid token as an option.

SENIORQ42. How does JSON-mode / structured-output decoding work internally — is it fine-tuning, a decoding-time constraint, or both?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

In production systems it's typically both, layered together. Fine-tuning (or instruction-following training more broadly) biases the model to want to produce well-formed JSON when asked, which improves quality and reduces how aggressively the decoding-time constraint needs to intervene. The decoding-time constraint — grammar-guided decoding against a JSON grammar, or a schema-derived automaton for JSON-mode-with-schema — is what provides the actual guarantee: at every step, only tokens that keep the output a syntactically valid (and, for schema mode, structurally conforming) partial JSON document are left unmasked. Relying on fine-tuning alone gives high-probability-but-not-guaranteed valid JSON; the decoding-time mask is what converts "usually valid" into "always valid" for the JSON layer specifically (semantic correctness of the field values is a separate concern the grammar cannot enforce).

SENIORQ43. Walk through what happens mechanically at each decode step when a grammar constraint (e.g., a JSON schema) is applied — how is the vocabulary mask computed?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

The schema is first compiled into an automaton (or a pushdown automaton for nested structures like JSON objects/arrays) whose states correspond to "positions" within a valid document — e.g., "expecting an opening brace," "inside a string value," "expecting a comma or closing brace." At each decode step, given the automaton's current state, the constraint engine determines which vocabulary tokens, if appended, would still form a valid partial string according to the grammar (this can require checking token-level string content against the automaton's transition table, since a single token may represent multiple grammar-level characters). Every token not in that valid set has its logit set to negative infinity before softmax, exactly like the causal mask; the model samples normally from what remains, then the automaton's state is advanced based on the token actually emitted, ready for the next step's mask computation.

SENIORQ44. A teammate wants to guarantee 100% valid JSON output from a decoder-only model. Compare prompt-engineering-only versus constrained decoding as solutions.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Prompt engineering alone (few-shot examples, explicit "respond only with valid JSON" instructions) meaningfully raises the probability of valid JSON but cannot guarantee it — the model is still free-sampling over the full vocabulary at every step, and any low-probability deviation (a stray trailing comma, an unescaped quote, truncation at the max-token limit mid-object) breaks validity, with failure rate typically in the low single-digit percentages even with good prompting, which is unacceptable for a hard-guarantee production dependency. Constrained decoding, by contrast, makes invalid tokens architecturally unsamplable at every step, so the output is syntactically valid JSON by construction, not by probability — the correct answer for a "100% guarantee" requirement is constrained decoding (with good prompting still layered on top to improve semantic quality of the content within the guaranteed-valid structure).

Interview Gold Point: Explicitly distinguishing "the model wants to produce X" (a training/prompting concern) from "the model is physically unable to produce not-X" (a decoding-time constraint concern) is the single clearest signal that a candidate understands decoding mechanics rather than just prompting folklore.
STAFFQ45. Design a decoding pipeline for a production feature that must emit syntactically valid SQL 100% of the time from a decoder-only model, including how you'd combine grammar constraints with sampling parameters.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Compile the target SQL dialect's grammar (or a safe subset of it, e.g., read-only SELECT statements if that's the actual requirement) into a token-level automaton and apply it as a hard logit mask at every decode step, guaranteeing syntactic validity by construction the same way JSON-mode does. Layer sampling parameters on top of that guaranteed-valid candidate set: low temperature (or near-greedy) for the parts of the query that should be deterministic given the schema (table/column names, matching a provided schema), with slightly higher temperature reserved only for genuinely open-ended parts like filter value choices if the task calls for creativity there — most SQL-generation features should actually run close to temperature 0 since there's usually one intended query. Add a schema-aware validation layer beyond pure grammar (e.g., only allow table/column tokens that exist in the actual database schema, encoded as an additional constraint on top of general SQL grammar) so the guarantee extends from "syntactically valid SQL" to "syntactically valid SQL against this schema." Finally, treat grammar-invalid completions from the underlying model as impossible by construction, but still validate and sandbox-execute (e.g., EXPLAIN-only or a read replica) before running against production data, since syntactic validity says nothing about the query being safe or semantically correct.

JUNIORQ46. What is an EOS token, and how does a decoder-only model use it to know when to stop generating?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

EOS ("end of sequence") is a special reserved token in the vocabulary that the model is trained to produce when it judges the response to be complete. At each decode step, after a token is sampled, the generation loop checks whether that token is the EOS token; if so, generation stops immediately rather than continuing. It is not special at the architecture level — it's just another vocabulary entry — but the model learns during training to assign it high probability at genuinely natural stopping points.

JUNIORQ47. What is a BOS token, and is it always required?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

BOS ("beginning of sequence") is a special token prepended to a sequence to mark its start, giving position 1 of the sequence a well-defined, consistent "anchor" token to attend to. Whether it's required depends on how a given model was pretrained — some model families prepend it to every input by convention and expect it at inference time too, while others don't use one at all or rely purely on chat-template special tokens (covered later in this section) to mark boundaries instead. Using the wrong convention for a given model (omitting an expected BOS, or adding one where none was used in training) can measurably degrade output quality even though nothing "errors out."

JUNIORQ48. Why doesn't padding matter during single-sequence generation but does matter during batched generation?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

When generating one sequence at a time, there is nothing to pad to — the sequence is simply as long as it is, and every position is real content. Padding only becomes necessary when multiple sequences of different lengths must be processed together as one batch (a fixed-size tensor), since GPUs require uniform tensor shapes; shorter sequences in the batch are padded with filler tokens so every sequence in the batch has matching length, and a padding mask ensures those filler positions never receive or contribute real attention weight.

MIDQ49. How does a decoder-only model handle padding tokens in a batch without letting them corrupt attention for real tokens?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Padding positions are excluded from attention via the same additive-mask mechanism used for causal masking: a padding mask marks each pad position, and negative infinity is added to any attention score where the key position is a pad token, guaranteeing zero attention weight regardless of what the raw Query-Key dot product produced. The pad tokens still occupy space in the tensor (so the batch shape is uniform), but they are made functionally invisible to every real token's attention computation, and the LM head's output at pad positions is simply ignored/discarded rather than used for anything.

MIDQ50. What are "stop sequences" in a chat API, and how do they relate to the model's own EOS token?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Stop sequences are application-defined strings (e.g., "\n\nHuman:" in a raw completion-style prompt, or a custom delimiter) that, when they appear in the generated output, cause the client-side or server-side generation loop to halt — even though the model itself never produced its trained EOS token. They're implemented as an additional check layered on top of the same decode loop: after each new token is appended, the accumulated text is checked against the configured stop sequences (in addition to checking for the model's own EOS token), and generation halts on either condition. This is commonly used to prevent a model from "continuing the conversation on both sides" when a raw prompt format doesn't clearly delimit turns.

SENIORQ51. Explain why left-padding (not right-padding) is required for batched generation in decoder-only models, and what breaks if you right-pad instead.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

In a decoder-only model, generation always continues from the last position in the sequence — the next token is predicted from whatever sits at the final index. If sequences are right-padded (real content first, pad tokens appended after), the "last position" for shorter sequences in the batch would actually be a pad token, not the real last content token, so the model would be asked to predict a continuation from garbage/pad context. Left-padding (pad tokens first, real content aligned to the end) ensures every sequence's real final token sits at the same rightmost index across the batch, so generation correctly continues from real content for every sequence simultaneously, with positional encodings and the padding mask handling the fact that "position in the tensor" and "position in the real sequence" no longer match for the padded sequences.

MIDQ52. Why is prefill described as compute-bound?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

During prefill, hundreds or thousands of prompt tokens are processed through the model in a single pass, which means the matrix multiplications at every layer involve large batches of positions at once — this gives the GPU plenty of independent arithmetic work to parallelize across, keeping its compute (FLOP) units highly utilized relative to the time spent moving data. The bottleneck is genuinely "how fast can the GPU do the math," because there's enough work queued up to saturate its arithmetic throughput — hence compute-bound.

MIDQ53. Why is decode described as memory-bandwidth-bound?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

During decode, only one new token's worth of computation happens per step — a tiny amount of actual arithmetic — but every layer still must read the model's full weight matrices (and the growing KV cache) from GPU memory to perform even that small computation. The GPU spends most of its time waiting on data transfer from memory to its compute units rather than actually computing, because the amount of arithmetic per byte moved is very low. The bottleneck is genuinely "how fast can data move from memory," not "how fast can the GPU do math" — hence memory-bandwidth-bound.

SENIORQ54. What specific hardware resource is decode-phase throughput usually limited by, and why doesn't adding more compute (FLOPs) fix it?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Decode-phase throughput for a single sequence is typically limited by GPU memory bandwidth — the rate at which weights and KV cache entries can be streamed from high-bandwidth memory (HBM) into the GPU's compute cores. Adding more raw compute capacity (more or faster FLOP units) doesn't help because those units are already sitting idle waiting for data; the constraint isn't "not enough math capacity," it's "not enough data delivery rate to keep the math units fed." This is exactly why the fix for decode-phase throughput is increasing arithmetic intensity — most commonly via larger batch sizes, so the same memory read (of the weights) is reused across many concurrent sequences' worth of computation instead of being read once per sequence.

SENIORQ55. How does batch size affect prefill versus decode differently, given their different bottlenecks?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Prefill is already compute-bound with a single long sequence, so increasing batch size (processing multiple prompts' prefill simultaneously) helps up to the point where the GPU's compute units are saturated, after which further batching mostly just queues more work without proportionally increasing throughput. Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound with a single sequence — a huge amount of unused compute capacity sits idle — so increasing decode batch size (serving many concurrent users' decode steps together) is almost pure upside up to a much higher ceiling: the same weight read from memory is reused across every sequence in the batch, dramatically raising arithmetic intensity and GPU utilization without a proportional latency cost per request, which is exactly the mechanism continuous batching exploits.

SENIORQ56. What is "arithmetic intensity," and why is the decode phase's arithmetic intensity low compared to prefill's?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Arithmetic intensity is the ratio of compute operations performed to bytes of data moved from memory — a high ratio means a GPU is doing lots of math per byte fetched (compute-bound territory), a low ratio means it's mostly waiting on data movement relative to the math it gets to do (memory-bound territory). Prefill has high arithmetic intensity because processing many prompt tokens at once means each weight matrix, once loaded from memory, is reused across all those tokens' computations in the same pass. A single sequence's decode step has low arithmetic intensity because the same weight matrices are loaded from memory but used for just one token's worth of math before the step ends — most of the "work" is data movement, not arithmetic, until batching multiple sequences' decode steps together raises the reuse (and thus the intensity) back up.

SENIORQ57. Explain how larger batch sizes during decode can improve GPU utilization without proportionally increasing latency per request.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Because decode is memory-bandwidth-bound for a single sequence, the GPU's compute units are mostly idle during any one sequence's decode step — there's substantial spare compute capacity. Batching multiple sequences' decode steps together means the same weight-matrix reads from memory are reused across every sequence in the batch in the same pass (the memory transfer, which was the bottleneck, doesn't scale linearly with batch size the way the useful work does), so throughput (total tokens generated per second across all sequences) rises substantially. Latency per individual token for any one sequence in the batch rises only modestly, because the extra work added by batching is mostly absorbed by previously-idle compute capacity rather than adding proportional new bottleneck time — up until the batch is large enough to become compute-bound itself, at which point further batching starts trading latency for throughput more directly.

STAFFQ58. You're designing a low-latency streaming chat service and profiling shows GPU compute utilization is only 15% during decode despite full queue depth. Diagnose and propose a fix.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Low compute utilization with a full request queue during decode is the textbook signature of being memory-bandwidth-bound with an under-batched serving configuration — the GPU is spending most of its cycles waiting on weight/KV-cache reads from memory rather than doing math, even though there's plenty of demand queued up. The fix is to increase effective decode batch size via continuous batching: instead of serving decode steps for only a small number of concurrent sequences (or worse, serializing requests), the inference engine should interleave decode steps from many concurrent sequences into the same forward pass, so each expensive weight-matrix memory read is amortized across far more useful computation. Concretely: verify the serving framework (e.g., vLLM-style continuous batching, or the provider's equivalent) is actually batching decode steps across requests rather than processing them one-at-a-time or in small static batches; check whether KV cache memory is the limiting factor preventing larger batches (if so, consider cache-efficient techniques like paged attention or quantized KV cache to fit more concurrent sequences in the same memory budget); and confirm request scheduling isn't artificially serializing traffic upstream of the batching layer (e.g., a synchronous per-request client pattern instead of a shared inference server pool).

MIDQ59. What is continuous batching, and how does it differ from static/request-level batching?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High

Static batching groups a fixed set of requests together, runs their prefill and decode together, and waits for the entire batch to finish generating before accepting new requests into a fresh batch — meaning a batch's throughput is limited by its longest-running request, and GPU capacity freed up by requests that finish early sits idle until the whole batch completes. Continuous batching instead treats each request's decode loop independently: at every single decode step, the scheduler checks which sequences in flight still need a next token, batches exactly those together for that one step, and immediately admits newly arrived requests into the next step's batch — so a GPU slot freed by one finished sequence is picked up by a waiting sequence within a single decode iteration rather than sitting idle until an entire batch completes.

MIDQ60. Why does continuous batching specifically target the decode phase rather than prefill?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Prefill is a single, comparatively fast, parallel pass per request that is already compute-efficient on its own — there's less idle capacity to reclaim by interleaving it with other requests' prefill work (though it can still be batched to some degree). Decode is the long-running, sequential, memory-bandwidth-bound part of a request's lifecycle where individual sequences leave substantial GPU compute capacity unused at any given moment — that's precisely the idle capacity continuous batching reclaims by packing many concurrent sequences' single-token decode steps into the same forward pass, which is why the technique's biggest throughput wins show up specifically in decode-heavy, high-concurrency serving scenarios.

SENIORQ61. Walk through what happens when a new request arrives mid-stream while 20 other requests are already in their decode loop, under continuous batching.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

The new request first goes through its own prefill pass (typically scheduled opportunistically, sometimes interleaved with or prioritized around the ongoing decode batch depending on the scheduler's policy), populating its KV cache and producing its first generated token. From that point on, at every subsequent decode iteration, the scheduler simply adds this 21st sequence's single-token decode step into the same batched forward pass as the other 20 — each sequence contributes one new token's worth of Query/Key/Value computation, all processed together in one GPU kernel launch, with each sequence attending only over its own cached history via appropriate masking. The new request doesn't wait for any of the 20 in-flight sequences to finish; it simply joins the shared decode loop at the next available iteration.

SENIORQ62. What complications does continuous batching introduce for KV cache memory management across many concurrent requests?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Because requests enter and leave the batch at different times with different, growing sequence lengths, naively allocating a fixed-size contiguous memory block per sequence's KV cache either wastes memory (over-allocating for worst-case length) or requires expensive reallocation/copying as sequences grow. Production inference engines solve this with techniques like paged attention, which manages KV cache memory in fixed-size, non-contiguous "pages" (analogous to OS virtual memory paging) that can be allocated and freed per sequence on demand, avoiding both fragmentation and the need to pre-reserve worst-case memory for every concurrent request. Without such a scheme, the maximum number of concurrent sequences a GPU can serve under continuous batching would be far lower, since memory (not compute) becomes the binding constraint on batch size.

STAFFQ63. Propose a batching strategy for a mixed workload of short chat completions and long document-generation requests sharing the same GPU pool.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium at staff level

The core risk in a mixed workload is head-of-line blocking under naive static batching — a handful of long document-generation requests can dominate a batch's decode iterations, starving short chat requests of GPU time and destroying their latency (bad for an interactive product surface). Continuous batching largely solves the scheduling half of this by giving every in-flight sequence a fair shot at each decode iteration regardless of how long it's been running, but memory pressure from many long-running KV caches can still crowd out capacity for new short requests, so I'd add priority-aware admission and eviction: reserve a portion of KV cache capacity/batch slots for interactive/short-latency-SLA traffic, and consider routing long document-generation workloads to a separate pool (or a lower-priority queue with relaxed SLAs) so a burst of long-form generation traffic can't degrade the interactive chat experience sharing the same hardware. I'd also monitor tokens-per-second and time-to-first-token separately per traffic class, since a single blended metric would mask one class's degradation behind the other's healthy numbers.

JUNIORQ64. Why does streaming generation feel natural for chat UIs, given how decoders actually produce output?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

A decoder genuinely produces its output one token at a time, sequentially, with each token only becoming available once the previous one has been generated and fed back through the model — there is no moment where the "full answer" exists all at once before that point. Streaming simply exposes this real, incremental generation process to the user as it happens rather than artificially buffering it until the end, which both matches the model's actual computational rhythm and improves perceived responsiveness by showing the first token almost immediately instead of making the user wait for total generation time.

JUNIORQ65. In Spring AI, what does calling .stream() on a ChatClient actually correspond to at the model-serving level?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

It opens a connection (typically server-sent events or a chunked HTTP response) to the inference server and subscribes to receive each newly generated token as an individual event, roughly as soon as that token is sampled at the end of one decode iteration on the server. The Flux<ChatResponse> returned in Java code is a reactive representation of exactly that server-side decode loop — each element emitted downstream corresponds to one token becoming available, not a client-side illusion of streaming over an already-complete response.

MIDQ66. How does server-sent events (SSE) map onto the decode loop token by token?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

SSE keeps a single long-lived HTTP response open and lets the server push discrete "events" (small chunks of data) down that connection as they become available, rather than requiring the client to poll. In a decoder's serving pipeline, each time a decode iteration produces a new sampled token, the inference server immediately writes that token as one SSE event over the open connection — there's a near-direct one-to-one correspondence between "a decode step completed" on the server and "an SSE event was emitted" to the client, which is what makes streaming feel like watching generation happen live rather than watching a pre-recorded playback.

MIDQ67. What happens to a Spring AI Flux<ChatResponse> subscriber if the underlying decode loop stalls mid-generation, and how would you handle that in code?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

If the server-side decode loop stalls (GPU contention, an upstream network hiccup, a provider-side issue), the subscriber simply stops receiving new elements on the Flux — reactive streams don't inherently distinguish "slow" from "about to fail" without an explicit timeout. In code, you'd typically guard against this with a .timeout(Duration) operator on the stream (raising a TimeoutException if no new token arrives within an expected interval) combined with proper error handling in the subscriber's error callback, and potentially a retry/fallback policy — because an indefinitely stalled stream will otherwise just leave the client hanging with no explicit signal that anything is wrong.

SENIORQ68. Design consideration: what's the difference in how you'd handle backpressure for a decoder's token stream versus a typical database result stream?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

A database result stream is usually producer-paced by the consumer's request rate in a fairly literal sense — the database can genuinely pause row production while the consumer catches up, since rows already exist in storage and simply aren't being read yet. A decoder's token stream is fundamentally different: tokens are being actively computed server-side by an expensive, ongoing GPU decode loop that is often shared across many concurrent requests via continuous batching — you generally cannot cheaply "pause" that computation mid-flight the way you can pause reading from a table, because doing so would mean holding GPU/KV-cache resources idle for a slow consumer while other requests wait. In practice, backpressure for LLM streaming is more often handled by buffering a modest number of tokens client-side (rather than propagating true backpressure to the generation loop itself) and, if a consumer genuinely can't keep up, cancelling the underlying generation request entirely rather than throttling it — because throttling a GPU-bound generation loop trades one request's slowness for wasted expensive compute capacity across the whole serving pool.

JUNIORQ69. What's the difference between "prompt tokens" and "completion tokens" in a chat API bill?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: High

Prompt tokens are the tokens in everything sent to the model — the system prompt, conversation history, retrieved context, and the current user message — processed during the prefill phase. Completion tokens are the tokens the model actually generates in response, produced one at a time during the decode phase. Most providers bill these two categories at different per-token rates (completion tokens are typically priced higher than prompt tokens), directly reflecting the fact that decode is more resource-intensive per token than prefill.

JUNIORQ70. Does a decoder-only model process prompt tokens and completion tokens through fundamentally different code paths?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

No — both prompt and completion tokens flow through the exact same decoder blocks, the same weights, and the same causal-masking rules; there is no architectural distinction between "prompt" and "completion" tokens once they're inside the model, they're just positions in one growing sequence. The distinction is purely about serving-pipeline phase and billing: prompt tokens are all processed together in one prefill pass before generation starts, and completion tokens are produced one at a time afterward during decode — but the model itself treats a completion token exactly the same way it treats a prompt token once that token becomes part of the context for whatever comes next.

MIDQ71. Why might a request with a huge prompt but a tiny completion cost less in latency than a tiny prompt with a huge completion, even with similar total token counts?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Prefill processes the entire prompt in one parallel, compute-bound pass, so even a very large prompt (say, 4,000 tokens) typically completes in a relatively small, roughly-flat amount of wall-clock time. Decode, by contrast, requires one full sequential pass through the entire model per generated token, and each of those passes has real latency that accumulates linearly with output length — so a request generating 4,000 completion tokens takes on the order of 4,000 sequential decode iterations' worth of time, which is dramatically slower than one parallel prefill pass over a similarly-sized prompt. This asymmetry is exactly why "time to first token" and "output length" are the two separate latency levers worth optimizing independently, rather than treating "total tokens" as a single undifferentiated cost.

JUNIORQ72. What is a chat template, and why is it needed for a decoder-only model that only understands "continue this sequence of tokens"?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

A chat template is a formatting rule that converts a structured conversation (a list of role-labeled messages — system, user, assistant) into a single flat sequence of text/tokens, using special delimiter tokens to mark where each turn begins and ends and who is speaking. It's needed because a decoder-only model has no native concept of "roles" or "turns" at the architecture level — it only ever sees one linear token sequence — so the template is what encodes conversational structure into that sequence in a consistent, learnable format that matches how the model was fine-tuned to interpret role boundaries.

JUNIORQ73. How does a multi-turn conversation with system/user/assistant roles get converted into a single token sequence for a decoder-only model?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: Extremely High

Each message in the conversation is wrapped with role-marking special tokens or textual delimiters and concatenated, in order, into one long string, which is then tokenized as a single sequence — for example, a system message, a user message, and an assistant reply might become <system>...</system><user>...</user><assistant>...</assistant> (exact delimiter tokens vary by model family), all fed through the decoder as one causal sequence exactly like any other prompt. The model was fine-tuned on data formatted this same way, so it has learned to treat text following an "assistant" opening marker as "my turn to generate" and to stop (emit EOS or a turn-closing token) at the appropriate point.

MIDQ74. Show a worked example of what a chat template does to a 3-turn conversation (system, user, assistant, user) before it reaches the tokenizer.

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Given the structured messages:

system:    "You are a concise assistant."
user:      "What's the capital of France?"
assistant: "Paris."
user:      "And of Japan?"

a typical chat template (using illustrative, model-family-generic markers) would flatten this into one string before tokenization:

<|system|>You are a concise assistant.<|end|>
<|user|>What's the capital of France?<|end|>
<|assistant|>Paris.<|end|>
<|user|>And of Japan?<|end|>
<|assistant|>

Note the trailing, unclosed <|assistant|> marker at the end with no content after it — this is what signals to the decoder that generation should continue from here as the assistant's turn, and the model's own next-token prediction picks up exactly where this flattened sequence leaves off.

MIDQ75. What role do special tokens like turn/role markers play in chat-formatted decoder input, and are they part of the normal vocabulary?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

These markers are typically reserved vocabulary entries added specifically for chat formatting — they occupy real token IDs in the model's vocabulary (and consume real embedding rows), but they never appear in ordinary prose, which is precisely what makes them reliable, unambiguous structural signals rather than something a model could confuse with genuine user content. During fine-tuning, the model learns strong statistical associations between these markers and behavior — e.g., "after an assistant-turn-open marker, generate a helpful reply and end with EOS or a turn-close marker" — which is how role structure gets baked into next-token prediction despite the underlying model having no architectural notion of "roles" at all.

SENIORQ76. What breaks if your application constructs the chat-formatted prompt slightly differently than the format the model was fine-tuned on?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at senior level

Nothing "errors" in any visible way — the model will still tokenize and process a malformed or mismatched template just fine, since it has no built-in validation of chat structure. What degrades is generation quality and reliability: the model's fine-tuning taught it strong statistical expectations about exactly how role boundaries and special tokens appear, and a subtly wrong format (missing a closing marker, using the wrong delimiter for that model family, inconsistent whitespace around markers) puts the input outside the distribution the model was actually tuned on. Symptoms include the model ignoring system instructions, failing to respect turn boundaries (continuing to "speak as the user"), degraded instruction-following, or unexpected EOS behavior — which is exactly why using the model provider's official chat-template utility (rather than hand-rolling string concatenation) is a strong production best practice.

SENIORQ77. How does instruction-tuning change what a decoder-only base model "wants" to generate after a chat template is applied, compared to a raw pretrained base model given the same templated input?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

A raw pretrained base model has only ever learned "predict plausible continuations of internet-scale text," so if you feed it a chat-templated prompt, it will tend to treat the special markers as unusual text and may continue in unhelpful ways — hallucinating additional fake user turns, ignoring the instruction implicit in the system message, or simply continuing whatever textual pattern seems statistically likely regardless of whether it's "helpful." Instruction-tuning (further training on curated conversational data that follows the chat template format, typically with a helpful/harmless/honest objective) reshapes the model's learned next-token distribution specifically at those role-boundary markers, so that following an assistant-turn-open marker, the highest-probability continuation becomes "a genuinely helpful, on-topic response that respects the system instruction and ends appropriately" rather than an arbitrary statistically-plausible continuation of internet text. The architecture is identical in both cases — the difference is entirely in what the training data taught the same decoder-only mechanism to do at those specific structural positions.

SENIORQ78. What do decoder-only scaling laws practically tell an engineering team choosing between a smaller fine-tuned model and a larger general model for a fixed task?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Scaling laws for decoder-only models show that quality improves predictably (though with diminishing returns) as a function of parameter count, training data volume, and compute, roughly following power-law curves — which practically means a much larger general-purpose model reliably outperforms a much smaller one on broad, open-ended capability, but the marginal quality gain per additional parameter shrinks. For a narrow, well-defined production task, a smaller model fine-tuned specifically on that task's distribution can often match or exceed a much larger general model's task-specific performance, because fine-tuning effectively "spends" the smaller model's limited capacity entirely on the task at hand rather than spreading it across broad general capability the task doesn't need. The practical decision hinges on task narrowness and latency/cost sensitivity: narrow, latency-sensitive, high-volume tasks favor a smaller fine-tuned model; broad, open-ended, lower-volume tasks favor a larger general model where fine-tuning a narrow model isn't even well-defined.

SENIORQ79. Why don't latency scaling laws follow the same curve as quality scaling laws, given that decode is inherently sequential?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Quality scaling laws describe how output quality improves with more parameters/data/compute spent on training — a property of the trained model itself. Latency at inference time, by contrast, is dominated by the decode loop's sequential structure: the number of decode iterations required equals the number of output tokens, completely independent of how large or capable the model is, and each iteration's wall-clock cost scales primarily with model size (bigger models mean more weight data to move per decode step) rather than with anything related to the quality curve. This means a model can get meaningfully "smarter" per parameter added (following the quality scaling curve) while simultaneously getting linearly slower per token generated (following a completely separate, size-driven latency relationship) — the two curves are related only through model size as a shared input, not through any deeper connection between quality and speed.

STAFFQ80. Given a fixed inference latency budget, how would you reason about the tradeoff between a bigger model with fewer generated tokens versus a smaller model generating more tokens (e.g., via chain-of-thought)?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

Total decode latency is roughly proportional to (per-token decode cost, driven by model size) × (number of output tokens), so the two levers genuinely trade against each other under a fixed budget: a larger model that reaches a correct, concise answer in fewer tokens (better prompting, less need for explicit intermediate reasoning) may cost about the same wall-clock time per response as a smaller, faster-per-token model that needs several times more tokens (via chain-of-thought reasoning) to reach comparable accuracy. The right choice depends on which lever is cheaper to pull for the task at hand: if the task genuinely benefits from visible step-by-step reasoning to reach correctness, a smaller model with more reasoning tokens can be both cheaper and comparably accurate; if the task is more about world-knowledge/nuance than stepwise computation, a bigger model answering directly and concisely often wins on both latency and quality simultaneously. I'd validate this empirically per task rather than assuming either direction — total latency and accuracy should both be measured, not inferred from parameter count or token count alone.

STAFFQ81. How do scaling and hardware-bottleneck considerations motivate speculative decoding with a draft/target model pair, and what decoder-only property makes this technique work at all?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at staff level

Because decode is memory-bandwidth-bound and under-utilizes available compute for a single sequence, there is substantial idle arithmetic capacity sitting unused at every decode step — exactly the same idle capacity continuous batching reclaims across multiple requests, but speculative decoding reclaims it within a single request. A small, fast "draft" model generates several candidate next tokens autoregressively (cheaply, since it's small), and then the large "target" model verifies all of those candidate tokens in a single parallel forward pass — because verifying a proposed token (checking whether the target model would have assigned it non-trivial probability) is architecturally identical to prefill-style parallel processing of a known sequence, not sequential generation. If the target model accepts several draft tokens as plausible, multiple tokens' worth of output are produced for roughly the cost of one target-model decode step; if a draft token is rejected, the target model's own prediction is used instead and drafting resumes from there. This works specifically because verification (checking many known tokens at once) is compute-bound and parallel, exactly like prefill, while generation (producing new tokens one at a time) is memory-bound and sequential — speculative decoding essentially converts part of the decode workload into prefill-shaped work to exploit the GPU's otherwise-idle compute capacity.

STAFFQ82. You're asked to design a low-latency streaming chat service. Walk through the key architectural decisions driven specifically by decode-phase bottlenecks.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

First, serve behind an inference engine that implements continuous batching with paged (or equivalent) KV cache management, since decode's memory-bandwidth-bound nature means throughput scales with how many concurrent sequences share each expensive weight read — a naive single-request-at-a-time server would leave most GPU capacity idle. Second, stream tokens to the client via SSE/websocket as soon as each decode iteration produces one, so perceived latency is dominated by time-to-first-token (a prefill-driven, largely prompt-length-dependent cost) rather than total generation time. Third, treat prompt length and output length as separate cost/latency levers in the product design — aggressively trim conversation history and retrieved context (cheap, since prefill is fast and parallel) while being more conservative about max output token limits (expensive, since decode is slow and sequential), and consider capping or steering verbosity for latency-sensitive surfaces. Fourth, consider speculative decoding if a suitable draft model exists for the domain, since it converts idle decode-phase compute into extra throughput. Fifth, instrument and alert on time-to-first-token and tokens-per-second as separate SLAs rather than one blended "response time" metric, since they're driven by different phases with different bottlenecks and different fixes.

STAFFQ83. A production decoder-only model has started producing visibly repetitive, degenerate text ("the the the...") for a subset of long-running conversations. Diagnose likely causes and fixes.

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

The most likely root cause is the autoregressive feedback loop discussed earlier: once a short repeated pattern appears in the context (often triggered by very low temperature/near-greedy decoding, an unusually long or self-similar context, or a degenerate earlier generation that was never corrected), the model's own repeated tokens become strong evidence in its own context that repetition is the expected continuation, and greedy or low-temperature decoding has no mechanism to escape that local trap. First-line fixes are decoding-parameter changes that don't require retraining: raise temperature modestly, ensure top-p/top-k are set to allow some exploration away from the single highest-probability token, and enable (or increase) a repetition penalty or frequency penalty so previously-used tokens become progressively less attractive. If those conversations specifically correlate with very long context (approaching the model's context window), check whether the degeneracy correlates with context length — some models degrade in coherence as the KV cache fills toward the training-time context limit, which would point toward context trimming/summarization as the actual fix rather than decoding parameters alone. Finally, add a production safeguard: detect repeated n-gram loops server-side (a cheap pattern check on the last k tokens) and truncate/regenerate with adjusted parameters rather than streaming visibly broken output to the user.

STAFFQ84. How would you choose decoding parameters differently for a deterministic production feature (e.g., a legal disclaimer generator) versus a creative one (e.g., a marketing copy generator), and why?

ROI: ★★★★★ · Frequency: High at staff level

For the deterministic feature, I'd use low temperature (near 0, functionally close to greedy) with tight or no top-p/top-k exploration, and possibly deterministic decoding entirely if the provider supports it — the goal is reproducibility and minimizing variance from the model's single highest-confidence continuation, since a legal disclaimer has essentially one "correct" phrasing per situation and any creative deviation is pure downside risk. I would also strongly consider constrained/grammar-guided decoding if the disclaimer needs to conform to an approved template structure, converting "usually correct" into "guaranteed structurally correct." For the creative feature, I'd use moderate-to-higher temperature combined with top-p sampling (rather than top-k, since top-p adapts better to how confident the model is at each step) to intentionally preserve variety across generations and avoid the bland, repetitive tendency of near-greedy decoding — and I'd deliberately avoid heavy grammar constraints, since creative copy benefits from the model's natural stylistic range rather than a rigid structure. The underlying principle in both cases is the same: decoding parameters don't change what the model "knows," they change how much of its learned probability distribution's tail is allowed to influence the sampled output, and that knob should be set based on whether variance is a production risk or a product feature.

MIDQ85. What is speculative decoding, and how does it exploit the fact that decode is memory-bandwidth-bound rather than compute-bound?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Speculative decoding uses a small, cheap "draft" model to quickly generate several candidate next tokens, then has the large "target" model check all of those candidates in one single parallel forward pass — which is computationally similar to prefill (verifying known tokens) rather than standard sequential decode (generating unknown tokens one at a time). Because a single sequence's decode step already under-utilizes the GPU's compute capacity (the bottleneck is memory bandwidth, not arithmetic), verifying several draft tokens at once costs barely more than verifying one, since the same memory-bound weight reads happen either way — so when the target model accepts multiple draft tokens as plausible, several tokens of real output are produced for close to the latency cost of a single normal decode step.

SENIORQ86. How does quantizing a decoder-only model (e.g., to INT8 or INT4) affect decode-phase throughput versus prefill-phase throughput, and why the asymmetry?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Quantization shrinks the size of the model's weights in memory (e.g., roughly 4× smaller going from FP16 to INT4), which directly reduces the volume of data that must move from memory to compute units at every step. Since decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, this is a near-direct win — less data to move per decode step means each step completes faster and/or more concurrent sequences fit in the same bandwidth budget, often producing a substantial decode throughput improvement. Prefill, being compute-bound rather than memory-bound, benefits far less from quantization's memory-size reduction alone (though some quantization schemes also speed up the arithmetic itself, which does help prefill) — the asymmetry exists because quantization primarily attacks the specific resource (memory bandwidth) that limits decode, while prefill's bottleneck (raw compute throughput) is a comparatively separate concern.

MIDQ87. What is min-p sampling, and how does it address a weakness of top-p sampling with very flat or very peaked distributions?

ROI: ★★★☆☆ · Frequency: Medium

Min-p sampling sets a dynamic probability floor relative to the single most likely token: only tokens whose probability is at least (min_p × probability of the top token) are kept as candidates, then the survivors are renormalized and sampled from. This adapts naturally to the distribution's shape in a way plain top-p can behave oddly for at the extremes — when the model is extremely confident (one token near probability 1.0), top-p with a high threshold can still admit a long tail of low-probability tokens if their cumulative sum happens to cross the threshold slowly, whereas min-p's relative floor cleanly excludes anything far below the top candidate regardless of cumulative sum; conversely, when the distribution is very flat (genuine uncertainty), min-p naturally keeps a wider set of comparably-likely candidates rather than being overly restrictive.

JUNIORQ88. What does a token's "logprob" tell you, and how is it derived from the decoder's output at each generation step?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

A logprob is the natural logarithm of the probability the model assigned to a specific token at the moment it was generated (or would assign to a hypothetical token, when requested for candidates other than the one actually sampled). It comes directly from the same softmax distribution over the vocabulary that the LM head produces at every decode step — taking the log of the probability the softmax assigned to the token in question. A logprob close to 0 means the model was very confident in that token (probability near 1); a very negative logprob means the model considered that token quite unlikely at that point.

MIDQ89. How can per-token logprobs returned by a decoding API be used to detect low-confidence generations in production?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High

Because each logprob directly reflects the model's confidence in the specific token it generated at that position, a production system can inspect the sequence of logprobs across a completion and flag responses where confidence is unusually low — either a single very negative logprob at a critical position (suggesting an uncertain or possibly hallucinated fact) or a low average logprob across the whole response (suggesting the model was generally uncertain, perhaps operating outside its comfortable knowledge distribution). This is commonly used as a lightweight, cost-free-at-inference-time proxy signal for triggering a fallback behavior — e.g., asking a clarifying question, routing to a larger model, or flagging the response for human review — without requiring a separate confidence-scoring model.

SENIORQ90. Why do code-generation features typically use low temperature / near-greedy decoding while creative-writing features use higher temperature, tying this back to what decoding parameters actually do to the next-token distribution?

ROI: ★★★★☆ · Frequency: Medium-High at senior level

Temperature and top-p/top-k don't change what the model "knows" — they change how much of the tail of its learned next-token probability distribution is allowed to influence the sampled output. Code has a comparatively narrow space of syntactically and semantically correct continuations at each point (a variable name must exist, a bracket must close correctly, a function signature must match its usage), so the model's highest-probability tokens at each step are usually also the objectively correct ones — low temperature reliably selects them and suppresses the small chance of sampling a lower-probability, likely-wrong token that would introduce a bug. Creative writing has a genuinely wide space of equally "correct" continuations at each point (many different next words could all produce good prose), so higher temperature intentionally samples further into that legitimate tail, producing variety and stylistic surprise that near-greedy decoding would flatten into generic, repetitive phrasing. The same mechanism (reshaping one probability distribution before sampling) produces opposite recommended settings purely because the two tasks have structurally different "correct answer" distributions.

16. SUMMARY

  • A decoder is a stack of identical blocks (masked multi-head self-attention, residual connections, layer normalization, feed-forward layers) that generates a sequence one token at a time, each token conditioned on everything before it.
  • The original Transformer paired an encoder (full bidirectional attention) with a decoder (causal attention) for translation; nearly every modern general-purpose LLM you call through Spring AI is decoder-only, because uniform, causal token processing generalizes to open-ended chat, coding, and reasoning without task-specific architecture changes.
  • Causality is enforced by a mask that adds negative infinity to "future" attention scores before softmax — a hard arithmetic guarantee, not a learned behavior, identical at every layer and every training step.
  • Training uses teacher forcing (always-correct prefixes, fully parallel); inference is strictly autoregressive and sequential (the model's own possibly-imperfect tokens feed back in), and this mismatch — exposure bias — is a real source of compounding errors in long generations.
  • The KV cache is the production-critical optimization that avoids recomputing attention over the entire sequence at every decode step, and it directly explains why context length drives both memory usage and cost in real inference systems.
  • Spring AI's streaming ChatClient API, its chat memory/trimming advisors, and the very existence of "time to first token" versus "tokens per second" as separate latency metrics are all direct, practical reflections of the decoder's prefill/decode architecture — this is not abstract theory, it is the mechanism behind APIs you will use in every chapter from here forward.
